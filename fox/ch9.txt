Chapter Nine: The Uncle Keeps Accounts

Bi Gan came to the queen's wing at the hour when the cooks were scraping the morning's millet pots, which Yue later understood to be deliberate. A man who wanted to catch a household unprepared did not come at dawn, when everyone expected visitors. He came at the dull hour, when the first excitement had worn off and people had begun to relax.

"The Prince Bi Gan," announced a maid, and Yue, who had been practicing the correct angle for lifting a wine-cup, put it down so quickly that the barley water slopped.

He was a small, neat man of sixty with a white beard trimmed square, an ash-white robe of good wool, and the tired, courteous, watchful face of a person who had spent forty years being the sensible one in a family of lunatics. In one hand he carried a bundle of bamboo slips, and in the other a little bronze weight on a cord, of the sort used to test a scale.

"Your Majesty." He bowed, exactly as deeply as protocol required. "Forgive an old man his early visit. I am the king's uncle and keeper of his accounts. It falls to me to tally the dowry."

"The dowry," said Yue.

From behind the lattice screen, so softly that it might have been a draft, came a small clink of a bowl being set down on a tray, once. Yes, said the clink. Yue had spent three days in a carriage learning to read the language of the maid's hands. A bowl set down once meant yes. Twice meant no. The long, slow scrape of a fingernail on lacquer meant for the love of heaven, say less.

"The dowry," Bi Gan agreed, and unrolled his slips. "Eleven carts left Zhongzhou, I'm told. Eleven carts arrived. Seven of them contained millet, as listed. Four contained," he looked up, mildly, "straw."

"Straw?" said Yue.

"Straw. Under a layer of grain sacks, so that the envoy's count should come out correctly." His voice was neither unkind nor accusing. It was the voice of a man reporting a slight leak in the roof. "I do not suppose the Marquis intended an insult. I suppose he intended economy. But it was my duty to look. A hostage who arrives with a falsified dowry is a hostage whose father has already begun to lie."

Scrape, went the nail on the lacquer.

Yue looked at her hands. She thought of the Marquis of Zhongzhou, whom she had never met, and of the girl behind the screen who had walked out into the road beside the carts and refused to ride, and she understood, with an odd hot pressure under her ribs, that this was the true weight of a daughter sent away: that every sack in the cart would be counted against her.

"My father," she said slowly, "has had three bad harvests."

"So I understand."

"The straw was not meant to deceive the king. It was meant to deceive the envoy." She had never said anything that felt so much like her own. "He thought a poor dowry would insult the crown. He thought a heavy-looking one would not. He's a proud man, and a frightened one. I don't think he understood that the king reads his own tallies."

There was a silence.

Bi Gan's fingers, resting on the bundle, went very still. Behind the screen the tray made no sound whatever.

"That," he said at last, "was exceedingly honest."

"I thought you'd count it anyway."

"I did." He permitted himself something that was not quite a smile. "Honesty is an unusual thing to meet at a state inspection. Most people lie in the first sentence. It tells me a great deal about the person." He tied the slips and set the bronze weight on the table, where it swung gently from its cord. "I will tell the king that the straw was a father's pride and not a treason, and that the queen offered the truth without being asked. He will like it. He has a weakness for things said plainly."

"Thank you, Uncle."

"Do not thank me yet." He rose. "I have one more matter. A small one."

He took a bamboo slip from his sleeve, an old one, much handled, and held it out to her with both hands, like an offering.

"This came with your escort, from the Marquis, in the case of letters. It is a list of Zhongzhou's districts, with the name of the magistrate in each. I would be obliged if you would read me the third line."

Yue took it.

The characters on it were the old square script of the Su court, spiky and unfamiliar, and she had never in her life learned to read them. She had worn the girl's face. She had worn her gowns and her gestures, her breathing, the exact angle of her wary, intelligent attention. She had not worn her literacy. A face could be stolen in a night. A girl's schooling was a different matter.

Say less, whispered a fingernail on the lacquer behind the screen, very faintly, in one long slow scrape.

She looked at the slip. She looked at Bi Gan's mild, courteous, patient face. She saw, with a cold clarity, that this man had never asked a question in his life to which he did not already know the answer.

"I can't," said Yue.

"Can't?"

"I can't read it." She put the slip down. "My father didn't teach his daughters the court script. He said it was a waste. I learned the common hand, from my mother's maid." She looked up. "It's a family disgrace. I'd hoped it would pass unnoticed."

There was a very long pause.

"That," said Bi Gan, "is also true."

"Is it?"

"I knew the Marquis in his youth. He thought women who read the old script were forward." He took the slip back and tucked it away. "I did not know whether the daughter would admit to it. Most would have guessed. It is a great temptation, at a state inspection, to guess." He bowed, deeply, correctly. "Forgive an old man for testing a queen. I only wished to know whom the king had married."

He went out. His footsteps faded down the corridor, slow, regular, unhurried.

"Well?" said Yue to the screen. "Did I do that right?"

There was a silence. Then the screen moved, and Fuji stepped out, with her tray in her hands and her face the color of old milk.

"I can read the old script," she said. "I wrote half my father's correspondence in it. If you had guessed, I'd have signaled and you'd have been caught. If you had said 'I cannot' in any other voice, he would have known you were lying." She put the tray down. Her hands were shaking. "You didn't sign. You didn't look at me. You just told him."

"I couldn't think of anything else to do."

"That," said Su Fuji, with feeling, "is the best strategy I have ever seen."

She sat down on the floor, abruptly, in a heap of grey skirts, and laughed until she had to put her head on her knees.


* * *

The garden of the queen's wing was a rectangle of beaten earth with a persimmon tree in the middle, its fruit gone orange and soft. A low wall bounded it. Beyond, the roofs of Chaoge rose and fell in the thin autumn light, tiled and thatched and stacked with drying millet, and above them the temple smoke wandered upward in a dozen thin columns.

Yue walked there in the afternoon with her tails tucked tight against the small of her back. It was the only place in the palace where she could breathe. Somewhere on the other side of the wall a flute was being played badly. She stopped to listen.

"Lovely tree," said a voice behind her. "A little overripe, perhaps. Like most things in the capital."

She turned.

The man was sitting on top of the wall with his legs crossed, in the unconcerned manner of someone who had been there for some time. He was thin and affable, in a patched brown traveling coat, with a pedlar's pack at his side and a leopard-colored scatter of freckles across his nose and cheeks. He looked about forty. His eyes were quite wrong. They were a pale spotted amber, set slightly too far apart, and they watched her with the lazy, delighted attention of a cat watching a bird that did not yet know about the cat.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said. "I sell rare things. I have a tortoiseshell comb you might like. A bronze mirror from Wu, in the south. Jade. Remedies for a bad stomach." His smile widened. "Remedies for a missing tail."

Yue's blood went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course not." He hopped down from the wall, landing with the noiseless fluidity of an animal, and strolled toward her across the beaten earth. He walked in a slow circle around her, sniffing delicately, like a man at a market. "Fox. Very clean. A little old, a little tired. And burned." He breathed in. "Eight. I would say eight. You paid for it recently. The edge is still raw."

"Who are you?"

"A broker." He bowed, with a flourish. "Shen Gongbao, of the Jie. A merchant of obligations, a dealer in debts and favors, a humble go-between among the powers. I deal with everyone. Heaven. The Earth. The odd goddess with an appetite and an agenda." He tilted his head. "I've been told you have both."

"Who told you?"

"A pheasant." He laughed, a high, warm, tuneful laugh. "Oh, don't look so alarmed, sweetheart. She wasn't confiding. She was bargaining. Hu Ximei has a very large appetite for information and very small pockets. I gave her a price. She gave me a name." He touched his nose. "And this did the rest."

A pheasant, thought Yue. She's selling me to the first buyer. The cold in her chest went a little colder, and under it something smaller and sharper began, slowly, to wake.

"What do you want?"

"At the moment? Nothing. I'm introducing myself." Shen Gongbao spread his hands. "A very new queen in a very old court, with an eight-tailed spine and a mortal's face. You'll need a friend, or at least a creditor. I'm both. When you find yourself short of a tail, or of a favor, or of a rare and valuable silence, you'll know where to look. I'm easy to find. Anywhere there's a market."

"And the price?"

"Ah." The pale eyes crinkled. "That's the beauty of it. We'll discuss it when you're desperate. It's so much simpler."

Yue folded her arms. It was the first gesture she'd made since coming to Chaoge that was entirely her own. "I'm not desperate."

"Of course not." He bent and picked up a fallen persimmon, turned it in his freckled fingers, took a neat bite. "Mm. Overripe." He wiped his mouth. "I'll leave you to your garden, Your Majesty. I only came to pay respects, and to deliver a small piece of news, free of charge, as a gift."

"News."

"Rumor, rather. I'm told it's all the rage in the south." He swung his pack onto his shoulder. He had already turned toward the wall. "The Zhou have a clever advisor, a patient old man who sits by a river with a hook and no bait. His name is Jiang Ziya, and he's collecting evidence. Of demons. In Chaoge." He paused, one foot on the stones. "He's very interested in fox-scent. He has a nose almost as good as mine."

"I'm not afraid of a fisherman."

"No?" Shen Gongbao glanced back over his shoulder, and for just a moment the amiable, freckled face was entirely grave. "Then perhaps you'd care for some advice, from a friend. Your sister is in the city too, you know."

"I have no—"

"The lute-player," said Shen Gongbao gently. "The one with the pipa, at the wine-shop by the east gate. The one who taught you your name."

The persimmon tree shook, though there was no wind.

By the time Yue had found her breath, the wall was empty, and a single bright orange fruit lay on the beaten earth where he had dropped it, with one neat bite taken out.

She stood looking at it for a long time.

She had not seen the pipa-player in sixty years. They had parted badly, on a riverbank, over a question Yue could no longer quite remember: something about whether a lamp had any business having opinions. It had been a stupid quarrel. The pipa-player had won it, as she won everything, by laughing, and Yue had gone away with her ears hot and the name she had been given still ringing in them. Yue, the pipa-player had said, on the night a nameless white fox crept in out of the rain to listen to her play. The moon. Look at you, small and cold and pretending not to shine.

Before that night, nobody had ever called her anything but little lamp.

The one who taught you your name. Shen Gongbao had said it as a gift, and as a hook. Yue knew perfectly well that she had just been shown the price of his friendship, and that he had shown it to her first so that she would pay it in advance. It did not make the hook any less sharp.

Somewhere beyond the wall the bad flute started up again, and fell silent, and started again, a little better.

She picked up the persimmon, and, after a moment's thought, carried it inside to give to Fuji, who would want to count the bite marks.
