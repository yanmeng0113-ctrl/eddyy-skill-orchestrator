Chapter Twenty-Five: The Uncle's Memorial

Bi Gan wrote the memorial four times, and burned it three.

He did it at night, in his own study, by a lamp turned so low that the brush was more a feeling than a sight. It was a small room in a large house. It held a table, a stool, a rack of bamboo slips, and a brazier whose coals he had banked to a dull red. The window looked on a courtyard where a single plum tree was dropping the last of its leaves into the dark. He had kept the house for forty years. He had never before been afraid in it.

To the Grand Preceptor, Wen Zhong, from Bi Gan, Prince of Shang, greeting. He read it over. It was a perfectly good opening. He had used it, in one form or another, ever since his brother died.

I write on a matter of the realm and of the house. I have reason to believe that the woman sitting at the king's right hand, who calls herself Su Fuji, daughter of the Marquis of Zhongzhou, is not what she appears.

He stopped.

It was true. It was, in fact, the single most carefully weighed sentence he had written in his life. And it did not say what he meant. It did not say that this woman had stood before the Master of Sacrifices and refused a priest the right to drag a half-dead servant back to her pit. It did not say that she had walked into the burning ruin of a granary in a brown coat. It did not say that in the long gallery above the empty court, the day after the wine-pond, he had seen the king look at her as he had looked at no living thing since he was nine years old and had been given a dying sparrow to hold.

That is the trouble, thought Bi Gan. I have no word for what she is. Only for what she is not.

He fed the slip to the brazier. It curled and went black.


* * *

He had been watching her since the morning of the dowry.

It was a habit of his, and the habit of a man of sixty who had served three kings. One looked at the people nearest the throne. One noted what they ate, whom they spoke to, whether they spent their money on silk or on grain. It was not suspicion. It was only a kind of long, patient bookkeeping. He had done it to the Marquises and the Master of Sacrifices and his own brother's concubines, and he had written the results down, in small, neat, secret characters, in a book that no one would ever read.

The girl from Zhongzhou had given him a great deal to write.

Cannot read the old script. Admitted it unprompted. That had been the first entry, on the day of the straw carts. He had underlined it, because it was too honest to be a lie. Holds her cup as a person who learned it last week. Rinses her own cup. Speaks of her father without resentment. Is afraid of her cousin.

Is not afraid of the king.

The last one he had underlined three times.

It had taken him a full month to understand that the girl was not who she said, and another to understand that she was not what he feared. He had gone looking for a demon. He had found, in the end, only a person: bewildered, brave, a little too quick to laugh, with a stubborn way of lowering her voice when she was frightened. He knew a demon would not have lowered her voice.

He knew other things, too. He had been to the South Court.

It had been the day after the fire, at noon. He had gone ostensibly to inspect the ruin, at the king's request, and had stood in the muddy yard with his hands in his sleeves while a crowd of the injured and the displaced and the merely curious eddied around him. A tall rawboned healer was dressing a child's burn. A thin woman with a cracked face was ladling porridge. And on a stool by the door, with a bundle of bamboo slips on her knee and an ink-stained hand moving quickly, sat the queen's maid.

He'd known her slightly. She was a grey, quiet girl in a headcloth, always a half-step behind the queen, with a face that was easy to forget. He had never looked at her closely. He did now, because she was writing, and he had been a clerk before he was a prince, and he could not stop himself from reading over a person's shoulder.

She was writing in the old square script.

It was a very fine hand. It was spiky, upright, perfectly regular, the formal hand of the court of Zhongzhou, which he had last seen on the margin of a letter from the Marquis twenty years ago. It was not a hand a maid learns. It was not a hand that anyone learns except in a particular household, at a particular age, under a very particular sort of tutor. And she was writing it as easily as breathing: Name, house, what was done, who knew.

He had stood there for a long time.

The girl had looked up. She had not been alarmed. She had met his eyes with a calm, tired, level look, the look of someone who has been expecting a particular footstep for some time and has had the courtesy to finish her sentence first. She had bowed her head, politely, and gone on writing.

Ah, thought Bi Gan.

It was the neatest piece of arithmetic he had ever done. A queen who could not read the old script. A maid who wrote it better than he did. A black carriage that had been ridden in by one girl and walked beside by another. A real daughter of the Marquis who had vanished, by all accounts, in the East Wing of a post-station, and a false one who had come to the capital in her face.

He had gone home that night and written nothing at all.


* * *

"You'll forgive me, my lord. I couldn't help noticing the lamp."

Bi Gan did not start. He had spent forty years in the antechambers of power and had learned not to start at anything.

He did not turn his head, either. The voice had come from the corner of the room, behind the door, from a place that had held nothing but shadow a moment ago. It was an easy, amiable, tuneful voice, the sort that would be at home haggling over cloth.

"You keep it so low. It must be hard on the eyes." A soft slither of canvas. "I always say, it's the thrifty who go blind."

"Who are you?"

"A pedlar, my lord. Shen, by trade. I sell rare things." A thin freckled man in a patched brown coat stepped out of the dark, with a pack at his hip. He had a leopard's scatter of spots across his nose and a lazy, spotted amber stare. He was smiling, in a friendly, harmless way, like a man who has been waiting for a very long time and has found something to eat. "I was told you'd had an exciting week."

"How did you get into my house?"

"Oh, I let myself in. It's a small talent." The pedlar glanced around, with open interest, at the table, the stool, the burnt slips in the brazier. "You've been writing, I see. And burning. It's a dreadful waste of good bamboo."

"I did not ask for a merchant."

"No. But you wanted one. That's the odd part." He set down his pack and sat on the edge of the stool, as comfortably as if he had been invited. "A man writes four drafts to the Grand Preceptor in a single night. He burns three. What he wants, my lord, is not to write the letter. What he wants is to be told what to write. I sell that. Direction. Certainty. A verdict, if you like." The amber eyes gleamed. "At a very reasonable price."

Bi Gan was silent.

He was thinking, with great calm, that he had never in his life seen anything like the man who sat on his stool. The cloth of the coat was real. The freckles were real. The smell, faint and warm, was not a man's smell at all. It was the smell of a great cat in a dry place, in the sun.

"I know what the queen is," said Shen Gongbao softly. "I could tell you. I could tell you the lot, her name, her mistress, the number of her tails. I could even tell you the real Su Fuji's whereabouts, if you'd care. The one with the very pretty handwriting." His smile widened a very little. "It would save you a great deal of trouble. And you'd have no need to write to anyone at all."

"And the price?"

"Ah. That's the beauty of it. It's a very small one." He spread his hands. "I'd only want you to say that you'd not write to Wen Zhong. To let it lie. A man of your sense could do it. He's very busy. He hates being sent for."

He wants me to keep silent, thought Bi Gan. Not to speak. Not to write. And it came to him, with the cold clean click of a counter falling into place, that this was the one thing in the whole world he would not do.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"I do not buy verdicts. I make my own." He looked at the pedlar steadily. "You say you can tell me what she is. I believe you. I do not want to know. I would prefer to find it out in front of the court, in the open, by a man whose eye cannot be bribed. If she is what I fear, I'll have the truth to bear. If she is what I hope, she'll have nothing to be afraid of."

Shen Gongbao sat quite still. For the first time something moved in the amber eyes that was not amusement. It was something older, and rather rueful, and almost like respect.

"That," he said slowly, "is the most inconvenient thing a man has said to me in a century."

"I am sorry to disappoint you."

"Oh, I'm not disappointed." He rose, and swung the pack to his shoulder, and was suddenly very much taller. "I'm only revising my accounts. You've just shown me exactly what I'm selling. And to whom." He paused at the door, with his hand on the latch. "I'll not trouble you again, my lord. But I'll tell you one thing for nothing, since you've been so very honest."

"What is it?"

"The Heavenly Eye does not see everything." The freckled face was grave. "It sees what a person is. It does not see what a person has been told to be. A great many things have been got round, down the years, by someone who knew the difference." A small, polite bow. "Good night."

The door swung open on an empty courtyard. The plum tree shook, though there was no wind.


* * *

Bi Gan sat for a long time in the dark.

He did not light the lamp. He listened to the small sounds of his own house, the creak of a beam, a distant dog, the fall of a leaf. He thought of the girl in the grey headcloth with her careful hand. He thought of a tall, tired, stubborn man, bent over his ledgers at the fifth watch, who had once been a boy with a dying sparrow. He counted his breaths. He had done it since he was a child, in the hours when there was nothing else to do, and the numbers brought a certain order. One. Two. Three.

He did not reach one hundred.

He took up the brush. He drew a fresh slip from the rack, and wrote, in the smallest and neatest of his characters, a single line.

To the Grand Preceptor: come as soon as you are able. I have been wrong about a great many things. I should like to be wrong about this.

He sealed it, and laid it on the table.

It was after midnight. It would be dawn in three hours. He told himself, with the sad firm logic of an old man, that there was time, that the Preceptor was still at the northern passes, that the moon would be new on the ninth. There is time, he thought. Time to speak to the girl. Time to speak to Fuji. Time to find a way to be wrong in front of the court.

He went to bed.

He was woken before sunrise by the sound of the war-horns.

They came from the north, low and rolling, very far away, and then nearer: the great bronze horns of the northern army, which he had not heard sounded in the streets of Chaoge for nineteen years. He lay for a moment with his eyes open, listening, in the grey light. Then, slowly, he sat up.

A servant was at the door, white-faced, with a lamp.

"My lord," he said. "My lord, the Grand Preceptor. It's the Preceptor Wen Zhong. He's at the northern gate. He's brought the army." He swallowed. "He's already outside the city walls."
