Chapter Thirty-Five: How a Rumor Is Made

By the third morning, there were four versions of the story in the south ward, and Su Fuji had written all of them.

She had not meant to. It had begun as a joke, in the dark gallery, in the first heat of an idea, and she had carried it back to the Counting Room like a stray cat, expecting someone to tell her to put it down. Nobody had. Now it was the fifth hour, and she sat in the long hall of the South Court with a slip of bamboo on her knee and a brush behind her ear, listening to a woman called Yaoniang tell it to forty strangers over a trestle table, and finding, with an odd cold pang, that it had improved in the telling.

"—so the old Preceptor opens his Eye," said Yaoniang, standing on a bench with a ladle in her fist, "and the whole hall goes dark, and out of the dark comes a voice like a great bronze bell, and it says: This one is a fox. And the whole court gasps! And he points at the queen. Our queen!"

"Our queen," said a man in the third row, reverently.

"Our fox," said Yaoniang. "Nine tails. White as a bone. She turned to him with a smile, and she said: Grand Preceptor, I have lived in the king's house four months, and I have never taken a single sack of grain that was not mine. Can you say as much for the lords of Shang?" She swept the ladle through the air. "And the Eye shut. It could not look. For there was nothing in her to see but a heart that had done no harm."

"That isn't what happened," said Fuji, from the back.

"It's what ought to have happened," said Yaoniang, without turning her head.

There was a murmur of delighted agreement. A child at the end of the table raised a hand. "Does she really have nine tails?"

"Five," said another child, with scorn. "She gave four to the poor."

"She gave them to a magistrate," said somebody's grandmother. "And he went to heaven."

Fuji laid her brush down on the slip and closed her eyes.


* * *

"You do know," said Di Xin, "that this is slander."

"Yes."

"It will do the Zhou's work for them. Jiang Ziya has been trying to write the queen is a fox since the autumn."

"I know. I read his notices." She was sitting on the bench in the Counting Room, in the grey gown, with her feet tucked under her and a bowl of cold millet in her lap. It was evening. The door was barred. The lamp burned low. Across from her, on the cot, a young woman with white hair sat cross-legged with a blanket round her shoulders, listening with an expression of grave, bright, doubtful attention, like a person trying to follow a conversation in a language she has only just learned. She had come out of the white fur the moment the door was barred, and she wore her own shape a little warily still, as one wears a new coat.

"So explain it to me," said the king, "very slowly. I'm told I'm quick with numbers and slow with people."

"It's quite simple." Fuji set down the bowl. "Consider what the court is afraid of. Not a fox. A fox is a story. What they're afraid of is a demon, a creature of the dark who has crept into the palace to eat the king's heart. It's why they were so angry. It's why Lord Geng turned the color of a plum." She ticked it off on her fingers. "Now. The Eye has looked. It has said, before the whole court: the Lady Su is human, and the king's beast is harmless. And no one believes it."

"Why not?"

"Because it's dull. It's very hard to run through the streets shouting everything is fine. A story needs a villain." She smiled thinly. "So the street has made one. It has taken the two halves of the afternoon, the queen who was not a queen, the fox in the sleeve, and welded them into a single creature. The fox-queen. The beast who sits on the throne." She spread her hands. "Which is nonsense. But it is sticky nonsense, and sticky nonsense can't be argued away. It can only be used."

"Used for what?"

"To make it work for us." She leaned forward. "Listen. Nobody can kill a queen. Not outright. Not with the Zhou in the west and every house in Shang watching. It would be regicide, or something very near it. But a rumor about a queen can be killed a hundred ways. They can say she's a witch. They can say she poisons wells, or steals children, or gnaws at the roots of the realm. And a human woman can be hanged for witchcraft by a mob, Di Xin, I've seen what the market does to a woman it has decided is a witch."

Yue's eyes dropped.

"But a fox," said Fuji gently, "is a different matter."

"How?"

"Everyone in Shang knows what a fox is. It's a thing you don't touch. It's a thing that curses your well if you chase it. It can't be caught with a net or killed with a spear, and if you do kill one, the dead fox's mate comes for your children in the winter." She said it in the cool dry tone of a clerk reading out a tax law. "The people of this city have been brought up from the cradle to be afraid of hurting a fox. I'm proposing that we allow them to go on being afraid." She drew a slow breath. "If the mob thinks I am a fox, they will not lay a hand on me. They'll bar their doors and leave bowls of millet gruel on the step."

There was a silence.

"Let them call me a fox," said Fuji. "A fox is harder to kill."


* * *

It was Yue who laughed first. It broke out of her in a startled helpless gust, and she put both hands over her mouth and rocked backwards on the cot.

"It's horrible," she gasped. "It's the most horrible thing I've ever heard. It's like building a trap out of the bait."

"It's economical."

"It's sneaky."

"I learned from the best." Fuji was smiling, quite unrepentant. "There's more. The people of the south ward are already telling it. We've no need to start it. We have only to make sure it's told in the right way."

"Which is?"

"That the fox-queen loves the poor." She said it plainly. "That she guards the south ward. That anyone who harms a child at the South Court will be cursed, in his house, in his field, in his bones. Let Yaoniang tell it. Let Qingpu say it at the sickbed. Let the boy Ming write it on a hundred slips, and leave them at the gates of the great houses." She paused. "Do you see? A rumor is a kind of tally. It's a ledger of what people believe. It can be kept honestly or dishonestly. But the main thing about a ledger is that whoever keeps the entries decides what is owed."

Di Xin was looking at her with an expression she had not yet seen on him: a kind of awed, rueful, entirely delighted horror.

"I have kept a ledger for three years," he said slowly. "I never once thought of doing that."

"You're an honest man."

"It's been remarked." He rubbed his temple. "And the cost? You'll be written as a fox. In the histories. A hundred years from now, some scholar will take down a slip, and read, the queen of Shang was a fox, and they'll believe it, because the Eye said so and the street said so and you've left nothing to say otherwise."

"Yes."

"You don't mind?"

Fuji was quiet.

She had thought about it, in the cell, counting stones. A great many things in the world went into the ledger and were never taken out again. A girl of nineteen could be a pheasant's rope, a fox's coat, a servant, a hostage, a name in a Marquis's study. It could be a very long time before anyone wrote down the real one.

"I'd rather be a fox who was alive," she said, "than a woman who was right."

Nobody spoke. On the cot, Yue had gone very still, with the blanket clutched at her throat and her amber eyes enormous.

"I'll write it down," said Fuji, lightly, to cover the silence. "In my own ledger. The real one. Su Fuji. Human. Kept a barn. There'll be a copy in the Archive, if the king permits. And if anyone ever cares to look, there it will be."

"There will," said Di Xin. His voice was not quite steady. "I'll see to it myself."


* * *

Her father left the next morning.

He went at dawn, on a good grey horse, with a pair of guards and two carts of seed grain, back to the hills of Zhongzhou. He had spent a night and a day putting his seal to a great many slips. He had sworn, before the king, to open every granary in his lands for the relief of the famine, at the royal price, under the terms of a certain roll sealed at a wine pond that nobody had read. He had wept, a little, at the gate. He had said a number of things to his daughter that she did not intend to repeat. And then he had done the one thing she had not expected: he had looked at the white-haired girl standing quietly behind her, and bowed, a great deep bow, from the saddle, with his hand over his heart.

"I will not forget what you did," said the Marquis of Zhongzhou.

"I only wore a face," said Yue.

"That," said Su Hu, "is the one thing that nobody has ever managed for my daughter." He had turned the grey horse's head to the road. "Look after each other. I find that I've very few opinions left. That is the last."

She watched him ride out of the gate, a thin stooped figure on a good horse, getting smaller in the dust. Yue's hand crept into hers and held on. They stood there until the carts had gone round the bend of the road.


* * *

She was crossing the garden of the queen's wing that night, an hour past midnight, with a lamp, when she saw the old man.

It was a cold, clear, windless night, with a nearly full moon riding high above the roofs. The persimmon tree stood in its square of beaten earth, black and silver, throwing a long crooked shadow across the stones. And at the garden gate, in the dark, in a plain white robe, with his hands folded in front of him and his face turned toward the moonlight, stood Bi Gan.

He had not come with a lantern. He had not come with a servant. He stood quite still, looking, with the expression of a man who has come out in the small hours to say a private prayer, and has been surprised by something he cannot put away. Fuji stopped.

She followed his eyes.

Under the persimmon tree, in a pool of moonlight, a small white fox sat on the beaten earth, washing her face. She was a very fine fox. She had five long silver tails that curved up behind her in the cold air, like a spray of reeds. She did not look up. She went on washing, slowly, with one paw, in the blithe unhurried manner of a creature who thinks herself alone.

And then, as the moon cleared the edge of a roof, she stretched, and rose.

It was not a swift thing. It was slow, soft, and quite beautiful, like a flower unfolding in a film of water. The small white body lengthened. The fur thinned and went to hair, long white hair, falling over bare narrow shoulders. The paws became hands. A thin pale girl in a plain undyed shift stood in the moonlight under a black tree, with five tails fading behind her like breath on a bronze mirror, and tipped back her head, and laughed, quietly, to the sky, for no reason at all, with a sound like water over stones.

Fuji heard a very small noise.

It came from the gate. It was the sound of an old man letting out a breath he had held for fifty years.

"Your Majesty," said Prince Bi Gan.

He did not look at her. He did not take his eyes from the girl under the tree. His voice was dry and quiet and perfectly steady, and there were tears running down his face, openly, into his square white beard.

"I think," he said, "that I have seen enough."
