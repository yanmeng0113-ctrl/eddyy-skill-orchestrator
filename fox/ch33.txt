Chapter Thirty-Three: What the Eye Saw

It was dark in the sleeve, and warm, and smelled of ink.

Yue lay curled against the king's forearm with her nose tucked under her tails and her ears flat against her skull, and listened to his pulse. It was going very fast. It beat against her ribs like a second heart. Through the weave of the wool, a few finger-widths from her face, a thin bright thread of daylight showed where the cuff had not quite closed, and through it, if she held very still, she could see a slice of the world: a stretch of clay floor, a bronze foot, the hem of a grey skirt.

She had been a fox since the morning. She had not been a fox in front of anyone since the night of the fire. It was extraordinary how quickly she'd forgotten what it was to be small: how loud the world was, how tall, how full of smells, ink and sweat and wool and the sharp metal tang of fear. The hall was full of it. She could smell Lord Geng's rage, and the old prince's sorrow, and under all of it, faint and sweet and unmistakable, the warm green scent of a pheasant who was very pleased with herself.

She knows, thought Yue. She saw me go in.

And then the light came.


* * *

It did not come through the thread. It came through everything.

It was cold. That was the first thing. It was a cold bright silver-gold wash, like the first light on a frozen pond, that rolled out across the hall from a point near the dais and bleached the color out of the world. The wool of the sleeve went transparent. The shadow under her chin vanished. She felt the light go through her as a hand goes through water, without resistance, without the least regard for the thing it passed through, and every hair on her body rose.

The Eye.

It was not looking at her. It was looking at the girl in the grey gown. She could tell, because she could feel the line of its attention, like a thread pulled very taut, running from the old man's brow to the place on the clay floor where Fuji stood, and she could feel what it found there.

She did not know how she knew. It was a thing that came with the light. It was like being inside a lamp, and seeing the wick.

A girl, said the Eye. It did not speak. There was no voice in it. There was only a plain, grave, mild report, as of a very old tally-keeper reading a column of figures. A girl of nineteen. Human. Entirely. Flesh and bone and a heart that does sums. Frightened. Honest. Tired. Has not slept in six days. Holds a great love for a man, a woman, a barn. Carries no enchantment. Carries no seal. Wears no face but her own. A pause. It is, I am quite certain, the plainest thing I have ever seen.

Yes, thought Yue, with a great surge of fierce pride. Yes. She is.

The light held for a long moment. Then, quite gently, it moved on.


* * *

"She is a woman."

Wen Zhong's voice was hoarse. It came from a long way off. She heard it as through water.

"Su Fuji, daughter of the Marquis of Zhongzhou. She is what she says. She is flesh. She is mortal. She wears no glamour, and she bears no spell, and she has told the truth." There was a faint, wet, rasping sound, as of a man drawing breath through a very tight throat. "I have never in my life seen anything so entirely what it is."

The hall exploded.

It began as a gasp and became a roar. Voices rose from every side: shouts, cries, the clatter of overturned cups. Somebody was laughing in a high, thin, helpless way. Somebody was calling on the gods. She heard the scrape of a dozen stools, the heavy rattle of bronze, and the shrill, furious, carrying voice of Lord Geng, shouting over everything.

"Then who's been on the throne?"

"The queen of Shang," roared one of the Zhong brothers. "Four months, four months! It's an outrage! The king took a demon to his bed!"

"She was a fox!"

"She was a witch!"

"I said so from the first! I said—"

"Silence."

It came from the dais. It was not a king's voice. It was dry and cold and perfectly quiet, and it fell on the hall like a handful of ice. Yue felt the arm she was lying against go rigid.

"I will have silence," said Di Xin, "in my own house."

It was an odd thing to hear. He was not shouting. He had never shouted, in her hearing. He was speaking in the same dry, level, slightly weary tone in which he read out a grain-return. But something in it made the clamor falter, and drop, and fade, until the only sounds in the whole hall were the rustle of cloth and the hiss of the lamps.

"The Preceptor has spoken," said Di Xin. "The Lady Su is exactly what she says. She is a woman, and a Marquis's daughter, and my guest. Anyone who raises his voice to her will answer to me." He paused. "I am sorry that the court was misled. I should have been more careful. The creature who wore her face for four months did so to save a life, and I will not hear it called a witch's trick."

"Then it was a fox!" shouted the elder Zhong.

"It was."

"Where is it?"

"I think," said a quiet voice, "that you had better ask the Preceptor."

It was Su Hu. He had put his arm around his daughter's shoulders. He was grey as a stone, and trembling, and quite unmistakably angry. "He has opened the Eye, Lord Zhong. It sees what a thing is. He has looked at my daughter. Let him look, in the same light, at the rest of the room."

Oh, no, thought Yue.

There was a very long silence.


* * *

She felt it turn.

It was like a great slow wheel. She felt the Eye leave the girl on the floor, and travel across the hall, past the nobles on their mats, past Lord Geng, past a pale woman in grey, who made no sound. It came toward the dais. It came up the three steps, with a cold, dreadful, unhurried gentleness, and stopped.

It rested on the king.

She felt it go over him. She felt it read him, in the same brief, grave, tender way it had read Fuji: A man. Thirty-four. Exhausted. Has not slept in two days. Counts. Keeps a jar of earth for every grave he did not prevent. Loves. A pause. Is afraid. And then, very quietly, as if with a sort of weary astonishment: Is the first king I have ever known who has not wished me to be wrong.

Oh, thought Yue.

It moved to the sleeve.

It was not like being seen. She had been seen many times. It was like being read. The silver-gold light passed through the wool, the skin, the little white body curled against the king's arm, and she felt herself opened, from end to end, like a bamboo roll, and every slip of her laid out and read at a glance. Four hundred years. A cave in the northern hills. A white kit in the rain, listening to a pipa. A voice like water under ice. A bronze lamp with nine wicks. A rafter, a cobweb, a drum. A girl in a post-station, pouring wine. A fire. A barn. A rain. A mound of ash, and a string, and a voice that said be nobody. A tall man on a roof, watching a red star come up. A kiss. A hand held in the dark.

I want to live, said a very small voice, somewhere in the middle of her.

She felt the Eye stop on it.

It stayed there for a long moment. The light did not warm. It did not change. But the grave, mild, tally-keeper's attention seemed, for an instant, to slow, like a man looking at a figure in a column that he had not expected to find.

I see, said the Eye. It was almost gentle. Five tails. A lamp. A goddess's order. A debt to a broker. It paused. And a name.

Please, thought Yue. She did not know to whom she thought it. Please don't.

You are not wicked, said the Eye.

It was a simple statement. It had no triumph in it, and no relief. It was only the plain, mild report of an old instrument at the end of a long day. You are a small, frightened, stubborn creature who has spent four hundred years being told what to do and has, in the last four months, decided to try a different arrangement. I have seen a great many foxes. I have never seen one who ought less to be burned.

She could not breathe.

It will not be enough, the Eye added, very sadly. You know that. He will have to speak. I am only the thing that sees. He is the one who must say what he has seen, and I am not able to say how that will go. A faint, warm, apologetic pressure, like a thumb on her brow. I am sorry, little fox. Good luck.

The light went out.


* * *

The first thing Yue knew, when she could see again, was that the hall was entirely silent.

The second was that the king had gone cold all over. She could feel it through the wool. It was as though a hand had been dipped in iced water and laid against the whole length of his arm.

She could smell the pheasant too, through the gap in the cuff, three mats from the dais. The warm green smell of self-satisfaction had gone sour in a moment, with a sharp thin edge to it, like milk on the turn. Hu Ximei had expected a verdict. A condemnation. A white fox dragged out by the scruff in front of the court, and a goddess's order carried out by a man who had cried while he did it. She had set the board with such care. She had not allowed for an eye that could weep.

A single bright whistle sounded in the silence, very short, as of a bird that has been struck. Nobody turned to look. Everyone had somewhere else to be looking.

The third was the sound of a man weeping.

It was very quiet. It came from just in front of the dais. It was a dry, harsh, painful sort of sobbing, that of a person who has not wept in thirty years and has forgotten how it is done. She could see through the gap in the cuff: a pair of worn grey boots, a hem, the scuffed toe of an old soldier's shoe. Wen Zhong was on his knees on the clay floor, with his helm off and his hands over his face. The black band lay on the floor beside him. His forehead was streaming with something that was not sweat.

"Preceptor," said Di Xin. His voice was very low.

"Give me a moment, boy."

"Take all you need."

"It's very bright," said the old man, brokenly. "I'd forgotten. It's so very bright. It's always so bright when it sees something it loves." He drew a long, shuddering breath. When he lowered his hands, his face was ravaged, and wet, and astonishingly calm. He looked up at the king. His eyes, the two good ones, were red. The third, in the middle of his forehead, was already closing, a thin vertical line, like a healing cut.

There were no gasps. No one in the great hall of Shang moved. They had all, at that moment, the stunned bewildered stillness of people who have seen a thing they will never be able to explain. The only sound was the old man's ragged breathing.

"Great King," said Wen Zhong.

"Yes."

"I have looked at the Lady Su, and she is what she says." He said it steadily. "I have looked at the Marquis, who is an honest man. And I have looked at you, who are, to my great sorrow and joy, the best king that I have served."

"Preceptor—"

"I am not finished." The old man's voice was very gentle. "I should say, in front of the court, that I have done my duty. I have used the Eye. I have seen what is in this room. And I should tell you, as a friend, that it is not in my power to pretend I have not."

He lifted one shaking hand and pointed. Not at Fuji. Not at Su Hu. At the sleeve of the king's left arm, where, in the warm dark, a small white animal had gone absolutely still.

"Great King," said Wen Zhong, "you have a demon in your arm."
