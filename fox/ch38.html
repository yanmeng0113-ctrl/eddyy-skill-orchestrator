Chapter Thirty-Eight: What the Gods Eat

Di Xin had been afraid, in a sensible and orderly way, of seven things that evening, and he found that none of them had been this.

She did not descend. There was no light, no thunder, no great white wing. There was only a moment when the sky above the court was empty, and a moment after when it was not, and the goddess Nüwa stood on the platform of the altar of the year, between the ring of singing tripods and the stone, as if she had been standing there for a long time and had merely been waiting for him to look up.

She was very tall. He had expected that. He had not expected the color. She was the brown-gold of a fired jar, with a faint glaze over her skin, like a light rain on a river-bank, and fine hairline cracks ran across her cheeks and along her forearms, glinting. Her hair hung to the ground in a long dark fall of something that moved like water. She wore no crown. Her eyes were two small steady flames. And below the waist, where a woman's skirts should have been, the long smooth coil of her went down over the steps in a slow bright curve, and away across the frozen court, and out of sight.

Through her left shoulder, quite plainly, he could see a star.

It's true, he thought. They're going out.

He did what he had been taught to do in front of a power he could not fight. He bowed. It was the correct bow of a king of Shang to a god, deep, from the waist, with his hands open and empty at his sides. Then he straightened up, and, quite deliberately, did not kneel.

"Mother of Men," said Di Xin.

"King of Shang." The voice was not loud. It was in the air and in the stones and in the bones of his own skull, all at once. "You have grown thin."

"It has been a busy year."

"So I have observed." The flame-eyes passed over him, and moved on to the girl at his side, and softened, in a way he could not have described. "Little lamp. You have put down my knife."

"Yes, Mother."

"I am not angry."

"I know," said Yue. She had gone very white. She had one hand wound into his sleeve and the other closed tight on something small. "That's the worst of it."

* * *

"There is a thing that I should like to explain," said Nüwa, "in plain words, since I think you have never been told it plainly. I should like it understood that I do not say it to be cruel."

She sat down.

It was an extraordinary thing to see, a god sitting on a step. She folded the long coil of herself in three slow turns on the lowest stair, like a woman settling to a task, and laid her cracked hands in her lap. At the foot of the steps, on her face in the frost, Hu Ximei lay with her nine heads bowed. No one looked at her.

"When I made your kind," said the goddess, "I made them from the yellow earth of the river, with my hands, one by one, in a very great hurry. I was lonely. They were warm. They were noisy. They were frightened of a great many things, and in their fright they looked up, and I was there." She turned one hand over. "I had not thought what it meant. A thing that is made must be sustained. A thing that is looked at must be looked at with something. And so I learned what it is that the gods eat."

"Fear," said Di Xin.

"Meaning." The flame did not move. "A great many things in this world are fed by grief. When a mother loses a child, the grief goes up, and it is a fire, and we warm our hands at it. When a man is led to the altar in front of ten thousand people, and every one of them knows what is about to happen, and every one of them is afraid, the fear goes up like a flame, and it lights the whole sky." She said it without a trace of appetite. It was a bleak, careful tone, that of a woman reading out the signs of her own sickness. "I do not say that it is good. I say that it is so. It is how we are made. A god lives on what a people will pay to be spared. If they are never made to pay, they forget to be afraid. If they forget to be afraid, they forget us. And what is forgotten—"

She lifted her left hand. The starlight passed through it as light passes through a thin sheet of horn.

"I have watched Lu of the Ford go out like a lamp in a draft. He was a small god. He had a river and a ferry and a name that nobody remembered to say. He asked me, near the end, if it would hurt." Her voice was perfectly level. "I told him that it would not. It was a lie. It is the only one I have told in five thousand years."

Nobody said anything. In the dark, far off, a dog was barking.

"You are the first king in a hundred generations," said Nüwa, "to have understood it. You counted. You looked at what the altar cost, and you decided it was too dear, and you laid it down. I have thought about it a great deal. I have thought that you may be right." She lifted her eyes. "But I am a god, king of Shang. I do not have the option of being right. I have only the option of being alive."

"I know," said Yue, very low. "You said that to me."

"I did."

"It's what I said, too." Yue's voice shook. "Before the market. When I thought I was going to die. I want to live." She swallowed. "I understood it, Mother. I understand you. That's why I couldn't hate you, even when you—" She stopped.

"Yes," said the goddess, gently. "I know. It is why I chose you."

* * *

It was very quiet.

Di Xin stood in the frozen court with a girl's cold hand wound into his sleeve, and he found that, for the first time in the whole of that strange long year, he had no list. It had all been taken away. He was a tired man in a night-robe, in front of a creature of fired clay who was dying, slowly and quite lucidly, of a thing that he had done. He thought of the great black ranks of the old rites. He thought of a girl of fourteen with a cord on her wrists, and a woman in the dust with her hands out. He thought of a pit.

He thought, She is not lying.

It was the worst of it. He had come prepared for a tyrant, for a thing of lies, for a liar in a robe. He had a speech ready, and a stack of good clean arguments, in order. And what he found, when he came to it, was a sad, plain, patient woman of clay who had told him, as one tells a healer, exactly where it hurt.

"May I say something?" he said.

"You may."

"It is not a very good speech."

"I have heard a great many of the other kind."

He drew a breath. It smoked in the cold. "You say that you are fed by what people will pay to be spared. By fear. By a death that's looked at." He kept his voice flat, and dry, and plain, the voice of a man reading out a column. "I have been reading the death-rolls of Shang for twenty years. In the reign of my grandfather, the Office took three hundred and four people in a single year. I have the figure. I have all the figures." A little muscle moved in his cheek. "Not one of them was a volunteer."

"No."

"Then I don't see what is being fed." He did not raise his voice. "A thing that lives on the deaths of the people who worship it is not a god. It's a wolf in the fold. I will not say it is evil, because I do not think it is. I think it is hungry, and I think it is afraid. But I think that if a creature must be given a child every spring in order to go on being, then its life is not worth the price. And a thing that is not worth the price," said Di Xin, "is not worth worshipping."

He had not planned to say it. It dropped into the court like a stone.

The tripods stopped singing.

There was no sound at all. The goddess sat on the lowest step with her cracked hands folded in her lap, and her flame-eyes looked at him without any expression whatever. He found that he was counting. He had got to nineteen, in the cold, before she spoke.

"Perhaps," said Nüwa.

It was not what he had expected.

"Perhaps that is so," she said. "I have wondered. I have had a long time to wonder it, in my hill, with my lamp, and the clay going dry about me." A very faint line, at the corner of her mouth, might have been the ghost of a smile. "Do you know, no one has ever said it to my face? They said that I was merciful. They said that I was terrible. A priest of the Office once told me that I was a mother. But no one has ever stood up in front of me and said that I did not deserve to be fed."

"I am sorry," said Di Xin, and meant it.

"No. You are not. It is why I like you." She unfolded herself, slowly, from the stair. Her height went up and up, until the great dark fall of hair stirred against the sky. "I shall tell you something that I have told no one. I think that you are right. It is no matter. A thing may be unworthy and still wish to live. It is the whole sad history of the world."

"Then stop," said Yue. Her voice cracked on it. "Mother, I'll go on being your lamp. I'll burn whatever you like. Only stop asking for it. Not him. Not the Office. Just the altar."

"I have stopped asking, little one. I stopped at the rite, when he cut the cord, and I did not send a second storm. You think that I did not choose that?" The goddess shook her head, slowly, and a fine dust of gold came off her hair. "But I am not the only one who has a hand on this country. There are others in the high places, the Chan, and the ones who sit above them, and they are not fond of lamps, and they are not tender about the price. If I do not act, they will. And they will not be gentle with him."

"Mother—" said Hu Ximei, from the frost. "Mother, the fox has refused. I can do it. I'll finish—"

"Be silent, child."

It was said very gently. It was the gentlest thing he had ever heard. The pheasant went quite still, as a hare goes still in the grass, and did not speak again.

"I made you a task, little lamp," said Nüwa, turning. "I gave you a knife, and a face, and a season. I asked one thing of you. You have done a great deal that I did not ask for, and you have declined the only thing that I did." The long coil slid over the steps, with a sound like a slow river. "I am very fond of you. I would not have you think otherwise. But I am bound, as everyone is. A covenant is a covenant. And a lamp that will not burn where it is told is no lamp of mine."

"Don't," said Di Xin.

He had stepped in front of her. He did not remember doing it. He stood in the frost with his empty hands spread, and his heart going like a drum, between a girl in white and a goddess who had been alive for five thousand years. It was, he thought quite clearly, the most ridiculous thing he had ever done. It was also the only thing that he could think of.

"It is not a matter of what I wish," said Nüwa, sadly. "I am sorry, king."

She raised her hand.

It was a long hand, cracked and gold, with the starlight running through the fingers. It rose slowly through the dark, palm outward, toward the girl who stood behind him, and where it passed the frost on the stones crackled and went white, and above it, high in the black air, very faint and far away, something began to glow: a small round bronze lamp, hanging in the dark, with nine wicks in a ring. Five of them were burning.

The hand stopped, an inch from his shoulder. It was pointed at Yue.
