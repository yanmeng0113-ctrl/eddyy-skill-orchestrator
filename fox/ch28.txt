Chapter Twenty-Eight: "My Queen"

Wen Zhong had two good eyes, and Yue had been afraid of neither of them.

It was the third she dreaded. She had never seen it. She did not know what it looked like, whether it was a slit or a jewel, whether it wept or burned. All she knew was what the market gossip said: that the Grand Preceptor of Shang kept it shut beneath a band of black bronze across his brow, and had opened it only a dozen times in thirty years, and that on every occasion something had died.

She stood on the dais in the great hall in a robe of deep red, at the king's right hand, with her face made up in the style of Zhongzhou and her hands folded under the base of an empty cup. It had been given to her by a steward so that she would have something to hold. Her fingers were damp.

"Breathe," said Di Xin, without moving his lips.

"I am."

"You're breathing as though you were being chased."

"I am being chased."

"Then breathe as though you'd been caught." He was looking straight ahead. His long face was perfectly composed, the face of a man at his accounts. Only a faint white line at the corner of his mouth betrayed him. "Three more breaths. He's coming in."

She had not seen Fuji for six days.

She had been told, on the first morning, by a grey-faced Qingpu at the palace gate: They've taken her. She says tell you she did the sums. She says it's all right. Yue had stood there for a whole long moment with her mouth open. Then she had turned and run for the Counting Room, and Di Xin had caught her at the door and held on to her, hard, while she tried to fight past him to the prison.

You can't, he'd said, again and again, into her hair. You can't. If you go, she's lost. They'll ask why the queen visits a maid. They'll ask what she is. We have a little time. We must use it to think.

She's in a cell, she'd said.

I know.

She's counting stones, she's alone—

I know. I know. I'm sorry. I know.

She had stopped fighting. It had cost more than anything she had ever done. She had gone back to the queen's wing and sat on the platform with her hands clenched in her lap, in the room that Fuji's presence had made into a home, looking at the lattice screen behind which a plain girl had once folded napkins and signaled yes and no with the sound of a bowl. She had not wept. She had been too angry. She had counted her tails instead, one by one, along the floor. Six. Six. Six.

On the other side of the wall, nothing had been heard for five days. Not a hum. Not a footstep.

She knows, thought Yue. She knows I'm trapped. She's waiting to see what I do.

In the front rank of the nobles, three mats down from the dais steps, sat the queen's cousin in modest grey, with her hands folded in her lap and a faint, fond, attentive look on her face, like a woman at a play whose ending she has already read. She caught Yue's eye. She smiled, and gave the smallest of nods, as one might to a friend across a crowded market. Soon, said the nod. Do hurry.

Yue looked away. It took every scrap of will she had.

There were other faces. Lord Geng, grey with a sleepless fury, sat between the Zhong brothers, who had put on their best bronze for the occasion and looked like men attending a feast. Young Wei sat behind them, sulking as ever. At the far end, on the Office's mats, there was one empty place. Yuan Shi had not come out of his temple in close on a month. Yue found, to her surprise, that she missed him. He had been the only man at court who had hated her honestly.

Breathe, she told herself. Three more breaths.

The doors at the far end of the hall swung open.


* * *

The Grand Preceptor entered alone.

He was smaller than she had expected. He was a lean, weathered, stooped man of sixty, in a plain grey campaign coat with the dust of the northern road still on its hem, and he walked with the slow, even, economical stride of a man who has covered a great many leagues on foot. He had a short grizzled beard. His hair was cropped close under a bronze helm. Across his forehead, beneath the helm's rim, ran a broad band of black metal, without ornament, from temple to temple.

That was all. There were no guards. His fifteen hundred horse were camped outside the north wall. He had come into the king's hall, at the head of the nobles of Shang, with a bundle of slips under one arm, and he looked, not like a terror, but like a tired clerk at the end of a long audit.

He stopped at the foot of the dais. He bowed. It was a short, correct bow, with the faintest suggestion of age in the knees.

"Great King," he said. His voice was very quiet. "I am glad to find you well."

"Preceptor." Di Xin inclined his head. "You've come a long way."

"Two thousand li, in twenty-one days. I have been told it was unnecessary." The grey eyes passed over the court, the nobles on their mats, the cold gleam of bronze, and fell on the queen. They rested there. They were ordinary eyes, steady and weary and courteous. They did not flame. "I came on rumour. I'm told the Prince Bi Gan had a letter sealed for me that same night; I have saved him the courier. And I've heard a number of things on the road."

"What things?"

"That the queen of Shang is a sorceress. That the south ward is a den of demons. That a mound of infant bones lies under a persimmon tree." He said it without inflection. "I have found, in my years, that when a rumor reaches me from three directions at once, it is generally a lie. But I have also found that it is generally a lie about something."

A ripple passed along the ranks.

"Therefore I will be brief. I intend, as is my right as Preceptor and as the king's oldest servant, to examine the queen. Not tonight. I should like a little more time, to settle my men and finish speaking to the district. But I will do it, publicly, in this hall, with the nobility of Shang for witness." The grey gaze did not flicker. "If she is innocent, she'll be cleared. If she is not, she will be dealt with. I do not mean to insult her. I mean to know."

Yue's hands tightened on the cup.

Di Xin said, in a voice of perfect mildness, "And if I say no?"

"Then I should be very sorry," said Wen Zhong, "and I should do it anyway."

There was a silence. It was not hostile. It was the silence of two old friends who had each said a difficult thing to the other and found it was true.

"I have asked one more person to attend," said the Grand Preceptor. "In the matter of identity. I have sent for the Marquis of Zhongzhou."

No, thought Yue.

She did not move. She did not breathe. The cup trembled once in her hands and was still.

The doors opened again.


* * *

He came in alone.

A tall, stooped, grey man, in a mourning-coat, with a scrape across one cheek and the dust of a three-day ride still on his boots. He walked slowly down the long aisle between the nobles, and the whispers rose and fell about him. His face was perfectly blank. He did not look to either side. He looked at the dais, and at the figure in red on the king's right, and his eyes did not leave her face.

She had never seen him before. She knew him at once. He had her friend's nose, and her friend's jaw, and a peculiar tilt to his head that she had seen on a plain girl in a lamp-lit room. He has always been afraid of everything, Fuji had said once, in the carriage, quite without bitterness. It makes him very careful. I think it's the best thing about him.

He stopped at the foot of the dais.

"Su Hu," said Wen Zhong gently. "Marquis of Zhongzhou. I have asked you here to look at the woman on the king's right. I should like you to tell the court whether she is the daughter you sent."

Su Hu did not answer.

He was looking at her. The whole hall was looking at him. In the silence, Yue could hear the faint hiss of the lamp and the dry click of a tablet being set down. She stood absolutely still. She wore Fuji's face. She wore her mouth, her nose, her hands. She had learned every line of it in a season, in the dark, and she knew that it was a good copy. It was a perfect copy. It would have deceived anyone in the world who did not love the original.

She saw the Marquis's eyes move. Over her brow. Her mouth. Her hands on the cup. Down to the hem of the red robe and back up again, slowly, with a kind of terrible, patient, loving scrutiny. She saw something change in them. It was a tiny thing. It began as a tightening and became a stillness. And then she saw the moment he saw it, as a man sees the thing under the ice.

"No," said the Marquis of Zhongzhou.

A gasp went round the hall.

"That is not my daughter."

Somebody cried out. A bronze cup rolled across the floor. On the dais, Yue felt Di Xin go rigid at her side, and she felt, far away, as in a dream, her whole body go very cold.

It's over, she thought. He knows. He's going to say it. He's going to say it in front of the Eye.

"This is not Su Fuji," said her father, in a high clear voice that carried to the farthest rafter. "My daughter is a plain girl with a plain face. She is tall. She cannot be made to hold a cup like a lady. She reads the old script, and the people of Zhongzhou used to say that she could add faster than the Master of the Granary. She walked beside the carts instead of riding. She was terrified of the king and would not have dared to stand in this hall." He drew a breath that shook. "The woman who stands there has her face. But she is not my daughter."

"Marquis," said Wen Zhong, very softly. "You are saying that the queen is an impostor."

"I'm saying nothing of the kind."

He stood a moment longer, with his head up and his hands at his sides. Then, quite slowly, as a man lowers himself into cold water, Su Hu of Zhongzhou went down on both knees on the clay floor of the great hall of Shang.

It was a thing a Marquis did not do. It was a thing, in forty years, that he had not done to any man living. His grey head bowed, and the lamplight caught the thin place at the crown. He put both hands flat on the floor, palms down, in the old obeisance of vassal to sovereign.

"I have not seen my daughter since the autumn," he said. "I sent her to this court as a hostage, and she did not write. I have been told that she died at Enzhou. I am not certain that she did not." His voice trembled, and broke, and steadied. "But I will tell the court what I have heard. I have heard that the queen of Shang, in her first season, opened a door in the south ward and took in the beaten and the sick. I have heard that she stood before the Master of Sacrifices and refused him. I have heard that she walked into a burning house for a stranger." A very long breath. "Whoever she is, she is the woman my daughter ought to have been. She is the woman the king chose. And I'll not be the one to say that she is not the queen."

Somewhere behind her, quite clearly, in the dead hush, a woman's soft voice murmured, "Oh, bravo."

Yue did not turn her head. But on her right, so quietly that it was no more than the shift of a breath, she felt Di Xin's hand close over hers beneath the sleeve of the red robe, and hold.

The Marquis lifted his head. His eyes were wet. They were fixed on Yue's face, quite steadily, with an expression she would remember until the end of her days. It was not a plea. It was a recognition, from one person who loved a girl to another.

"My queen," said Su Hu.
