Chapter Fourteen: An Unblemished Offering

Yue had been awake for less than a hundred breaths, and already she was being asked to defy a god.

"Madam." Ling's face was an inch from hers, grey-white, with eyes like a pair of lamp flames. "The Master of Sacrifices is at the outer door with six priests and a petition. The guard won't hold him long. I've put the healer in the back with the woman, and told the cook to start a very loud pot. You have to dress."

"Dress as what?"

"A queen."

"I'm wearing a queen. I've been wearing it for half a month." Yue sat up and discovered that her spine had stiffened in the night into a curved bronze rod, and that her tails, which had been folded into the base of her back all night, had gone to sleep. "What do I do about him?"

"Don't let him through the door."

"He's a priest, Fuji. I'm a fox. I have no standing whatever."

"You're the queen of Shang." The maid took her hands and hauled her to her feet. "Nobody in this palace is entitled to open a door into the queen's wing without her leave, and he knows it. He's knocked on it and stood outside to be polite. That is all he has done. He won't break it. He's too correct." She was already pulling the brown coat off Yue's shoulders. "Go and stand in front of it. Say nothing you have not planned. And whatever you do, do not let him say the word consecrated three times."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a legal formula. Three repetitions makes it binding." Fuji pulled a gown of dark green over Yue's head. "I read it in your husband's library. In his own hand, in the margin."

It took the rest of that little while to be a queen. By the end of it Yue was in dark green wool, with her hair bound up in bronze pins, and a hundred small unmatched things were rattling around inside her skull. She went down the corridor in a kind of dream. The guard at the outer door, a boy with a long nose and a very strained face, stepped aside with the relief of a man who has been holding up a ceiling.

Yuan Shi was waiting in the antechamber, with six priests at his back.

He was exactly as he had been in the hall of the court, tall, grave, with the close grey beard and the mild absorbed eyes of a scholar who has found an error in the world. Close up she saw more. There were lines in his face that had been drawn by long vigils, and a fine pale scar across one knuckle, and the shadows under his eyes were the color of old bruises. He did not look like a man who ordered thirty hostages onto a pit. He looked like a man who had been awake all night worrying about the weather.

"Your Majesty." He bowed. "Forgive my intrusion. I come with the greatest reluctance."

"You come with six priests."

"I come with witnesses, as the law requires." He did not smile. "A woman called Yaoniang, bondservant of the Geng, consecrated to the temple acre, has gone missing from the east holding. A kitchen boy saw her carried through your gate in the night. She is the property of the goddess. I ask that she be returned to her field, with such care as her condition permits. I have brought a litter."

Consecrated, said a small cold voice at the back of Yue's skull. Once.

"She is in no state to be moved," said Yue.

"I am sure she is receiving every kindness. But she belongs to the temple, Majesty. A consecrated person must be returned. To retain her would be a theft from the goddess, and I fear—" he hesitated, with what looked like genuine pain, "—that the court would not look kindly on a queen who began her reign by robbing a god. She is consecrated, and she must go back."

Twice.

Yue took a very slow breath.

She did not know what she meant to say. She had spent half a month learning how to hold a cup, and she knew a great many ways of pouring water, and not one of them applied. Behind her, down the corridor, she could hear the faint thud of Ling's slippers retreating toward the kitchens. Somewhere beyond the wall a long way off, in the great hall, a gong began to beat for the morning audience.

And then she thought, quite clearly: He didn't say that to me. He said it to the six priests.

They were witnesses. They would carry it back to the Office and set it down as having been said before the queen. By the third time, the matter would be settled by law, and nothing she did would unsettle it. Yuan Shi was not a bully. He was a man who followed the rules, and the rules, as far as she could tell, were on his side.

Then, thought Yue, we had better go to someone who writes them.

"The morning audience has begun," she said. "I think the king should hear this."


* * *

She had never entered the great hall except by the dais, at the king's side, in a robe of state. She walked in alone, in green wool, through a side door, with a Master of Sacrifices and six priests behind her and a ripple of astonishment spreading out across the mats like a stone dropped in a pond.

It was a very long hall. It was lined with the nobility of Shang, who had been drinking until dawn and now sat in rows with the pallid, dazed, vaguely apologetic look of men trying to remember where they had left their dignity. Lord Geng, near the front, appeared to be holding his head on with both hands. At the far end, on the dais, sat the king.

Di Xin was in his ordinary robe, with his hair tied back and ink on his hands. He had not slept. He looked at her as she came in, once, across the long length of the hall. It was an odd, level, arrested look, as though she had done something he had hoped for and hadn't dared to expect.

"Great King," said Yuan Shi, bowing, at her elbow. "I beg your pardon. I am here on the matter of a consecrated woman. The queen has taken her into her house."

"Has she?" said Di Xin, mildly. "How enterprising."

"The woman is the property of the temple," said Lord Geng, rising with the effort of a man climbing out of a well, "and of my house. She was stolen from my east holding. I demand her return and compensation. If the queen, in her innocence, has been misled, I am prepared to forgive it. But the principle..."

"She was beaten," said Yue.

Every head in the hall turned.

She had not planned to say it. It came out of her at a volume she'd never used, flat and carrying, in the voice of the little white fox who had once stood at the mouth of an ice cave in the north and told a hunting party to go home. Don't use that, she told herself. Don't use that voice. It was too late.

"A Geng steward beat her. In the night. Within the last day. I have seen what he did to her face. Her ribs are cracked. She was left at my kitchen gate because she thought a queen might have a door." She heard her own voice shake and let it. "If she was property of the goddess, then the goddess's property has been abused by a steward of the Geng. I'd like to know what the law says about that."

A silence.

Lord Geng's face went the color of old tallow. He said, with great dignity, "A servant may be disciplined."

"To the edge of death?"

"The goddess's acre is a place of discipline—"

"Lord Geng," said Di Xin.

It was not loud. It cut through the hall like a blade through silk. The great lord stopped.

"The queen asks a question." The king had risen, slowly, from the high seat. He came down the three steps of the dais one at a time, with the unhurried gait of a man who has just remembered something amusing. "I find I am curious about the answer. Master Yuan. You are the keeper of the temple law. Tell me, in what condition must an offering be, to be accepted by the goddess?"

Yuan Shi's brows drew together. "Great King?"

"The laws of the altar. You've recited them to me since I was a boy. A beast must be unblemished. A grain-offering must be sound. A vessel must have no crack." He had reached the foot of the dais, and now he stood with his hands clasped behind him, as though discussing the weather. "What of a person consecrated to the goddess's field?"

There was a pause. The old priest's face was perfectly still, but his eyes moved, once, to the queen, and back. He was a good man, Yue thought. He was a good, honest, careful man, and he had just seen the trap and was trying to decide whether to walk into it.

"The law applies equally," he said at last, slowly. "A consecrated person must be whole. In body, and in mind."

"Whole," said Di Xin. "Unblemished."

"Yes."

"And a woman with a closed eye, and two broken ribs, and the marks of a steward's rod?"

"Is blemished." The word dropped like a stone. "Is, for the time being, not fit to be consecrated. Nor to be given to the altar. Nor to be returned to the field as an offering."

"Then she is not, at present, the goddess's."

"No, Great King. She is, for the present, no one's." Yuan Shi bowed his head. There was something in the line of his shoulders that Yue could not name. It was not defeat. It was the weariness of a man who has watched the ground shift beneath him and knows that the law that has held up his whole life has just been used to drive a nail in it. "Until she is healed."

"How long does it take a rib to knit?"

"A month," said Yue, before she could stop herself. "I'm told."

"A month." Di Xin's eyes met hers, and for an instant something flashed in them, very bright and very fast, like a fish turning under the ice. "Then for a month, the woman Yaoniang is under the protection of the queen. And so is any person of that holding, or any other, who comes to her door, blemished, as the law defines it. Lord Geng, I'd be obliged if you would send word to your east holding that the king is very interested in the state of its fields. I'll want the steward's name. And the clerk's."

"Great King." The lord's voice cracked. "That is a very irregular—"

"I am a very irregular king. Ask anyone." He turned to the hall. "The queen has taken in a woman who was beaten half to death on an estate under my protection. She did it without being asked, and in defiance, I gather, of three of our oldest customs. I'd like it known that she has my full approval." A pause. "And that anyone who wishes to dispute it may do so to my face."

Nobody did.

Somewhere at the back of the hall a pale, composed figure in grey, with a smile like a freshly cut peach, had put one hand to her mouth. Yue caught a glimpse of it as she turned. It might have been a gesture of delight. It might equally have been a way of concealing a bared tooth.


* * *

"You didn't tell me you were going to do that," said Di Xin.

They were standing on a long gallery on the north side of the palace, under a roof of dark timber, overlooking an empty court. The audience was over. The nobles had fled to their houses. The wind came up from the river with a smell of mud and smoke, and the sun, struggling clear of the clouds, laid a long pale stripe across the flagstones.

"I didn't know I was going to do that." Yue's hands, which had been steady throughout, had begun to shake. She folded them into her sleeves. "I thought I'd be arrested."

"For stealing a goddess's field hand?"

"For defying a priest."

"You'd have to try a good deal harder." The king was leaning on the railing with his chin on his arm. He looked, in the thin light, exhausted and very much alive. "I've been defying priests for three years. I've found it's mostly a matter of choosing the right clause."

"Was it a trap?"

"It was a loophole. A trap is when you know who's going to fall into it." He glanced at her sideways. "I didn't know it would be you. I'd been hoping, I think, for somebody who might."

She wanted to ask what he meant. She was afraid to. The wind pulled at her hair, and at the pins, and she put up a hand to hold them.

"A month," she said instead. "It isn't long. When the rib's mended, he can come back, and say she's whole, and take her."

"Yes."

"Then what?"

"Then you'll need a place." He said it simply, as a man states a fact he's been carrying around for some time. "A place with a wall and a lock, and a long enough roof that a priest can't argue it's the queen's own bedroom. A house that's the king's, and not the Geng's, and not the temple's, where a woman can say I'm here and nobody can say she isn't." He turned his head. "You'll want to bring more than one. They'll come. You know that. Having opened a door, you can't shut it."

"I know." She hadn't known. It was only now, saying it aloud, that she understood that she'd known all along.

"There's an empty granary," said Di Xin, "in the south ward. Left over from my father's time. It's not much. It leaks, and it smells of rats, and it's been sitting there for twelve years with nobody in it." He straightened. "I don't suppose you'd care to look at it?"
