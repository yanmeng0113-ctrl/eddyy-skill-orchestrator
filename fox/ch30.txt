Chapter Thirty: The Face That Would Not Come Off

It was a very simple thing to take off a face.

Yue had done it a thousand times. She had done it in the dark and in a hurry, in cellars and carriages and once, memorably, inside a rain-barrel. It required nothing but a certain turning inward, a small, quiet, deliberate letting, like opening a hand. The borrowed thing slid away, and her own lay underneath, cool and a little strange, like a bed that has been waited in.

She sat on the edge of the platform in the queen's wing, an hour before midnight, with a lamp, a basin of water and a polished bronze mirror, and let go.

Nothing happened.

She frowned. She shut her eyes. She breathed in, slowly, as she'd been taught on a cold hillside four hundred years ago, by a voice like water under ice, and turned the whole of her attention inward to the place behind her eyes where the face lived. It was there. She could feel it, warm and close and perfectly fitted: the stubborn mouth, the straight nose, the faint line between the brows. She took hold of it, as one takes hold of the edge of a garment, and pulled.

It did not move.

It was not that it resisted. That would have been easier. It was only that, where there should have been a seam, there was nothing at all, no join, no edge, no place to get a fingernail under. It was smooth and whole and rooted, as if it had grown there. She pulled harder. She felt a faint hot twinge between her eyes, and a sort of sick lurch, like putting out a foot for a stair that isn't there.

She opened her eyes and looked in the mirror.

Fuji looked back.

It was a perfect likeness. It was also, now, unmistakably, the only face she had. She raised a hand to her cheek, and the girl in the bronze mirror raised hers. The skin was warm. The mouth, when she opened it, did not belong to her. The eyes, which ought to have been amber, were a dull clear brown.

Oh, she thought. Oh, no.


* * *

"It's pinned," said Di Xin.

He knelt in front of her on the matting with his hands on her shoulders, looking into her face. It was an odd thing, to be looked at like that. He was looking at her, and at Fuji, and he did not seem to be able to tell which. His own face was very pale.

"I don't understand."

"Nor do I." Yue's voice came out thin and strange, in a girl's voice that was not hers. "I've done it a thousand times. It's the easiest thing in the world. And I can feel it, it's just there, and it won't come, it's like there's a nail through it." She swallowed. "Somebody's fixed it. From outside."

"The goddess."

"Who else?"

He did not say anything. His thumbs moved against her collarbones. She saw him think it through, quickly, in the silence, the way he thought through everything, and saw the arithmetic come out the same way each time.

"She heard," said Yue. "Someone told her. About the plan."

"The pheasant."

"Of course the pheasant." She laughed, a high cracked sound. "Three days. She had three days to find out, and she's found out in one. She's good at this. She's so good at it." Her hands were shaking. "If I can't take it off, Fuji can't stand up. There'd be two of her. The Eye would look at the dais and see a fox in a girl's skin, and look at the floor and see the girl, and then he'd know. We've been outplayed."

"There's still time."

"For what? I can't take it off."

"We'll find a way."

"It's not a thing you can find a way around, Di Xin." Her voice rose. "It's a goddess. She's made the mountains. She's nailed a face to my head, like a mark burned into a gatepost, and she's laughing at me, somewhere, in a cold clay hall, and I can feel her looking."

"Then we'll go to her."

"And say what?"

"That she's cruel." He said it very quietly. "That she's being cruel, and she knows it. That she's afraid, and that I understand it, and that I will give her everything I can, all but the one thing. I'll tell her to her face. I'll tell her I'd give my life if it would feed her." His hands tightened. "But I will not feed her the girl in my arms."

She stared at him. And then, because there was nothing else to do, because she was a creature of fur and flame who had learned in four months what it was to be loved, she put her borrowed face against his shoulder and began to shake.


* * *

They tried everything.

It was the next day, between the morning audience and dusk, in the Counting Room with the door barred and the shutters closed, and it was the most undignified afternoon of Yue's long life. Qingpu came, with a basket and an expression of weary resignation, and applied a salve that smelled of vinegar and wet dog. The face did not so much as itch. Su Hu, who had been told what was needed and had gone a little grey, proposed a hot cloth. Di Xin, who had read of such things in the archives, tried a recitation of the names of Nüwa's seven daughters, backwards, in the old tongue, while holding a bronze bowl of salt water to her brow. She sneezed. The face stayed where it was.

"It's like trying to unpick a scar," said Qingpu, wiping her hands. "I've never seen anything so thoroughly set. If I had to say, I'd say it was done by someone who meant it."

"She means everything she does," said Yue.

"Aye. That's what makes her a goddess." The healer looked at her thoughtfully. "There's one person who might undo it. I'd not like to say who."

"A broker," said Di Xin slowly.

Nobody answered. It was a thing none of them wished to say aloud. But Yue, sitting on the cot in her borrowed face with a salt-wet cloth on her forehead, thought of a freckled man on a wall, with a persimmon in his hand, saying, quite lightly: When you find yourself short of a tail, or of a favor, or of a rare and valuable silence, you'll know where to look.

She said nothing. She found that she was very much afraid of the next part of the sentence.


* * *

Fuji came out of prison the next night.

It had been done quickly and without ceremony. Su Hu's petition had been heard at the morning audience, in front of Wen Zhong and a thin scatter of nobles, and granted by the king with a show of boredom. The maid called Ling, said the Marquis, was a loyal servant of his household, who would not harm a child. The captain of the watch had been unable to produce a single witness. The three weeping women had been found in a ditch by the dyers' lane, hours after the arrest, very drunk, with a string of cowries each. They had remembered nothing. Ling was released into the Marquis's custody. And at dusk, in a closed litter, accompanied by two guards and a very quiet old man, she was brought to the king's private garden, and set down at the door of the Counting Room.

Yue was waiting for her.

She had planned something to say. She had practiced it for a day and a half. It had a number of clauses, and an apology in the middle, and a joke at the end. She stood in the doorway in a red robe, with a plain borrowed face, as the girl who owned it came up the steps in a rough brown coat with her hair coming down.

She had lost weight. There were shadows under her eyes. A fresh bruise, yellowing, ran along one wrist where the hemp had chafed. She stopped on the top step and looked at the woman in the door. They looked at one another, the two of them, with the same mouth, the same nose, the same straight, tired, level brows.

"It's a very good face," said Fuji.

"I'm so sorry. It won't come off."

"I know. Father told me." She came up the last step. She put out a hand, hesitantly, and touched Yue's cheek, as one touches a thing on a market stall that one has been told not to handle. "Is it strange? Having it on?"

"It's like being someone else's coat."

"It was always yours, I think." Fuji's voice shook. "It's looked much better since you've been wearing it."

"Fuji—"

"I counted two hundred and sixteen stones," said Fuji. "In the east wall. Did anyone tell you? There was a man next door called Tan who'd been inside for eleven years, and he said that in a place like that you either learn to count or learn to scream. I counted." She was crying and laughing at once. "And every night I thought, she's going to do something stupid. She'll walk into the prison in a red robe and say 'I'm the queen, give her back.' And I've been so frightened, and so angry, and I've missed you, and—"

She did not finish. Yue had pulled her into her arms, and for a time neither of them said anything at all.

They stood there in the lamplight, the queen and the maid, two identical figures in the doorway of the Counting Room, one in red and one in brown, with their faces pressed together, indistinguishable. It was the strangest embrace that Chaoge had ever seen. It was witnessed by nobody, which was just as well.


* * *

On the morning of the third day, Hu Ximei came to the door of the queen's wing.

She did not knock. She stood in the corridor, in plain grey, with a lacquer tray in her hands, and a look of warm concern. A few feet behind her, two guards from the king's own household watched with the tight, ugly stillness of men who would very much like to be told they might remove a person's head.

"Cousin," she said. "You haven't eaten."

"I'm not hungry."

"I brought you something. Plum wine, and a little cake." She set the tray down. It was set with the care of a person arranging an offering. "I thought you might like to say goodbye to it, you know. It's a nice cake. It's from the kitchen at the Hill of Nüwa."

Yue said nothing.

"Mother's very sorry." Hu Ximei's gold eyes were soft, and sad, and absolutely sincere. "She did not enjoy it. She wanted me to tell you that. She doesn't like doing a thing like that to a child she is fond of. But she was afraid, little lamp. She's so very old, and so very afraid. And the plan was so clever." She lifted her hands, helplessly. "You'd have got away with it. You know that? I almost admired it."

"Take it off," said Yue, in Fuji's voice.

"I can't. It's not my work."

"Then tell her."

"Tell her what?"

"That I'm not going to run." She stood. It was hard to do it with the borrowed legs. "Tell her that I'll go to the hall at noon, in this face, and I'll stand there, and let the Eye do what it likes. If she wants me dead, she can have it. I'm not going to hide behind Fuji. I won't have it said that I hid behind a girl."

Something moved in the pheasant's face.

It was very slight. It might have been annoyance. It might, very faintly, have been something else.

"Oh, little fool," she said. And it was not the sweet voice at all. It was a rough, thin, tired voice, old as a river-stone, and for one moment the lovely face was only a mask on something that had been waiting for a very long time. "She said you'd say that."

She turned. She walked away down the corridor, with her grey skirts swaying, and the guards shrank from her as she passed.

A bronze gong began to sound in the distance. It was the signal for the middle of the morning. It would sound again, and again. Then midday.

Yue went back into the room and sat down on the platform. Beside the lamp, on the lacquered table, lay the little cake. She looked at it for some time. Then she picked up the mirror, and looked into it, one last time.

In the polished bronze, a plain girl with a stubborn mouth looked back at her, very steadily, with brown eyes that were not hers.

Noon, she thought. And I'm still wearing someone else's face.
