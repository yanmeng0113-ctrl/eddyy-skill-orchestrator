Chapter Thirty-Seven: The Longest Night

The knife came at sunset, and it was lying on her pillow when she went to look for it.

Yue had not expected it to be beautiful. It was a thin leaf-shaped blade of bronze, no longer than her hand, with a handle wrapped in black cord and nine small notches filed along the spine, five of them bright and four of them dull. It was cold as a pond at dawn. She held it in her palm and turned it, and the light of the lamp slid down it like water.

Five and four, she thought. That's what she thinks of me. A tally.

She laid it down on the bed again, and sat beside it, and put her hands between her knees. It was the shortest day of the year, and the sun had gone down early behind the roofs of Chaoge, like a cowrie dropped through a crack. In the South Court, a long way off, somebody was beating a drum, very slowly, for the turning of the year. She could hear a child singing the song of the white fox, which had not existed a month ago and now had eleven verses.

"I made supper," said Fuji, from the door.

"I can't."

"I thought you'd say that. I made it anyway." She came in with a bowl in either hand and set one down on the bench. She was in her plain grey gown, with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and a smudge of ash on her cheek. She sat on the floor beside the bed, quite as if it were any other evening, and began to eat. After a while, Yue took the other bowl. It was millet and a little dried pork. It tasted of nothing at all.

"What if I don't come back?" said Yue.

"Then I'll have supper anyway." Fuji did not look up. "I've a great gift for it. It's one of the only things I'm good at. I wish you'd consider it a compliment."

"Fuji—"

"No. I've said what I'm going to say. I said it in the cell, I said it in the garden, I said it to the wall of the Counting Room." She put down her spoon. Her mouth was perfectly steady. "I will not tell you what to do tonight. I have been told what to do all my life, and I've noticed that it only makes one good at obeying." She felt in her sleeve. "I brought you a thing."

It was a small grey stone, flat and round, worn smooth at the edges. Yue turned it over in her fingers.

"It's the two hundred and sixteenth," said Fuji. "From the cell. I took it with me, when they let me out. I thought it would be a useful thing to keep. It's no good for anything. It's only a stone that I got through a hard week by counting."

Yue closed her hand on it. It was warm from the girl's sleeve.

"If you can't think what to do," said Fuji, "count something. And then do whatever you'd have done anyway."


* * *

The altar of the year stood in the inner court, to the south of the great hall, on a round platform of rammed earth faced with river stones and ringed by nine bronze tripods. Nobody had lit them. In the old days, the old king would have had a hundred torches. Di Xin had said that it was a waste of oil.

She came into the court in the second watch of the night, alone, with the knife in her left hand, held low and out of the way along her thigh, as one carries a thing one is ashamed of. It was bitterly cold. The sky above the roofs was a great blue-black bowl, scattered with stars so thick and so hard that they looked like frost on a pond, and there was no moon at all. The flagstones had frozen. Her breath went up in front of her face in a single pale plume.

The pheasant was waiting at the foot of the steps.

She wore a gown the color of a new peach, trimmed with marten, and her hands were folded at her waist, and she looked, in the starlight, so entirely lovely and so entirely at her ease that Yue had a moment of purely animal horror. She's dressed for it, she thought. She's dressed for a wedding.

"Little sister." Hu Ximei's gold eyes shone. "I wasn't sure you'd come."

"Did you expect me to run?"

"I expected you to dither, which isn't quite the same." She tilted her lovely head. "But you're here. You've the knife. You look quite well, you know, in your own face. White suits you." She came a pace closer, and her voice dropped, warm and conspiratorial. "I should like you to know that Mother's very pleased with you. She said so. You've been such a clever girl. You've been the only one of us with the courage to play the long game. She's going to be so kind."

"Go away, Hu."

"Oh, I can't. I'm to witness." Her smile did not alter. "It's quite a quick business, you know. One stroke. Under the ribs, upward, at the angle I showed you. He'll scarcely feel it. And afterwards you've only to put your hand on the stone and say her name, and she'll take your lamp from the shelf and trim it, and light it, and you'll have a hundred years of peace." A pause. "And if you can't, dear, I'll do it for you. I'd rather you didn't make me."

She said it lightly. It was the lightness that made Yue's skin crawl.

"I'll be at the top of the steps," said Yue. "I'd like you to stay here."

"Of course." The pheasant bowed, with perfect grace. "I'm only the witness."


* * *

He came at midnight.

She heard him before she saw him: a footfall on frozen stone, a single pair, steady, unhurried. She stood on the platform in the middle of the ring of cold tripods, with her back to the altar stone and the black knife hanging at her side, and watched him come across the court. He wore a plain undyed robe, the kind a man wears to bed. He had left off the crown. He carried no lamp, and no sword, and no ledger, and nothing at all in his hands. They hung empty at his sides, open, with the fingers a little curved, the hands of a man who has put down everything that he was carrying and has just discovered that it was heavy.

He stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Forty-one," said Di Xin.

"What?"

"Paces from the Counting Room. I counted." The ghost of a smile touched his mouth. "It seemed the sort of evening on which one ought to know."

"Di Xin."

"Don't." He said it very gently. "I know what you're going to say. I've spent three weeks working out what you were going to say, and I find I've got every word of it wrong, so I'd rather you didn't spoil it." He drew a breath of the freezing air. It smoked. "May I come up?"

She nodded. She did not trust herself to speak.

He climbed the steps. He did it slowly, with great care, as a man climbs a stair in the dark, and he stopped three paces away from her, on the edge of the stone. She could see his face quite clearly in the starlight. It was thin, and tired, and very pale, with the fine shadows of sleeplessness under his eyes. He was looking at her as he had looked at her on the roof of the Terrace on the night of the rite, with the whole of his attention, as if she were a column that came out right.

"Here is what I've brought," he said. He held out his hands. "Nothing. I thought about a sword. I thought about a hundred guards and a ring of bronze, and the Preceptor's horse, and a priest from the Office with a name for the goddess. I thought about all of it, and every time I came back to the same thing." His voice was quite steady. "You've been somebody's lamp for four hundred years, Yue. A tool. You were handed a knife, and told to use it, and nobody asked. I won't be the one who asks you with a spear at your back. I'd like it if, in all of this, there were one person who had the choice."

"That's the cruelest thing that anybody's ever done to me."

"I know." His mouth twisted. "I'm sorry. It's the only honest thing I have."

She lifted the knife.

She did not decide to do it. It was a thing her arm did, a thing it had been trained for, as it had been trained for the sun and the rain. The black blade came up through the frozen air, and the star-glint ran along it, and she set the point of it, very lightly, in the hollow of his breastbone, on the thin undyed cloth, over the quick small beat of his heart.

He did not move. He did not flinch. She felt him breathe in, against the point, once.

"Now, sister," said Hu Ximei, from the foot of the steps. It was barely a whisper. "Quickly."

"Is this the part," said Di Xin, "where I say a last word? I've prepared three. I've discarded all of them."

"Be quiet."

"Yes. I thought you might say that."

Her hand was shaking. The point trembled against his chest, a hair's-breadth, a hair's-breadth, and she saw it make a tiny dimple in the cloth. She could feel her own pulse in her wrist. She could feel the lamp, away on its cold hill, with its five bright wicks and its four dead ones. She could feel the goddess's hand, patient and gentle and enormous, resting over her own.

Five and four, she thought. A tally.

She thought of a cave, and of a man with a pipa who had said, You'll need a name. She thought of a plain girl in a post-station, pouring wine. She thought of a small flat stone, warm from a sleeve, which she was holding, she realized, in her other hand, so tightly that it had left a mark.

Count something, said Fuji, in her head. And then do whatever you'd have done anyway.

She counted the stars. There were a great many. She stopped at nine.

Then she took the knife from his chest.

She did it slowly, as one lifts a cup that is too full. She stepped back. She looked at the black blade in her hand, with its nine small notches, and then she bent, and set it down on the altar stone, very precisely, with the point toward the sky, as a person lays down a thing she has finished carrying.

"No," said Yue.

It was a very small word. It fell on the frozen court like a pebble dropped down a well.

"No," she said again. "I won't. I don't care what she does to me. I'm not going to be her hand." Her teeth were chattering, with cold or with something else. "I'd rather be nobody. I'd rather be a fox. I'm not—I don't—" She put her fist, the one with the stone in it, against her mouth. "I'm so tired of being useful."

Di Xin said nothing at all. He took a step toward her, and then another, and put his arms around her, and she turned her face into the cold undyed cloth over his heart, and the world, for a long moment, was no larger than the space between them.

"Well," said a clear cool voice below. "That's tiresome."

Hu Ximei was on the steps. She had come up them without a sound. She had lost the lovely, easy smile entirely, and her face under the starlight was fine and narrow and peculiarly blank, as a face is when the person behind it has stopped troubling to arrange it. A feathered thing moved in her sleeve. Nine small dark heads rose out of her collar, nine slim necks swaying, and nine gold pairs of eyes turned together toward the stone.

"I did say I would," said the pheasant, and reached for the knife.

She never touched it.

It began with the tripods. They were cast bronze, nine of them, heavy as oxen, standing in a ring in the frozen court. Each of them gave out, in the same instant, a long low singing note, like a finger drawn round the rim of a bowl. The frost on the stones turned white. The note rose. The starlight, which had been hard and bright and cold, flickered, and thinned, and went out in a long wave from east to west, as though a hand were drawing a cloth across the sky.

Hu Ximei's gown went silver at the hem. She stood quite still with her hand an inch from the knife. Her long white face had turned upward. Every one of her nine heads, in turn, bent.

"Mother," she said.

There was no wind. There was no sound at all. And over the roofs of Chaoge, on the longest night of the year, in a sky that had no more stars in it, something large and slow and grave began to come down, like a lamp being carried through a dark house.
