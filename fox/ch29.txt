Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Heavenly Eye

Wen Zhong came to the Counting Room at the second watch, alone, with a lantern, and sat down on the bench where Yue had once failed to read the character for goose.

Di Xin had not sent for him. He had not expected him. But he had known, on some level below the reach of arithmetic, that it would be tonight, and so he had dismissed the clerks at dusk and sat up with the lamp turned low and the door unbarred. When the old man appeared on the threshold, with the dust of the road still on his shoulders, he got to his feet and bowed. He did not know why. It was not a thing a king did.

"Sit, boy," said the Grand Preceptor. "You look like a man who's been sitting all day."

"I haven't been sitting. I've been standing in the great hall pretending to be calm."

"I noticed. It was very good." Wen Zhong set the lantern on the table. It threw a long, wavering light over the shelves, the ink-stained boards, the cot in the corner with a rumpled cloak on it. His eyes took it all in, one thing after another, the way a soldier looks over a camp. "You've made this room very comfortable."

"It's a working room."

"It's a room somebody lives in." The old man's mouth twitched. "I held you on my knee in this palace, when you were five and your father was away at the western wars. You were extremely solemn. You asked me how many men there were in the northern army, and when I told you, you asked how they were fed." He shook his head. "I thought: this child will either be a very good king or a very bad accountant. I see I was right about both."

Di Xin sat down. He found that his hands were not quite steady, and put them between his knees.

"You're going to tell me you don't want to do this."

"I don't. I'm going to do it anyway. I wanted you to hear that I don't." The Preceptor rubbed his face. In the lamplight he looked every day of his sixty years, and a number besides. "I've been your father's servant and yours for forty years, and I have never once been afraid of anything I had to look at. I am afraid of this."

"Of what?"

"Of her." The grey eyes were very steady. "Not of what she is. I've an idea of that already, and I find I'm not as alarmed as I ought to be. I'm afraid of what I'll feel when I have to say it."

Di Xin said nothing.

"Su Hu knelt to her," said Wen Zhong softly. "A Marquis of the first rank, who has not knelt to a living soul since the day he was invested. He said in front of the whole court that she was not his daughter, and then he kissed the floor and called her queen. I've known the man twenty years. He cannot be bought. He cannot be frightened into a lie. He has the courage of a rabbit, and the integrity of a stone. So I ask myself: what did he see?"

"A friend of his daughter's," said Di Xin.

It came out before he had decided to say it. He saw the old man's eyes narrow, very slightly.

"That's an odd answer."

"It's the truth."

"I'm sure it is. It's a very odd truth." Wen Zhong sat back. "I'll tell you what I know, boy, and then I'll tell you what I don't. I know that your wife has the face of a girl called Su Fuji. I know that the girl called Su Fuji, if she was ever here, was a plain, clever creature who could add. I know the woman on your dais has her face and not her manner. I know a good many other small things, about rain, and a fire, and a market. And I know that last night, when the door was shut and the lamp was out, I put my hand over my eye, and listened, and a very small voice said fox."

The lamp seemed to burn lower.

"It was only a whisper," said the Preceptor. "I am a soldier, not a seer. The Eye does not speak to me except when it is open. But it has a way of leaking, in the dark. It told me, years ago, about a barley field at the passes, and a night patrol that turned out to be eleven spies. It's never wrong." He paused. "It has not told me whether the thing is wicked."

"She isn't."

"You say so."

"I have known a great many people in my life, Preceptor. I have counted their sins, in my own ledgers, in my own hand. She is the first one who has never once lied to me." He met the old man's gaze. "She was sent to destroy me. She told me so herself. And she did not."

For a long moment the Grand Preceptor looked at him. Then something went out of the stern weathered face, some long-braced thing, and left it very tired and very kind.

"Ah, child," he said. "You've fallen in love with her."

"Yes."

"I'm very sorry."

"So am I." Di Xin smiled, crookedly. "It's been the best month of my life."

Wen Zhong laughed. It was a short, rusty, startled sound, and it broke off, and he rubbed his eyes with the back of his wrist. When he lowered his hand, his voice was perfectly even.

"Three days," he said. "At noon. In the great hall. Before the whole court. I'll open the Eye, and I'll look at her, and I'll say what I see." He rose. "I'll tell you something else, since you've been honest. I haven't the least idea what I'll do after that. I have a duty to Shang, and a duty to the king. I've never before found they pointed in different directions." He took up the lantern. "I'd like to be wrong, boy. I should so much like to be wrong."

He went out. His footsteps faded down the corridor, slow, even, and a little stooped.

Di Xin sat for a long time in the dark.


* * *

"Three days," said Yue.

"Three days."

"We've got three days to turn me into somebody who isn't a fox."

"Yes."

She had come in at the third watch, silently, through the door in the shelves, and sat down on the cot with her knees drawn up. She was not wearing the queen's face. She had let it go in the corridor. It lay folded in her mind like a garment, soft and slightly warm. Her own face, white and sharp and amber-eyed, looked out from under her loosened hair with an expression of enormous fatigue. Beside her, in the high carved chair, sat the Marquis of Zhongzhou.

He had been released into the king's custody that afternoon, under Wen Zhong's seal, with the proviso that he not leave the palace. He had spent three days on horseback and two in a cell. His hands, on the arms of the chair, were not quite still. But he sat very straight. He had not once looked away from the white-haired girl on the cot.

"I beg your pardon," he said, at last, in his dry careful voice. "It's very rude to stare. I find I can't help it. You've got my daughter's expression."

"Which one?"

"The one she wears when she's about to be reasonable."

Yue made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

"I've told you the plan," said Di Xin. "Let me tell it again, in order, so that nobody can say they didn't understand." He stood. It was easier to think on his feet. "Tomorrow, at the morning audience, the Marquis will petition the king for the release of his maid. He'll say he's been to the prison and recognized her as a servant of his household, a girl called Ling. He will say that she's no kidnapper, and that she's a loyal girl who would never harm a child. I'll agree to hear her in open court. The day after, at dawn, she'll be brought before the nobles."

"And she'll say her name," said Su Hu.

"She'll say her name. In front of the court. In the old script, if she wishes, so that Wen Zhong himself can read it." Di Xin paused. "She'll say: I am Su Fuji, daughter of the Marquis of Zhongzhou. I left my father's house in the autumn. I was nearly killed at Enzhou by a servant of the Marquis's enemies. I have been in hiding in the south ward since, in a maid's coat, because I did not know whom to trust." He said it quickly, like a clerk reading a figure. "She'll tell them that the woman on the dais is not an impostor. She's a fox in the king's service. A white fox, a pet, a thing that came to the palace of its own accord. Bi Gan has agreed to swear he has seen it. Wen Zhong will examine the Lady Su, the true one, and he'll see a girl who counts. He'll see a perfectly ordinary human woman."

"And the queen?" said Yue.

"There is no queen. There's a girl in a red robe who was released from prison, and a fox in the king's sleeve."

She was silent.

"It's the only way," said Su Hu, gently. "We've turned it over a hundred ways. The Eye sees what a person is. It sees through a face. If my daughter sits on the dais, in her own, he'll see a girl. If you hide, as an animal, in the one place nobody thinks to look, he'll see a pet." His voice cracked slightly. "You'll be given a collar. I do apologize."

"I've worn worse," said Yue.

"And then?" said Di Xin. "After. If he clears her. If the Preceptor tells the court that the queen of Shang is a woman of flesh and bone and nothing more, then the rumors are dead, and the fisherman has nothing to put in front of the Zhou. It buys us a few weeks." He turned. "It buys us until the solstice."

The solstice. Nobody said it. It lay on the table, in the lamplight, like a small cold stone.

Yue got up from the cot.

She walked across the floor to the table. She was shaking a little, and she did not hide it. She stopped in front of the Marquis and looked at him for a long moment, at the red-rimmed eyes and the grey beard and the scrape on his cheek, and then, slowly, she bowed. It was not the queen's bow. It was a small, awkward, very young bow, the bow of someone who has not practiced it.

"I'm sorry I wore her," she said. "I'd have asked, if there'd been time."

"Child," said Su Hu.

"I'll give it back. I promise." She lifted her head. "I'll give her back her face, and her name, and her place. All of it. I only borrowed it for a while."

The Marquis of Zhongzhou was crying again. He did not hide it. He rose, with a certain stiffness, and did a thing that he had never done to anyone in his life: he took both her hands in his, and held them, and said, in a perfectly steady voice, "You'll forgive me if I say that I've never been so grateful to a thief."


* * *

The pheasant had been waiting on the roof of the Hill of Nüwa for an hour.

She did not mind. She liked the roof. It was a high, quiet, cold place, with a view of the whole misty valley, and a view, through the crack in the plaster beside the door, of the hall within. The goddess sat on her plinth in the dark. The lamp with its six wicks burned before her, small and pale and still.

Hu Ximei dropped from the ridge and landed on the flagstones as a woman in grey. She smoothed her skirts. She had come in the dusk, down the long road, on foot, past the sleeping shrines and the dark pines, and she was perfectly composed.

"Mother."

"Daughter."

"I've news." She knelt. It was a graceful movement, unhurried, like the settling of a bird. "It's about the fox."

The painted eyes did not move. The voice, when it came, was soft and cold and entirely without surprise.

"Tell me."

"They're going to change the faces." Hu Ximei lifted her head. In the lamplight her smile was bright, and tender, and without the least trace of regret. "In three days. Before the Eye. The maid will stand up and give her name. The fox will go into the king's sleeve. And the Preceptor will find, to his great relief, a perfectly ordinary girl."

There was a silence. The six flames in the lamp did not flicker.

"It is a good plan," said Nüwa, at last.

"It's very clever."

"It would work." A faint stirring in the dark, like the first thin edge of ice on a pond. "It would work perfectly. If the fox could take the face off."

Hu Ximei's smile deepened.

"But I don't think," said the goddess, very gently, "that she'll be able to."
