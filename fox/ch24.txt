Chapter Twenty-Four: Ash and Strings

The Edict of the Street took Di Xin less than a quarter-hour to write, and it was the shortest he ever composed.

From this day, within the walls of Chaoge and the district of its fields, no person shall be put to death by fire except under the seal of the king, in a place appointed by the king, after the charge has been heard before the king. Whoever kindles a pyre in any street, market, or yard in defiance of this order shall answer for the life as though he had taken it with his own hand.

He read it over once. It had no hooks. His hand, he saw, was perfectly steady, and he thought that this was a strange thing to be proud of.

"It won't stop it," said Bi Gan.

"No."

"Half the quarter was there. They saw a demon revealed. They saw rain fall from a clear sky on a burning stake and a fire that rain could not put out. They've gone home and told their wives." The old man stood in the doorway of the Counting Room with his hands in his sleeves. "You can forbid a pyre, nephew. You cannot forbid the thing that lights one."

"I know." Di Xin sealed the slip with his thumb. "But I can make it a crime. And a crime has to be explained to a magistrate."

"That is a very thin wall."

"I've built a great many thin walls. Some of them have held."

He put the edict into the hands of a courier and watched the boy run. When he turned back, Bi Gan was looking not at him, but past him, at the long carved chair by the table where a girl in a brown coat sat with her knees drawn up and her chin on her arms.

She had been there for two days.


* * *

On the first morning a maid had come to the Counting Room door with a covered dish.

"From the queen's cousin," she said, bobbing, wide-eyed. "The Lady Ximei sends it with her deepest sympathy. For the loss of the queen's friend."

Di Xin had taken the dish from her himself. Under the lid lay a small jar of plum wine, tied with a bit of blue silk, and beside it a single bamboo slip in a neat hand. Such a pity about the music. I'd hoped to hear her play at the palace. She was so very fond of the goose song. He had read it twice, standing in the corridor, while the maid waited. Then he had returned the jar to her hands and said, quite gently, that the queen was resting and wished to be left alone, and that he would be obliged if the cousin would extend the same courtesy.

He had not told Yue. He was not sure he ever would.

From behind the east chamber wall, for the first time in weeks, there had been no humming at all.


* * *

She did not weep. He had expected it, and he could have borne it. Weeping was a thing one could sit beside. He could have put a hand on her back, and said nothing, and waited for it to pass.

She sat very still instead, with her white hair down and her eyes open, and looked at nothing. She ate when he put bread in her hand. She drank water. When Fuji came, in the evenings, grey with fatigue from the South Court, she let herself be held, and she held on, hard, and said no word. Her six tails lay along the floor behind the chair, so faint that he had to turn his head to see them. They did not move.

It was the stillness that frightened him. He had seen animals do it. A bird in a trap, when it stops struggling.

On the second evening he brought her the jar.

It was a small clay one, like the others on the shelf in the Archive, stoppered with wax. Fuji had gone back to the market that morning with a basin and a broom, alone, and come home at dusk with her sleeves black to the elbow. She had not said anything. She had set the jar on the table, and put beside it a sliver of dark grained wood, no longer than a finger, singed at one end. She had found it in the gutter. The rain had almost taken it.

"I've no right to ask," said Di Xin, setting the jar by the chair. "But I'd like to put her in the Archive. With the others. If that's something you'd want."

Yue's eyes came slowly to the jar.

"She wasn't a person," she said. It was the first thing she'd said in two days. Her voice was a cracked whisper. "She was a pipa. They said so. A thing of wood and string."

"I've heard a great deal said about what a thing is." He knelt, so that their faces were level. "She sang the goose song to a crowd that had come to watch her burn, and made them laugh. I should like to meet any person who has done as much."

For a long time she said nothing.

Then she reached out, took the sliver of wood, and held it in her palm.

"Her name," she said, "was not Pipa."

He waited.

"That was just what I called her. What everyone did. She had a name, a real one, from before she was a lute. She told me once, a long time ago, when I was very small. I thought it was funny. It was the kind of name you'd give a goat." Her lip trembled. "I don't remember it. I remember laughing. I remember she said, you may not use it until I'm dead, because then it'll be too sad to laugh at."

"Yue—"

"I can't remember it." Her hand closed on the wood, and her whole body began to shake. "She's dead, and I can't remember her name."

It broke then.

It came out of her in a great, ugly, terrible sound, as if something had torn loose at the root, and he caught her as she fell forward off the chair. She was shaking so hard that he could feel it in his teeth. She clung to his robe with both fists, and cried as he had never heard anyone cry, hoarsely, helplessly, with her face against his chest, for her friend who had sung in a wine-shop for sixty years, and for the grain of wood she held, and for the name that had been given back to her and lost. He held her until the lamp burned low. He did not say it would be all right. He had stopped saying things that were not true.

He put the sliver of wood into the jar himself, afterward, with the ash. He sealed it with wax, and wrote on the shoulder, in his quick upright hand, a single word with no hooks at all. Pipa. Then, below it, smaller: who sang.


* * *

On the third night she spoke to him in the dark.

The lamp was out. They lay on the narrow cot in the corner of the Counting Room, without touching, in the cold, listening to the wind move over the roof. He had thought she was asleep. Her breathing was even. But after a long while a small clear voice said, out of the blackness:

"Di Xin."

"Yes."

"I'm going to die."

He lay very still.

"Not tonight. Not tomorrow." She said it quite simply, like a woman reciting a list. "Six tails. A goddess who's given me till midwinter. A fisherman who knows my scent. A pheasant who watches through the wall. And whatever happens at the solstice, with the knife and the altar, I don't think it ends with me still standing in your Counting Room." A breath. "I've been lying here for three days, thinking it through. I've counted every way. I've counted them over and over, the way you do. And every one of them comes out the same."

"Yue."

"I never minded before. I want you to know that. For four hundred years, if anyone had asked me if I was afraid of the lamp going out, I'd have said it was a thing that happened to lamps." Her voice cracked. "I didn't want anything. It was very restful."

"And now?"

She was silent a moment.

"I want to live," said Yue.

It was almost inaudible. It fell into the dark like a stone into a well, and there was no splash, and for a moment he thought he had imagined it. But she said it again, louder, with a kind of raw wonder, as if she were trying it on.

"I want to live. I want to knead dough and be bad at it. I want to learn the characters for granary and cat. I want to see the red star again in the winter and not be afraid of it." She had turned toward him. He could feel her breath. "I want to be in your Archive when you're old, and put the jars in order, and tell you you're doing it wrong. I want a very ordinary afternoon. I want Pipa to have a name that I can remember."

He found her hand in the dark. It was cold, and small, and clutched his with astonishing strength.

"I don't know what to do with it," she said. "I've never wanted anything. It's the most dreadful feeling. It's like being skinned."

"I know," said Di Xin.

"How?"

"Because I've been wanting you to say it," he said, "since the night I poured you a cup of wine." His voice shook, and he let it. "I haven't the least idea how to give it to you. I'm a king of a falling house with a mad goddess at my gate. But I swear to you, Yue, by every name in the Archive, that I'll spend what I have trying."

"That's not a plan."

"No."

"It's a hope."

"It's the only one I've got."

She laughed. It was tiny and wet and furious and almost joyful, and it turned into a hiccup, and she put her face against his shoulder. They lay in the dark. After a time the wind dropped, and the roof was quiet, and she slept.


* * *

He was woken at dawn by a knock.

It was not a loud one. He had been awake enough, in his life, at inconvenient hours, to know the difference between an urgent knock and a mournful one. This one was very slow. It was the knock of a man who has stood at a door for some time, rehearsing.

He got up without waking her. He drew the cloak over her shoulder, and went to the door, and opened it a crack.

Bi Gan stood in the grey light of the corridor. He was fully dressed, in an ash-white robe, with his square white beard freshly combed. His face was drawn and very still, and in his hands he carried an empty bamboo letter-case, bound with black cord, of the kind the court used for dispatches to the northern passes.

Di Xin did not need to be told where it was meant to go.

"Uncle."

"Nephew." The old man's voice was low and perfectly steady. "I came at first light because I could not bear to do it at any other hour. I should like you to hear it from me, not from the court."

"Hear what?"

Bi Gan looked past him, through the crack of the door, at the cot in the corner and the white hair spilled across the pillow, and for a moment something moved in his face, a long sorrowful complex movement, like a man putting down a very heavy thing he has carried for a very long way and not wishing to drop it.

"I am going to write to the Grand Preceptor," he said. "He is at the northern passes. I have been weighing it since the winter rite, and in the night I decided. I mean to ask him to come back to the capital to perform an examination."

"What examination?"

"Of the queen."

Di Xin said nothing. He found that he could not.

"Wen Zhong has the Heavenly Eye," said Bi Gan, very quietly. "It sees through any flesh and any glamour. It sees what a thing is. I will write to him because of the rain at the South Court, and the storm at the winter rite, and a number of small things. I will write because I love you, nephew, and I'd rather be wrong in the dark than right in front of the court." He held out the bamboo case. "It is still empty. I wanted you to see it empty, before I fill it."
