Chapter Thirty-Six: The Prince Who Said Nothing

In forty years of keeping the records of Shang, Bi Gan had learned that there are two kinds of silence, and that the court could tell them apart at fifty paces.

The first was the silence of a man who has nothing to say. That one is cheap, and the court rather liked it. The second was the silence of a man who has something to say and is choosing, with great effort, to sit on it, and that one the court feared above all things, because it never failed to cost somebody a head.

He stood at the garden gate with the cold coming up through his sandals and the moon on his beard, and he discovered that his knees had decided to give way. He locked them. He was a prince of the blood and the senior archivist of the realm, and he had no intention of sitting down in the dirt in front of a girl who had just been a fox.

"Please," said the girl. She had not moved. She stood under the persimmon with her white hair down her back and her arms wrapped tight around her ribs, and her amber eyes were fixed on him with the flat, patient terror of an animal that has already worked out how the trap closes. "Please. Say it quickly."

"Say what?"

"What I am. Whatever you have to say. Say it and let it burn." Her voice cracked. "I'd rather know."

Behind him, a lamp swung. Fuji came out of the dark with the flame cupped in her hand and put herself, without a word, between the old man and the tree, shaking, with her chin up, like a small stubborn post set against a flood.

"Prince Bi Gan," she said. "It is a covenant. A wonder seen by a mortal, and known for a wonder, takes one of her tails. She has five. I think you had better understand that before you open your mouth."

"Known," said Bi Gan. "In what sense?"

"What?"

"You say known. A wonder seen and known. I want to be exact. If I see a thing, and I do not know what it is, does it burn?"

The two of them stared at him.

"I don't," said the girl in the white hair, very slowly, "actually know."

"Then let us conduct an experiment. I am sixty. My eyes are poor. It is night, and I have been out of my bed for an hour on account of my stomach, and I find that I have seen, under a tree, in the moonlight, a young woman in a shift who appears to be taking the air." He drew a careful breath. "I have no category for what else I saw. I do not propose to make one. I should be obliged if you would tell me whether anything has happened to your tails."

The girl closed her eyes.

For a long moment nothing happened. A dry leaf scraped across the beaten earth, and stopped. And then, with a small, startled, wavering laugh, as of a person who has walked off a roof and found the ground still there, she said:

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"All five." She opened her eyes. They were wet. "I can feel all five. Oh, you old fraud."

"I have been called worse by better," said Bi Gan, and sat down in the dirt after all.


* * *

They took him to the Counting Room, because it was the only room in the palace with a bar on the door, and because the king, when he was fetched, took one look at the three of them and said, "Oh, no," in the voice of a man who has just remembered where he left the fire.

It was a long night. Bi Gan sat on the stool by the lamp with a bowl of barley water going cold between his hands, and listened, as he had listened to ten thousand petitioners, and let them tell it in whatever order they could. The girl told it badly, in pieces, and Fuji filled in the gaps like a woman mending a net. A cave. A lamp with nine wicks. A goddess who was afraid. He did not interrupt. Twice he took off his cap and rubbed his scalp, and each time Di Xin flinched as though he'd been struck.

When it was over, nobody spoke. The lamp hissed.

"I have two questions," said Bi Gan.

"Uncle—"

"The first is for the fox." He turned. She was sitting on the cot with her knees drawn up and her five tails curled round her feet. "What is your name?"

"Yue."

"Moon." He turned it over. "A good name for a thing that does its best work after dark." He nodded slowly. "And someone has been leaving a pinch of millet beside the Pipa jar in my archive, every dawn, for weeks. I took it for the king's doing. I am obliged to correct my record."

Yue went scarlet to the ears. "It was a very small pinch."

"It was exactly the right size," said Bi Gan, and there was a faint dry wobble in his voice. "I have been meaning to thank you."

He put down the bowl.

"The second question is for the king." He did not look up. "Do you love her?"

There was no sound in the room at all.

"Yes," said Di Xin.

He said it in his grain-return voice, dry and flat, with his hands folded and his eyes on the wall. It was the voice of a man reading out an inventory, and it was the loveliest thing the old prince had heard in thirty years.

"Then that is all I need," said Bi Gan.

"All you need for what?"

"To know what I'm to do." He rose, slowly, like an old building. "I am going to do nothing. I should like you to understand it. I am going to see nothing, and say nothing, and write nothing, and when I am asked I shall say that the Lady Su is a woman of flesh and bone, and that I once saw a fox, and that I have no opinion. It is the only thing I have to offer. It is, I'm afraid, very little."

"It's everything," said Fuji, hoarsely.

"It is a lie of omission," said Bi Gan. "I have never told one. I should like it recorded that I find I do not mind." He put on his cap. At the door he turned, and the light fell across his small neat tired face, with the white beard trimmed square, and Di Xin, who had known it for twenty-five years, saw for the first time how thin it had gone.

"I keep the records," said Bi Gan. "A record is a vow, you see. It says: this happened. I have been very proud, all my life, of never having sworn to a thing that did not." He gave a short, wry, ashen laugh. "So I shall leave the page empty. And I suppose the histories will take their revenge. They always do. I should not be surprised if they cut my heart out for this."

"What does that mean?"

"Nothing, boy. An old man's idiom." He opened the door. "Go to bed."


* * *

They came, over the following three weeks, to know what an empty page was worth.

The rumor of the fox-queen had caught in the south ward like fire in dry thatch. By the end of the month there was hardly a house in the city that had not heard it, in a different version each time, and not one that did not leave something on the step: a bowl of gruel, a string of cowries, a twist of red thread. Fuji went to see it with her own eyes, on a cold morning, in a plain brown cloak, and Bi Gan went with her. A small white fox's face had been scratched in charcoal on every door. At the corner of the third lane a child of six, sitting on a step with a bowl in her lap, looked up at the tall woman in the cloak, and offered her the bowl, solemnly, in both hands.

"It's for the fox," she said. "Will you give it to her?"

"I will," said Fuji, in a voice not quite her own.

Bi Gan, a little behind her, looked up at the winter sky and began to compose, with great care, a sentence he would never write.

It was not all gruel and charcoal. In the third week the lords of the north began to ask, in the antechamber, with their hands in their sleeves, in the voice of men discussing the weather. You have the freedom of the king's garden, Prince. You will have seen the creature. Did it seem quite what it appeared? And Bi Gan, who for forty years had believed that he could not lie, discovered that he could do something more useful. He could answer a different question.

"I have kept this house's accounts for twenty years," he would say. "They balance."

It was a perfect reply. It was not a refusal, and it was not an answer. It was a very dull piece of truth, set down in the road like a stone, and every time they tried to go round it, it was in the way.


* * *

The fisherman found him on the stair of the Deer Terrace, on the twentieth evening, as the light went.

He came up from the dusk in a reed rain-cape, with a bamboo rod over his shoulder and a creel at his hip, like a man who has lost his way to the river. Nobody had challenged him on the way in. In the creel, wrapped in a cloth, was a lantern that did not burn with ordinary fire. It gave off a cold, still, pale gold light, steady as a held breath, and it was not at all troubled by the wind.

"Prince Bi Gan," said Jiang Ziya, politely. "I thought we should meet before the solstice."

"We have met."

"At a distance. In a hall." The fisherman settled himself on the step below. He had a kindly face, lined and brown and weathered as a boat, with eyes that were gentle and rather tired. "I'll be brief. I am told that you do not buy verdicts. I have no wish to sell you one. I wish only to ask, in plain words, whether you saw the fox change."

"I have told the court what I saw."

"You have told it a great deal that is true. I wonder if you would tell it something useful. A prince of the blood who stood up in the great hall and said, I saw it, would have the whole north behind him by dawn. They'd put his name on a stone. I should see to it myself."

"No."

"I thought you might." Jiang Ziya turned the rod across his knees. "A silence is not a wall, Prince. It's a hole, and someone will fill it. If you do not write what you saw, I shall, and I'll write that you saw it and held your tongue to shield a demon." The pale gold light moved on his face. "You will not like how that reads."

"I don't suppose I shall."

"Then—"

"I think," said Bi Gan, quite gently, "that you've misjudged what I am afraid of."

The fisherman waited.

"I am afraid," said the prince, "of being right in the wrong book." He rose. His knees cracked. "Write what you like. I have lived honestly, and the page will have to say what it says. But it will not have my hand in it."

"It's a promising line," said Jiang Ziya, softly. "They'll cut my heart out for this. I wonder if I might borrow it."

Bi Gan, who had said it once, to one room, three weeks before, with the door barred, did not ask how he knew. He went up the stair without a word, with his back as straight as a spear, and did not look round.


* * *

On the last night before the solstice he did not go to bed.

He sat on the roof of the Deer Terrace with a rug over his knees, and the archive of Shang in the dark beneath him: four thousand names on their red-bound rolls, and a shelf of small clay jars for the ones who had none. The air smelled of frost. The longest night of the year was still a day away. Far to the north, on the road beyond the walls, a great many small fires burned in a line: fifteen hundred horse, waiting.

He had gone to see Di Xin that evening. It had not gone well.

I'm going alone, the king had said. No guard. No sword. Uncle, I've been over it forty ways, and every way I come back to it. The knife was never for me. It's for her. If I walk in with an army, she'll have no choice but to use it.

And if you walk in with nothing?

Then she'll have a choice. He had smiled, tiredly. It seems to me that all my life I've been trying to give people one.

Bi Gan had said all the things a man says. It had made no difference. He had said, in the end, only: Take the South Court's roll. If you do not come back, someone must have it. And the king had taken it, the old red-bound roll with a hundred and sixty-one names in it, and held it against his chest, as a man holds a child.

You'll outlive me, said Di Xin. You'll see to the record.

Perhaps, said Bi Gan.

He had not said what he thought. It was a very old habit. He sat now on the cold roof under the great black bowl of the sky, with the thing he had not said lodged in his throat, and looked at it very coolly, as at a column of figures that did not balance.

The histories will take what they need, he thought. They will need a villain's cruelty. They will need a heart on a plate. And there is no other man in Shang with the plain look for it.

He sat with it a long time. The stars wheeled. In the south, in the great dark tangle of roofs, he saw one small lamp burning, in a window that he knew, in a queen's wing. A girl with five tails would be sitting up beside it, counting.

Be brave, little fox, he thought.

The east began, very slowly, to grey.

It was the day of the winter solstice. He watched the first thin light come up over the roofs of Chaoge, pale and cold and late, as it does on the shortest day of the year, and bring no warmth with it at all.
