Chapter Thirty-One: The Broker's Price

She said his name three times to the persimmon tree, and felt like a fool.

It was the dead one in the garden of the queen's wing, a black crooked thing with its branches stripped by the winter, standing in a rectangle of beaten earth under a pale morning sky. She had not been to it since the day the pedlar sat on the wall above it with his pack and his freckles and his stolen fruit. She stood where he had stood, in a red robe that was too heavy, in a face that was not hers, with her hands clenched at her sides.

"Shen Gongbao," said Yue. "Shen Gongbao. Shen Gongbao."

The tree did nothing.

Anywhere there's a market, he had said. It had seemed, at the time, an extravagant sort of boast. She looked around the empty garden, the low wall, the roofs of Chaoge beyond. Somewhere behind the palace a gong sounded, a deep slow bronze note. The late-morning gong. She had until midday.

"I know you can hear," she said, a little hoarsely. "I haven't any more time. I'll pay."

"I do so like it when they say that."

He was sitting on the wall.

He had not been there a moment ago. He sat as he had before, one leg crossed over the other, in the patched brown coat, with the pack at his side and a fresh persimmon in his hand. The freckles stood out sharply on his thin cheeks. His pale spotted eyes were bright, and entirely friendly, and not in the least surprised.

"Your Majesty," he said, and bit into the fruit. "Or, no. Not Your Majesty, I see. A great deal of trouble about the face. I heard." He chewed. "It's a very handsome one, though. It does suit you."

"Take it off."

"Straight to business! I admire that." He wiped his chin with a freckled thumb. "Do you know how many of my clients begin with a compliment? They want to be loved. It's very wearing. Whereas you've simply marched up and put a debt on the table." He slid down from the wall. "Sit. Let's be comfortable."

"I haven't time to be comfortable."

"My dear, the first rule of a bargain is that the one who isn't comfortable pays." He settled, cross-legged, in the dust at the foot of the dead tree, with the ease of a man at his own hearth, and patted the ground. "Besides, it's no good pacing. Midday will come at midday."

She sat. It was undignified, in the robe. She did it anyway.

"The nail," said Shen Gongbao. He tilted his head, considering her, as a jade-cutter considers a flawed stone. "I can see it from here. A very elegant piece of work. A single bronze pin, hammered through the root of the glamour at the base of the skull. Cold. Old. Rather beautiful." His eyes narrowed. "It's the goddess's own. She's been keeping that one in a drawer for centuries. She doesn't use it on just anyone."

"Can you remove it?"

"Remove it? Heavens, no." He sounded scandalized. "One doesn't pull a goddess's nail. One would lose a hand. And a great deal more than a hand." He took another bite of persimmon. "But there are other ways to get a nail out of a board."

"Which?"

"By showing that it was driven into the wrong wood."

She stared at him.

"Think," said Shen Gongbao, patiently. "What did Nüwa pin? A theft. That is the form of it. 'The fox has stolen a face.' It's how she wrote it down in her ledger: the lamp took the girl's likeness, to wear in her place. And as long as the entry stands, the nail holds, because it is a rule of the old law that a stolen thing cannot be returned except by the thief's own death." He shrugged. "A great many things have been got round by finding the entry. But what if the entry were wrong? What if it were not a theft at all?"

"It wasn't," said Yue slowly. "Fuji said I might."

"Did she say it aloud?"

"Yes. In the East Wing. She said, wear me." It was a strange feeling, saying it. It was like remembering a dream. "And she's said it since. Several times. She said it again last night."

"There you are." The broker beamed. "A gift is not a theft. A thing that's lent can be handed back. If the original owner of the face stands before the fox, in front of a witness, and takes it back willingly, as a thing that was lent for a purpose and is now repaid, then the goddess's nail has nothing to hold. It falls out. Like a tooth from a rotted jaw." He spread his hands. "A simple matter of the books. A little rewrite. Of course, one needs a person who understands both sides of the ledger. A person who can stand between the lender and the law. A broker."

"And you'll do it."

"I'll witness it. It's a great deal more binding." His smile did not change. "For a price."

"What price?"

"Oh, nothing alarming." The pale eyes held hers. "A tail."

She had known. She had known since she'd said his name to the tree. She had known since a pedlar in a patched coat bit his first persimmon on the garden wall. It did not stop the cold from running through her, from the nape of her neck to the base of her spine, where five of the six pale things lay folded, and the sixth stirred and curled, as though it, too, had heard.

"The goddess says I'll be down to five by the solstice," she said.

"Then I am only asking you to arrive a little early." His voice was very gentle. "I'm sorry. I'm not being cruel. A tail is the only payment that's honest. It's the only thing you have that's yours. If I were to take your cowries, or your name, or your kindness, I'd be stealing. But a tail, freely given, to pay a freely made debt, is as clean as a bell." A pause. "And I do need a lamp."

"Why?"

"The Jie have a great many lamps. We have not very much oil." He said it lightly. "I told you. I deal with everyone. The Chan have the throne of Heaven. The goddess has her temples. The fisherman has his Zhou. We have the little ones, the wandering ones, the ones that fall between the lists." His eyes were very old. "Someone has to carry a bit of light for the small and the unwritten. I take what I can get."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"I thought you were a thief."

"I am a thief. It's an honest trade." He chuckled. "I also happen to be on the side of anything that's about to be burned. It's a weakness. Do you know, that old man in the tall house, the prince with the square beard, told me last night that he doesn't buy verdicts? I've been sitting up ever since." He shook his head. "It's the most extraordinary thing. I came away with the odd feeling that I ought to apologize."

"Bi Gan?"

"The very man. He wouldn't take it. And so I thought: very well. If I can't sell an honest man a lie, I'll sell a frightened girl a door." The leopard's smile came back, thin and bright. "Do we have a bargain?"

Six, she thought. Six, and one to the broker. Five, and a solstice.

It was a dreadful sum. It was not, she found, a very hard one. She thought of a plain girl with a stubborn mouth, laughing in a lamp-lit room. She thought of a tall tired man at a parapet, looking at a red star. She thought of a pipa, in the rain, saying: Be nobody.

"Yes," said Yue.


* * *

It did not hurt as she'd expected.

He took out a small knife. It was a very small one, with a blade of black bronze no longer than a fingernail and a hilt of polished horn, and he held it up in the thin morning light and breathed on it. The blade flickered, once, with a dull pale gold. It was not the gold of the fisherman's lantern. It was warmer. It was the color of a lamp in a window, a long way off, in the dark.

"Hold still. It's quite painless."

"That's what they say."

"It's quite true. The pain comes after." He knelt behind her. She felt him gather something up, a soft cold weight like a handful of mist, and heard him murmur a few words in an old, quick, dry tongue she did not know. "Fifth from the left. The one that's been hurting, since the market. I thought so. It wants to go."

She shut her eyes.

There was a very small sound, like a stalk of millet being snapped, and then nothing at all. She felt it leave. It left cleanly, with a kind of faint relieved sigh, like a held breath let go, and somewhere far away, on a cold hill, in a hall of painted clay, a lamp with six wicks guttered, and burned with five.

She felt the goddess feel it.

Little lamp, said the voice, very faint, very cold.

I know, said Yue. I know. I'm sorry. I didn't have a choice.

There was no answer. There was only a long, listening silence.

"Now," said Shen Gongbao. His voice was not quite steady. "Your friend with the ledger. Is she near?"

"She's in the Counting Room."

"Fetch her. And be quick. We've midday coming."


* * *

Fuji came across the garden at a run.

She had been waiting. She had stood by the door of the Counting Room since dawn with her hands knotted in front of her, in a plain grey gown that Su Hu had found her, while Di Xin walked up and down the corridor like a caged animal. She came through the gap in the wall with her hair streaming and her skirts hitched in her fists, and stopped dead at the sight of the pedlar.

"This is the broker."

"Mistress Su." He bowed from his seat on the ground. "I have heard so much. Please sit. Take her hand. No, the left one. It's customary."

Fuji sat. She took Yue's hand. They were two identical girls in the dust under a black tree, with the same stubborn mouth and the same steady brows, and between them lay the length of a morning.

"You gave her your face," said Shen Gongbao. "In the East Wing. Say it."

"I gave it freely," said Fuji. Her voice was low and clear. "I said, wear me. I meant it."

"And you give it now."

"It was never a theft."

"Not as a theft. As a gift." He lifted the little knife again. "Say that you take it back as a lender takes back a loan. That the debt is paid. That the goddess has no claim."

Fuji looked at Yue.

It was a long look. It had in it a great many things: the carriage, the lamp, the wine, a barn, a fire, a prison. A string of two hundred and sixteen stones. A man in a hall who had knelt. Yue saw all of it pass over her friend's face in a moment, and then, with a perfectly steady voice, as though she were reading a column of figures aloud, Su Fuji said:

"It was lent. It's repaid. I take it back."

Something went out of the world.

It was very quiet. There was no flash, no sound. There was only a small dry tick, as of a clasp coming undone, and a faint sensation, behind Yue's eyes, of a weight lifting. Something small and cold and bright slipped out of the base of her skull, like a thorn from a wound, and fell, unseen, to the dust. The borrowed face loosened. It slid off her like water from a stone. Cool air touched her cheeks.

She opened her eyes.

Fuji was gazing at her, white-faced, wide-eyed, with her free hand pressed against her mouth. And on the other side of the dust, across the span of their joined fingers, a girl with white hair and amber eyes, in a red robe a size too large, was breathing in, and out, and in, in a face that was entirely her own.

"Oh," said Yue. "Oh. It's me."

"It's you," said Fuji, and burst into tears.

Shen Gongbao got to his feet. He dusted the dirt from his coat, with some care. He picked up his pack, and swung it to his shoulder, and gave the two of them a small, grave, almost courtly bow.

"A very good morning's work," he said. "I congratulate you both. You'll find that the fox goes very nicely into a king's sleeve." He turned toward the wall. "One thing before I go. A courtesy. I should hate to leave you with a misapprehension."

"What?" said Yue, through the lump in her throat.

"I did tell you that a tail's an honest payment." He swung one leg over the wall, and paused, and looked back at her with those pale old amber eyes. There was nothing unkind in them. There was only a great, tired, rueful patience, like that of a man who has been paying other people's debts for a very long time. "But I do so hate to let a thing go unremarked. The tail was the price of the face, my dear. It was not the price of me."

"I don't understand."

"No. You will." He smiled. "You've bought yourself an hour, and a hope, and a very pretty piece of rewriting. And now you owe the Jie a favor, too."
