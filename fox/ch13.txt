Chapter Thirteen: The Woman at the Door

It took Su Fuji until dawn to understand that she had made a very large mistake, and that it was the best one of her life.

The woman's name was Yaoniang. She was twenty-six, she said, though in the lamplight of the maids' room she might have been forty. Her left eye had swollen shut. Two of her ribs moved wrongly under Fuji's fingers when she breathed. She lay on a straw pallet in a corner, under three blankets, with her hand clamped around Fuji's wrist as though the world were a river and Fuji were the only branch.

"Don't try to talk," said Fuji.

"I have to." The whisper was thin as a thread. "If I don't say it tonight I won't say it."

"Then say it slowly."

The room smelled of lamp oil and blood and cold ash. Beside the pallet sat the queen of Shang, on an upturned washtub, with a basin of warm water in her lap and a rag in her hand. She had taken off the plum gown, which had been ruined, and wrapped herself in a servant's brown coat. Her paint was half rubbed off. She was washing a stranger's face with the grim, awkward tenderness of someone doing a thing for the first time, and her own hands were trembling.

Fuji did not look at her for long. It hurt too much.

I put her at the center of it, she thought. The queen's maid. The bread. The back door. For two weeks, whenever there was a heel of loaf to spare, she had walked it out to the kitchen gate and left it on the step. She had not told anyone. She had not signed it, or spoken of it, or imagined that anyone would think it was more than a girl's foolishness. And somewhere in the lanes of the south ward, in the way gossip runs in a city, the whisper had been passed from mouth to mouth: The new queen has a door. Go knock on it.

It was, she realized, the first petition she had ever received.

"I'm from the Geng fields," said Yaoniang. "The east holding, past the river. There are three hundred of us. We work the temple acre." Her voice caught. "You know what that is?"

"Tell me."

"It's the field that grows the goddess's grain. Seed from the temple, harvest to the temple. The Geng hold it for the Office. We're not hired. We're given. To the field, to the goddess. When the bones say they need a life, they ask the steward, and the steward picks." She swallowed. "The king closed the pits three springs ago, in the third year of his reign. We heard it from the carters. We wept in the barns. And then the steward said he was very sorry, but the Geng were a great house and the goddess was a greater one, and it would not do to let the old rites lapse altogether."

Fuji's hand had gone very still on the blanket.

"They still pick," said Yaoniang. "Quietly. Four or five a season, from the east holding. They take them out at night, to the old pit behind the granary. In the book they write dead of fever. The clerk who keeps the book is a boy called Ming, he's fifteen, he can't sleep. He told me. He's been counting them for a year, in a hole in the wall behind his bed. Seven this year. He couldn't bear it. He told me where he'd hidden the slips." She was crying now, without sound, tears running sideways into her hair. "I said I'd take them to the king. I said I'd walk to the palace. And somebody must have heard, because they came for me at the gate, and Ming's gone, and I don't know..."

"Hush."

"I don't know if he's alive."

"Hush now." Fuji turned her wrist and took the thin, cold hand in both of hers. "I'm going to find out. I promise you. I'll find out."

Seven, she thought. Seven this year, from one holding. She saw them as clearly as columns on a ledger: seven names, entered in a boy's small frightened hand, under fever. She had spent a week learning the king's Spared list by heart. It was a list of the ones he had caught. She had never once thought to ask how many he had not.


* * *

The healer arrived at the fifth watch, with her hair in a grey braid and a basket on her arm and an expression of weary irritation, like a woman who has been woken at an unreasonable hour to be told something she already knew.

"I'm Qingpu," she said. "From the south ward. A girl came to my door in the night, in a maid's cap, with a handful of cowries and a very polite note. I don't generally go to palaces." She set down the basket. "Where is she?"

She knelt. She was a tall rawboned woman of perhaps fifty, with herb-stained fingers and a slight limp, and she went over the battered body on the pallet with the brisk, professional, unsentimental thoroughness of someone who has seen every possible way a person can be hurt and has given up being surprised by any of them. She listened at the chest. She pressed. She muttered. At the sound of a catch in the breath, she frowned.

"Two ribs. Cracked, not broken. A good deal of bleeding under the skin, but nothing leaking in the belly, thank the old ones." She sat back. "Whoever did this was a professional. He knew where to stop."

"Will she live?" asked Yue.

It was the first thing she had said all night. Qingpu looked up. Her eyes went over the queen's smeared face, the brown coat, the basin in her lap, and something in them changed, very slightly. Not softened. Sharpened.

"She'll live," she said slowly. "If she's kept warm, and kept still, and doesn't try to climb a wall for a month. Yes." She considered the queen for a moment longer. "That's a good cloth you're holding."

"It's a rag."

"It's a good one. You wring it well." She turned to her basket. "Most people squeeze out the water and leave the dirt. You've done it properly. I'd have said you did this for a living."

Yue looked at the cloth as if she had never seen it.

"Is it... usual to wring them like that?" she said.

"Only among people who've done a great deal of caring for others and don't know it." Qingpu uncorked a small clay jar. "I'm going to bind the ribs. Hold her hand, Your Majesty, and don't let go. This is going to hurt."


* * *

It was done by sunrise. The woman lay sleeping under a clean sheet, with her ribs wrapped and a bitter-smelling draught inside her. Qingpu sat on the floor with her back against the wall, drinking hot barley water from a clay cup with the air of a person who had earned it. Yue had fallen asleep sitting up, head against the washtub, with her hand still holding Yaoniang's.

Fuji stood by the window with her arms wrapped around herself, looking out at the grey square of sky.

Seven, she thought. And the king has no idea. He had read every report that came to him. But the reports came from the Office. They were written by clerks who would sooner lose a hand than send up a figure that contradicted the bones. A great house with an old pit and a pliable steward could conceal as many deaths as it liked, and write fever in the book, and the king would sign for them. He had seen a vast pile of slips every night of his reign, and not one of them had told him.

She wanted, suddenly and fiercely, to tell him.

She made herself go through it as she would a column of figures, line by line, until the wanting went quiet. If the maid Ling carried a petition to the king, he would ask who Ling was. If he asked who Ling was, he would, being the man he was, find out. And a king who discovered that his new queen had a double, a stranger in his own house wearing a servant's cap, would have two choices, and both of them ended with somebody's head on a spike. She had a good deal of respect for his arithmetic. She had none at all for his luck.

But she had also, somewhere around the third watch of the night, with Yaoniang's blood drying on her cuffs, stopped being able to believe in a version of the world in which she simply carried the bread to the door and went away.

It was not a reasonable thing to want. She had a name that wasn't hers and a face that belonged to someone else, and she was standing in a servant's room in a grey cap with a woman's blood on her sleeves. She was a hostage who did not exist. She had arranged to be a ghost. But she found that she had already begun, in the back of her mind, to draft the petition: to the king, from the queen's maid, regarding the Geng east holding. A short one. One slip. It would fit in a sleeve.

"You're thinking of telling him," said Qingpu.

Fuji turned. The healer had not moved. She was looking at her over the rim of the cup with a pair of very shrewd, very tired grey eyes.

"I'm not sure what you mean."

"The maid who sent for a village herbalist in the middle of the night, with a perfectly good handful of cowries and a perfectly good hand. The queen who washes the floor." Qingpu drank. "I do not know what you are. But I know a person who is about to make a very large and dangerous move, and I know it better than you think. I've done it once myself."

Fuji said nothing.

"Whoever you are," said Qingpu, "do it properly. A woman like that, one beaten for talking, has no standing. She's a field hand of a great house, a thing consecrated to the goddess. In law she isn't even hers." She set down the cup. "Any lord can reclaim her. Any priest can say the word. You need a place for her. Somewhere that no steward can walk in and ask. Without that, anything you do is only a bandage."

A place. Fuji turned it over. It was the right thing, of course. It was exactly the thing she had not yet let herself think. Not the petition, not the ledger. A room with a door that locked from the inside.

She did not have one.

Behind her, a soft, odd sound came from the corner. Yue, still asleep, had said something indistinct and turned her face into her shoulder. Yaoniang's hand lay in hers, quite relaxed now, as though it belonged there. In the cold grey light, without the face she wore, the white-haired girl looked very young. Fuji's heart stopped; but the healer, squinting across the room through the poor light, gave the sleeping girl only the glance a healer gives anyone worn out, and made no wonder of it.

"She's yours," said Qingpu, following her gaze. "The queen. Isn't she?"

"I'm her maid."

"Mm." The healer rose, joints cracking, and took up her basket. "I'll come back at noon to see to the dressing. If anyone asks, I was called to the palace to treat a kitchen girl for a scald. It's a common enough story." She paused in the doorway, looking back. "Child. If you do take that petition to the king, take the slips, too. The boy's slips. They're the only thing that will make him believe you."

"I don't know where they are."

"Then you had better find out before somebody else does."

She went out.

The light grew. Somewhere in the palace a gong sounded, and a door banged, and a long way off a dog began to bark. Fuji went to the washtub and began, with careful hands, to take the cold rag from Yue's fingers, and stopped, because the white-haired girl had turned in her sleep and gripped her sleeve.

She stood there for a while, motionless, with a stranger's hand in one of hers and a fox's in the other.

The knock, when it came, was not loud.

It was quite precise. Three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man with all the time in the world and an entire institution at his back. It came from the outer door of the queen's wing, where a bored guard was drowsing on his stool. Fuji heard the guard stand up. She heard a voice speak, level, courteous, a little regretful: a voice she had heard once before, across a hall, saying something about three hundred oxen.

"I beg the queen's pardon for the hour," it said. "I am Yuan Shi, Master of Sacrifices. A woman of the Geng east holding has escaped her field. She is consecrated to the goddess. I have reason to think she's within these walls."

He paused.

"I have come to take her home."
