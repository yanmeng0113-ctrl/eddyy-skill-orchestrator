Chapter Twelve: The Pond

Di Xin had decided, some years ago, that the worst thing about being slandered was not the lies. It was the expense.

Nobody had ever explained to him how much a lie cost to run. You had to feed it. You had to let it go on being believed, sometimes for years, so that something else could be done while everybody was busy believing it. He thought of it as a kind of rent, paid in reputation, and he had been paying a great deal of it lately. This evening he was about to pay a year in advance.

The great forecourt of the palace at Chaoge had been transformed. Four hundred torches stood in bronze brackets along the walls. Long tables, borrowed from every inn and temple kitchen in the city, ran in rows down the beaten earth, and between them, in a square trench lined with clay, six hundred jars of new millet wine were being poured by a chain of sweating servants into a glittering, dark, fragrant pond. It lay in the middle of the court like a pool of black lacquer. The smell of it carried half a mile.

On the north side, in the old plane trees, a thousand cuts of meat hung from cords. Flanks of boar, haunches of deer, long ribbons of cured beef, all swaying in the torchlight and dripping a little onto the heads of the guests below. The royal cooks had been at it for three days. They had wept at the expense, then become competitive.

It was, without question, the most disgraceful thing the House of Shang had ever done. He stood at the head of the high table in his dark ceremonial robe and surveyed it with the calm of a man looking at a very large bill.

"It's obscene," said Bi Gan, at his elbow.

"Thank you, Uncle."

"I mean it as a criticism."

"I know. That is why I'm grateful." He lowered his voice. "Has Yuan Shi come?"

"He's at the end of the third table. He has brought a cup of water and a clerk to write down what he sees." The old man's face was drawn, and there was a strain around his eyes that Di Xin hadn't noticed before. "Nephew. May I ask what this is for? You don't want a feast. You don't like noise. You've not drunk more than a cup of wine in your life."

"I know."

"Then—"

"You'll see by morning." He put a hand on the old man's shoulder, briefly, which he did not often do. "Trust me for one night, Uncle. It's only one."

One night, he thought. Not yet. It was a thing he'd have given a good deal not to have written.


* * *

The nobles came in at dusk. They came in their best bronze and their softest wool, with their wives and their cousins and their retainers behind them, and they stopped, one after another, at the edge of the forecourt, and stared. Then, the way people always do when something has been made as easy to be wicked as possible, they began to smile.

Lord Geng came first, a heavy man with a rolling gait and a reputation for his estates, which ran for forty miles along the river and employed, in a manner of speaking, eleven thousand people. He looked at the pond. He looked at the meat in the trees. He looked at the king, and Di Xin saw a series of small thoughts cross his face, in order: surprise; contempt; relief. It was always the third that mattered. Men were so very much happier when their opinion of one was confirmed.

"Great King," said Lord Geng, with a grin. "I was beginning to fear you had turned philosopher."

"One does what one can," said Di Xin, and gestured to the nearest cup.

They drank.

He watched them do it from the head of the high table, as he had watched people for twenty years: patient, alert, expressionless. The Zhong brothers, loud and quick, who owned the western mines. Lord Lü and his three sons. Young Wei, sulking, who was made to sit between his uncles. A hundred lesser lords at the lower tables, in their dozens and scores, their retainers, their stewards, their scribes. Every household that held a granary in Shang had sent someone, and every one had come hungry. The wine was very good. It had been put aside for the coronation of a king who never wanted one.

By the second watch the singing had begun.

By the third, the first man had fallen into the pond.


* * *

Yue sat at his right hand in a gown of deep plum, with her face made up in the Zhongzhou style, white and pointed, and her veil thrown back. She was sitting very straight. She had not touched her cup. In the long light of the torches he could see her fingers, curled tight around the stem, and the tension in her shoulders, and the small hot flush rising up her neck.

She was frightened.

He had been watching it for an hour. She was frightened of the noise and of the smell, and of Lord Geng's thigh, which kept finding its way toward her knee under the cloth. She was frightened of the pheasant, who sat three seats down in a modest grey gown, smiling and nodding and sipping and watching with an expression of fierce, secret joy. And she was frightened, he thought, of him. Not the way she had been on the first night. It was subtler now, and more complicated. As though she had asked for something terrible and were waiting to see what it would cost.

If I were another man, thought Di Xin, I'd stop her fear. But he was not another man, and he could not.

He leaned toward her. "You've not eaten."

"I'm not hungry."

"Neither am I. But we must be seen." He lifted a dish of venison and set it before her. "Eat. Slowly. Smile at Lord Geng's jokes. In about an hour you may go to bed with a headache."

"Why?"

"Because in an hour," said Di Xin, very quietly, "something will happen that you won't want to watch."

She turned her head. Her eyes, which were not the eyes of the girl whose face she wore, but a deep, clear, clouded amber that he'd seen only by lamplight, searched his face. He wondered what she saw there. Not the truth, surely. He had never in his life been so careful with the truth.

"You're not drinking," she said slowly.

"No."

"Not at all."

"Not at all." He tipped his cup, just slightly, so she could see what was in it. Plain water, cool and clear as a mountain brook, in a bronze goblet that had been filled by his own steward.

She stared at it. A change passed over her face, as though she had worked something out, the first small click of a lock.

"Oh," she said.


* * *

It began with the Master of the Granary, as these things do.

He was a nervous man and a cautious one, and he had spent the first half of the evening carrying jar after jar of wine to the pit with the look of a person disposing of evidence. Now, at the king's signal, he walked up the high table steps with a lacquer tray in his hands, bowed to the nobles in the front row, and cleared his throat.

"If it please my lords," he said. "The king wishes to propose a toast."

There was a roar.

Di Xin rose. It cost him something to do it, the long body unfolding behind the high table, the torchlight catching in the bronze of his collar. He lifted his goblet of water in a hand that did not shake. Every face in the forecourt turned toward him, flushed, grinning, damp with sweat and wine.

"My lords," he said. "I have been a poor host. I've given you little to eat, and too little to drink, and I am told you have been sorely missed at my table. I have come to make amends." A cheer. "Tonight, in honor of my queen and the peace with Zhongzhou, I propose a toast to the realm. To the granaries of Shang. To the harvest that fills them. To the fidelity of every house that guards them." He raised the cup. "And since a toast should be sealed, I ask each house to set its mark on this tablet, as witness before gods and men that the harvest is one thing, and the realm is another. That the granaries of Shang belong to the people of Shang, in time of need. Whoever seals it drinks first."

The Master set the lacquer tray on the table. Upon it lay a long roll of bamboo slips, bound in red, and a stack of clay seals.

Nobody heard what the slips said. Nobody was listening. That was the whole beauty of it. They heard toast and drink first, and the way a hundred men who have been pouring wine since dusk will hear a thing: as a delightful new game, in which one gets a cup for putting one's name to a piece of wood.

"Geng will seal!" roared Lord Geng, who had been drinking since noon and was a boy again. "Give it here!"

He pressed the seal, with a flourish, into the wet clay at the foot of the roll. The Zhong brothers fought for the next. Lord Lü, flushed and beaming, pressed his thumb so hard into the clay that it left a whorl. Young Wei, sulking, was made by his uncles to do it twice. The lesser lords came in a stumbling, singing queue, each of them signing without reading, as men do who have been told it is an honor, and each of them took a cup and drank it to the dregs.

What the roll said, in the king's own clear and upright hand, was this: In the event of famine declared by the crown, each house undertakes to open its granaries at the king's price for the relief of the stricken districts, the stock so released to be accounted against the tax of that house in the following year.

It was not a very exciting sentence. It had taken him eleven nights to write. It would open four hundred granaries in the spring.

They will curse me for it, he thought, watching the seals go down. When they wake. When they read it. They will say I got them drunk and cheated them, and they will be right. But they would also have signed in front of two thousand witnesses, and the thing could not be unsigned.

On the third table, a tall man in ash-white put down a cup of water.

Yuan Shi had not drunk a drop. He had sat the entire evening in the same spot with a clerk at his elbow, writing, writing, in a small neat hand. Now he rose slowly. His eyes went from the king to the roll, to the nobles stumbling past the tray, and Di Xin watched understanding rise in his face, not triumph, nor indignation, but a long slow grave dismay.

He's seen. Di Xin met his eyes across a hundred torches. He knows what it is. And he knows that I know that he knows.

Yuan Shi lifted his cup, an inch, in a salute so slight that nobody else could have seen it. He sat down. He did not put his mark to the tablet. He did not interfere.

He went on writing.

He is going to write it, thought Di Xin, with a strange cold pang. The pond. The meat in the trees. The queen at my right hand. All of it, in his small clear hand, for the temples. He's going to write down exactly what he saw. And a hundred years from now, in a temple library, some clerk would take out a tablet and read it, and say: the king bathed in a lake of wine.

Yes, he thought. That's the cost.


* * *

It was after the fourth watch, when the torches had burned low and the last lord had been carried out on a door, that he found her.

She was standing alone at the end of the forecourt, by the north wall, under the dripping trees, with her back to the empty tables. She had not gone to bed. She had been waiting. As he came up beside her she said, without turning, "The roll."

"Yes."

"It opens the granaries."

"In a famine. At my price. In a spring, I hope, that will never come." He looked at the pond, black and still under the moon, with a single drowned jar floating in it. "They'll remember tonight as the night the king drank himself stupid. Let them. It does no harm. It will be a very long time before anyone thinks to ask what I was signing."

There was a pause. Then, softly, with a note in her voice that he could not read: "Did you know what I'd ask?"

"No." It was the truth. "I only knew what I'd do with it."

She turned and looked at him. The torchlight was low and red, and her face under the paint was tired and very young and quite unguarded. It was not the face she'd been given. It was hers, he thought. It showed in the set of the mouth. It showed in the way she was looking at him, as if for the first time, and trying to decide whether to be angry.

"You made me ruin you," she said. "On purpose."

"I made use of it."

"They'll hate you for it."

"Some of them already do." He tried to smile, and found, with surprise, that it was difficult. "I had it in the ledger."

She drew a breath to say something. She never said it.

Across the torchlit court, from the dark archway of the kitchen passage, a figure came walking toward them with a slow, dragging, uneven step. It was the maid, the quiet one in grey, Ling. She was bent nearly double. In her arms, wrapped in a stained blanket, she carried something that moved.

It was a woman. A young one, with a face swollen almost out of recognition, one eye closed, her hair clotted with blood and her sleeve torn to the shoulder. She hung limp as a rag, and her breath came in small, whistling gasps.

The maid stopped three paces from the king, in the red light, in front of the empty tables and the pond. She did not kneel. She did not lower her eyes. She looked straight at the queen.

"Madam," she said. "I found her at the kitchen gate. She says she was beaten by her master's steward for talking to a clerk about the king's tallies." The girl's voice cracked, and steadied. "She asked for the queen. She said she'd heard that the queen has a door."
