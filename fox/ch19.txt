Chapter Nineteen: Lessons in Being Human

Nobody had ever taught Yue to read, and by the first snows of winter she had discovered that she was extremely bad at it.

"It's a grain," said Di Xin. "Not a goose."

"It looks like a goose."

"It looks like a stalk of millet with a tail. This is the tail." He tapped the slip with the butt of his brush. "The goose has four strokes at the top and a hook. You're reading it as the goose because you're thinking about the goose."

"I'm not thinking about the goose."

"You've said 'goose' three times since the lamp was lit."

"The pipa-player has a song about one."

"I'm aware," said the king, with some feeling. "Your cousin hums it through the wall every night at the fourth watch. I could recite the verses."

It was the tenth month, and winter had come early. Snow had fallen on Chaoge, the first real snow of the season, a thick quiet blanket that muffled the roofs and the streets and the sound of the market, and lay in heaped soft drifts against the walls of the palace. Inside the Counting Room a brazier burned, and on the long table lay a roll of household registers from the south ward, spread open under four stone weights. They had been copied out, in the king's clear hand, so that she could learn from them. Yue sat on the bench with her chin in her hands and her white hair tied back, scowling at a column of small black characters as if they had insulted her mother.

She was wearing her own face. It was a luxury she'd been allowed twice a week, behind the locked door of the study, with the lamp turned low. The first time, she had sat bolt upright on the bench, staring at the wall. The king had politely not looked at her and had talked about millet for the length of a long meal. By the third visit she had begun to forget she was doing it.

"Try again," said Di Xin.

She tried again.

It was not entirely a disaster. She could read her own name now, which was three strokes and a flourish, and the character for fox, which she found insulting for being so easy, and the numbers up to ten. She could pick out Su and Geng and granary. When she got something right, the corner of his mouth went up, very slightly, as though he had been given a small private present. She had started doing things correctly mostly to see it.

"What's that one?" he said.

"Door."

"And this?"

"Hm. Two doors. Gate."

"Very good. And this?"

She looked at the long sprawling cluster of strokes. She had never seen it before. But her eye caught on a small mark at the top, like a drop of water, and a shape beneath it, like a bent bow.

"Star," she said slowly. "Is that star?"

"It is." His voice sounded different. "It's the old form. How did you know?"

"I don't. I just..." She stopped, embarrassed. "I've seen it. In the sky."

"Have you?"

"I used to lie on the roof of Nüwa's temple. When I was small. There's a hill behind it, and no lamps, and on a clear night you can see all of them." She said it to the slip, not to him. "There's one low in the east, in winter, a little before dawn. It's red. It's very bright. It doesn't twinkle. Pipa told me once that it was a joyful star, that it governed weddings. I thought it was a stupid thing for a star to do."

The brazier crackled. A cold draft came under the door.

"The Joy-Star," said Di Xin quietly.

"What?"

"The Heavenly Joy. It's low in the east in winter, before dawn. The astrologers use it to fix the day of the new year." He had turned in his chair and was looking at her, with an odd arrested expression on his long face. "I've never seen it. Not really. I've only ever read about it, in the tables."

"Never?"

"I'm always up at that hour. But I'm always inside."

"Why?"

"There's a lot to count."

She looked at him. She thought of a thin, hunched, sleepless man in a high lamp-lit room, bent over his slips while the whole bright wheel of the sky wheeled round outside, uncounted, unread. It came to her with a force that was almost a pain that nobody had ever shown him the stars.

"Come on," she said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Get your cloak. I'll show you."

There were other lessons, in the south ward, that she was better at.

The South Court had a roof now, thatched thick and tight by a team of carpenters whom nobody had hired, and a new gate, and a long trestle table in the middle of the main hall where on most nights sixty people sat down to eat. Yue came there in the afternoons in her brown coat. She was not the queen there. She was a woman called Moon, who had a bad temper, a great deal of strength in her hands, and the unaccountable privilege of being Ling's friend.

Yaoniang had taught her to knead.

"Not like that," she said, from the other side of the board. She was thin still, and favoring her left side, but the swelling was gone from her face and she had begun to laugh again, a small hoarse crackle, like a fire catching. "You're fighting it. Lean. Let the weight do it. It's not a person."

"It feels like one."

"It's dough. It doesn't mind." Yaoniang put her hands over Yue's and pushed, a slow, easy, rolling motion, the heel of the hand, then the fold. "There. Feel that? It gives. You have to give it something to give back."

Yue felt it. The warm resistance of it, the slow yielding. She had been taught nine hundred ways to take a thing from a mortal, in four hundred years. She had never once been taught to make one.

"It's like," she said slowly, "a thing that wants to be bread."

"That's all any of us want, madam. To be bread." Yaoniang grinned, a crooked, gap-toothed flash. "You'll make someone a good wife."

"I'm married."

"I know. I've seen him. He's much too thin." She slapped the dough. "Eat something, Moon. You look like you've never had a meal in your life."

Across the hall, Qingpu, sitting on an upturned bin with a sleeping child across her knees, caught Yue's eye over the rim of a clay cup and raised one eyebrow, a slow, dry, unreadable lift. She had never once asked what Moon was. She had never asked why a woman who wore a brown coat in the afternoons could be seen on certain evenings, by certain of the ward's older gossips, in a carriage with the royal banner. She had simply laid out a second bowl at table, and said, You'll want a spoon.

It was the first time in four hundred years that anyone had given Yue a place to sit and not asked what she was for.

She went home that night with flour in her hair, and for once the cousin's humming through the wall did not trouble her at all.


* * *

They went up to the roof of the Deer Terrace in the black hour before dawn, in snow that had frozen hard on the steps, with a single shuttered lantern and the guards left grumbling at the bottom.

It was not a pleasure tower. Yue had heard the rumors in the market, the same ones the cook told Fuji: that the king had built a great tower of jade and gold on the north side of the palace, where he feasted with a hundred concubines and watched men be torn apart by dogs. It was nothing of the kind. It was an old rammed-earth mound, forty feet high, with a flat top and a low parapet, and the only things on it were a round stone table, a bronze bowl of water for measuring the shadow of a gnomon, and a long bench. Underneath, in the hollow of the mound, ran a series of low cool rooms lined with shelves. They were the archives. They held the king's father's tallies, and his grandfather's, and the records of the great houses, and in one dry corner a very large stack of bones.

"The first thing I did when I came to the throne," said Di Xin, as they climbed, "was put the old kings' records in order. Nobody had touched them in forty years. The bones were stacked with the cracks facing the wrong way. They'd been consulting the wrong ones." He sounded faintly amused. "I'm told that explains a great deal about the state of the harvest."

"And the roof?"

"I like the roof."

He stopped at the top and held out a hand. She took it. It was a warm, hard, ink-stained hand, and she didn't let go until she'd reached the parapet.

The sky stood open above them. It was as black as the inside of a bowl and very vast and very still, and the stars burned in it with a hard crystalline brilliance that the lamp-lit streets of Chaoge had never allowed her to see. Away to the east the first thin grey was beginning, like the edge of a knife held to a wall, and below it, lower than any other light on the horizon, a single point of deep, steady, pulsing red.

"There," said Yue. "That one."

He did not say anything.

He stood at the parapet with his cloak pulled round him and his breath streaming from his mouth in the cold, and looked. He looked for a long time. The red star hung low over the roofs of Chaoge, and a pale light began to climb the sky behind it. The king of Shang did not move. In the lantern-glow his face was bare, and quiet, and astonished, like a child's.

"It's rather good," he said at last, in a small voice.

"I know."

"It's red."

"I said that."

"I thought it would be smaller." He let out a long breath, a small plume of white. "I've been looking at the tables for twenty years. I'd no idea it was so... present."

She found herself laughing. It was an unfamiliar sensation. It came up from somewhere beneath her ribs, light and sudden and silly, and she put her hand over her mouth to stop it, and he turned and looked at her, and he began to laugh too. They stood there on the roof of the Deer Terrace in the grey before sunrise, the King of Shang and a nine-tailed fox with seven tails, helpless with giggles, while the sun came up over the Wei river and the last stars went out one by one.

When it stopped, there was a quiet.

It was very close. She could feel the warmth of his shoulder against hers through two layers of wool. She could feel her heart going, quite fast, quite hard, in a way that had nothing to do with the climb. She did not look at him. She looked at the red star, which was slowly fading in the growing light.

"Tell me what it governs," he said.

"Weddings."

"Is that all?"

"And joy. Pipa said." She swallowed. "She said it was the star you looked for when you wanted to know whether the thing you were afraid of was going to be worth it."

"And is it?"

She did not answer. She wanted very much to say yes. She wanted to say it in a voice that carried across the roofs, and the snow, and the sleeping city, down to the dark house in the south ward where a woman she loved was writing the names of the damaged by a single lamp. She thought of the roof of a burning granary. She thought of seven pale tails, and a lamp with seven wicks, and a goddess who waited on a hill.

It was not a good time.

"Ask me at the full moon," said Yue.

He did not say anything. But after a moment, quite quietly, as though by accident, his hand closed over hers on the cold stone of the parapet, and stayed there.


* * *

They came down at sunrise. The snow had begun again, a slow, tender, lazy fall that settled on their shoulders like feathers. Neither of them spoke. At the foot of the Terrace, where the guards waited, stamping and blowing on their hands, Yue straightened her hood and drew on her queen's face, one smooth movement, like a woman drawing a sleeve down over her wrist. Beside her, Di Xin lifted his chin and became the king.

It was almost too easy. She was not sure whether she was relieved or sorry.

A messenger was waiting on the steps of the Counting Room, a thin young man in a courier's grey with snow on his boots and a sealed bamboo case in his hand. He bowed to the king, and looked at the queen, and went white.

"Great King. Great Queen." His voice cracked. "A runner has come from the Hill of Nüwa. The Master of Sacrifices begs me to deliver, in person, the word of the goddess."

"What word?" said Di Xin.

"That the winter rite is ordained." The courier swallowed. "In the old style. At the full moon of the tenth month. At the foot of the altar, before the whole of the court." His eyes slid, helplessly, to the queen. "She asks that the king himself should preside. And she asks that the queen, in her own person, should bring the offering."
