Chapter Fifteen: The Granary on the South Wall

The south granary smelled of rats, wet clay, and twelve years of disappointment, and Su Fuji loved it on sight.

It was a long low building of rammed earth, the size of a small barn, with a thatched roof that had lost most of its thatch and a stone floor that had been laid by someone who believed in drainage. A central aisle ran its length, flanked by rows of waist-high clay bins, each with a wooden lid. At the far end, a door the width of a cart opened onto a walled yard where a dead persimmon tree held up its black hands against the sky. There was a well. There was a stable, collapsed at one end. There was a smaller building by the gate, which had once been the granary keeper's lodging, and had a hearth and a chimney and a single window that actually closed.

"It's dreadful," said Yue.

"It's enormous." Fuji walked down the aisle with her skirts hitched in one hand, counting bins. "Forty. With lids. They'd make beds, if you laid planks across them. The roof can be thatched in a week. The well is sound, I'd stake my life on it. Look at the stone lip." She whirled. "Do you realize what we have here? A walled yard. A single gate. A door that bars from the inside. Nobody can walk in."

"I thought you said it smelled."

"It smells of rats. Rats leave when you bring in cats." She stopped in front of the king, who stood at the doorway with his hands behind his back and a look of mild, wary pleasure, like a man who has produced a rabbit from his sleeve and is not sure of the audience. "Great King. May I ask the clerk's valuation?"

"Fourteen strings of cowries. In my father's time. I should think it's worth less now."

"I'd pay the fourteen. Today."

"With what?"

"With the queen's allowance for the next year," said Fuji, and then closed her mouth, because that was not what a maid said.

There was a small silence. Beside her, Yue made a noise that might have been a cough.

"She's very attached to the queen," said Yue, with enormous gravity. "She says things on my behalf. I've stopped trying to prevent it."

"I see." The king's eyes crinkled, slightly, at the corners. He looked at Fuji for a moment, a quick, level, assessing look, and then away, toward the rafters, where a single sparrow had begun to scold. "A maid with strong opinions on cowries. How refreshing. I've been looking for a clerk like that for six years." He let the silence sit. "Fourteen strings. It's yours, madam. I'll have the deed sent to the queen's wing by evening."

"Thank you, Great King," said Yue.

"It's nothing. It's a very leaky granary." He turned to go, and then hesitated. "I'd ask only that you let me know what you intend to call it. For the ledger. It seems wrong to keep a place nameless."

Yue looked at Fuji.

Fuji looked at the dead persimmon. A name. She had not considered it. In all her years of making lists, it had never occurred to her that she might one day be asked to name a thing she'd made. It was strange and slightly dizzying, like being asked to name a child.

"The South Court," she said. "Plain. Nobody will want to burn it down."

"The South Court." The king nodded slowly. "Very good."

He went out. The sparrow, finding the audience diminished, redoubled its efforts.


* * *

It took them eight days.

On the first, Qingpu arrived with two apprentices, a cart of clean straw, and a jar of vinegar, and set them scrubbing the bins. On the second, a carpenter turned up, sent by nobody in particular, with a ladder and a bag of nails. On the third, Fuji went to the market in the south ward with a handful of cowries and came back with forty blankets, two cooking pots, a sack of lentils, and a tomcat. It was a very good cat. It had been due to be drowned.

By the fifth day the roof was on. By the sixth the first families had come.

They came in ones and twos, mostly at dusk, along the back lanes of the south ward where the lamps burned low. A bondwoman with a baby on her hip. A boy with a burned hand who'd run from the temple acre of the Zhong. An old man whose son had been taken from the western mines three years ago and had not been seen again. They did not knock. They stood at the door of the granary and looked at the lamp in the window. Then, after a long moment, they came in.

Nobody had told them where to come. On the seventh night Fuji asked a woman at the well how she had heard, and the woman looked at her as though she had asked how one hears about rain. Everyone knows, she said. There's a door in the south wall that doesn't ask what you did first. It was, Fuji thought, the most frightening compliment she had ever been paid, because there were only forty beds, and she could already see the forty-first person walking down the lane.

Fuji met them with a ledger.

It was a small one, bamboo slips on a cord, kept by the door on a stool. She wrote their names. She asked what they needed. She asked, in a quiet voice, what had been done to them, and in what house, and by whom. She wrote that too. It was not a thing she'd planned, the recording. It had simply begun, on the first night, when a woman had told her an address and she had reached for a slip. Name. House. What happened. Who knew. A column for each. In the evenings she read them over, one by one, in the lodging by the gate, with a single lamp, until the words ran together and she had to put her head on the table.

The king came on the sixth night, alone, in a plain brown cloak, with the hood pulled low and a sack of lentils on each shoulder.

Fuji, at the door with her ledger, did not recognize him until he set the sacks down on the step and said, "I'm told the soup is a bribe to the poor. I thought I'd lose these on the road. Somebody may as well find them."

"Great King." She bowed so fast she nearly dropped the slips. "The queen is in the yard. I'll fetch—"

"No, don't. I came for the maid." He straightened. "Ling. That's your name? I'd like to see the book."

She could not think of a reason to refuse. She handed it over. He turned it to the lamp and read down the first column with his lips slightly moving, the way a man reads who has spent his life among tallies and trusts only what he has counted himself. She watched his thumb move down the slip. It paused at one line, then another. A small furrow appeared between his eyebrows.

"You write who knew," he said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because someone always knew." It came out more sharply than she'd intended. "A woman is beaten, a child is sold, a man is sent to the pit. And somewhere in the house is a cook or a clerk or a boy who held the lamp and said nothing. I want to know who held the lamp."

He looked at her for a long moment. It was an odd look. Not suspicious. Almost wistful.

"I've done the same for three years," said Di Xin. "Not as thoroughly." He handed her back the book. "I had a word with Lord Geng's steward today. The one who beat Yaoniang. He sent his apologies. He says the clerk of the east holding, a boy called Ming, died of a fever, four days ago. A very sad business."

Fuji stopped breathing.

"Fever," said the king. He was watching her face. "I thought you might want to know. Since the name is in your book."

He pulled up his hood, and went away down the lane. She stood on the step in the dusk with a sack of lentils at her feet and her hand pressed flat against the ledger, trying to work out whether she had just been warned or informed, and how much of her he had seen.


* * *

Yue found her there on the ninth night.

"You've stopped sleeping," said Yue, from the doorway.

"I'm sleeping very efficiently."

"That isn't a thing." The fox came in and shut the door, and sat down at the other end of the table with her elbows on the wood and her chin in her hands. She was in her plain brown coat again, and her hair was escaping its bronze pins. She had been spending her days at the South Court with a shovel, and had a streak of clay across one cheek. She looked, Fuji thought, about as little like a queen as it was possible to look. She looked like someone who had come home.

"I thought you'd have given up by now," said Fuji. "It's a good deal of digging."

"It's very restful. Nobody looks at the digger."

"That's what I said about the maid."

"It's a mistake." Yue picked up a slip, looked at it, and put it down. "Forty-one," she said. "In nine days. Do you know that? I counted. Forty-one people through that door. Twenty-three women, eleven children. I've no idea what we're going to do with them when winter comes."

"Eat lentils."

"Fuji."

"I know." Fuji laid down her brush. "I know. It's insane. We've a roof and a well and a tomcat and a granary with forty beds, and by midwinter we'll need four hundred. I have a ledger with forty-one names in it and nowhere to send them. I've a king who will pay for the walls and not the soup, because if he pays for the soup the Office will call it a bribe to the poor." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "And I am enjoying myself more than I've ever done in my life."

There was a pause. Yue had gone quite still.

"I wasn't supposed to say that," said Fuji.

"No." The fox was smiling, a small, crooked, helpless smile. "You weren't. But I'm glad you did."

"I came to Chaoge to get the petitions delivered."

"And have you?"

"No." She laughed, a little shakily. "No, I haven't. I've been so busy with the door that I forgot about the king." She looked down at her hands. They were stained with ink and clay and a little blood from a splinter. "I'm supposed to be in the room with the tray. Listening. That was the plan. I was going to watch him, and learn what he was, and decide. And instead I'm here, in a barn."

"Is it worse?"

"It's better," said Fuji. "That's what frightens me."

Outside, the cat began to yowl, and a child cried out in the dark, and somebody in the long room began to sing a very soft, very old lullaby. Fuji listened to it with her chin on her fist. After a while she became aware that Yue was listening too, with her head tilted, absolutely still, like an animal.

"Do you know it?" she asked.

"Pipa used to play it," said Yue. "When I was small."

They sat in the yellow lamplight and listened, and neither of them noticed the smell.

It was faint at first. It came through the cracks in the shutter, thin and sweet and ominous, a smell like a hot summer afternoon. Fuji lifted her head. Yue's nostrils flared. Her pupils had gone suddenly, enormously wide.

"Oil," she said.

The word was still in the air when the long room began to scream.

Somebody had thrown a torch through the thatch.

It caught at once, with a dry roar, the new straw they'd laid six days before blazing up all along the ridge-pole in a bright orange sheet. Fuji was on her feet and through the door before she knew she'd moved. The long granary was a pit of noise and light, people running, bundles of bedding smoking, a child's thin wail somewhere in the smoke. She saw Qingpu, in the doorway, with her arms full of blankets. She saw an apprentice, wide-eyed, dragging a bucket across the floor. Above all of it the roof had begun to come down in long burning strips, and on the far side of the yard she saw, for the space of a single heartbeat, a dark figure on the wall, a woman in grey, with a small round cup of oil still in her hand.

The pheasant smiled at her.

Then the figure was gone, and the first great beam of the South Court's roof began, slowly, to come down.
