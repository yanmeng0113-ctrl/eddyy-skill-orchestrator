Chapter Twenty-Three: The Market Fire

The district magistrate swore on the life of his mother that he had never signed the warrant.

"It's a forgery," he kept saying, wringing his hands in the pre-dawn dark of the palace anteroom. He was a small plump man in a sleeping-cap, who had been dragged out of bed by six royal guards and still had one slipper on. "It's the seal of my office, Great King, I do not deny it, but the hand is not mine. The clerks swear no such slip ever passed through. Somebody has been in the seal-room. I'll have the whole staff flogged—"

"Where is she being held?" said Di Xin.

"I don't know! Nobody knows! The jail has no record, the watch has no record, the men in grey were not my men—"

"Then who gave the order?"

The magistrate's mouth opened and closed. It was perfectly obvious that he did not know, and that the not knowing frightened him a great deal more than the king did.

Yue stood against the wall in her brown coat with her hands pressed flat to the plaster and listened. She had not slept. Nobody had. She and Fuji and the king had sat up in the Counting Room through the whole of the long night, with the lamp low and the shutters closed, going over it again and again. A warrant under a forged seal. A notice in a clerk's perfect hand. A public burning, called for the very hour of the market, in the heart of the east quarter, where a crowd of ten thousand would be gathered to buy and sell and gossip. Let all who love the realm attend. There was no safe way to stop it. If the king's guards moved in force, it would be called a defense of demons, and the Zhong brothers' four hundred retainers were already said to be in the quarter, keeping the peace.

If they did not move at all, a woman would burn.

"Forty men," the king had said at last, quietly. "In plain dress. Spread through the crowd. They'll take her from the stake the moment the fire is lit, and no one will know until she's out of the square."

"And if someone sees?" said Fuji.

"Then someone sees."

"And if the fire isn't ordinary?"

Nobody had answered that.


* * *

The east market lay under a dazzling noon sky.

It was the last warm day before the deep of winter. The early snow had melted off the roofs and run down in gutters, and the whole quarter smelled of wet earth, and woodsmoke, and a hundred cooking pots. Stalls ran down the length of the main square in lopsided rows, hung with bright cloth and clay jars and strings of dried fish. Children darted between the legs of the crowd. A man was selling hot millet cakes from a tray. A woman was shouting the price of eels.

It would have been, Yue thought, a perfect day to be nobody.

She stood at the edge of the square, in the shadow of a wine-seller's awning, with Fuji at her elbow, close enough to feel the girl's shoulder tremble. The crowd was enormous. It filled the square to the walls and spilled out into the lanes, ten thousand people, perhaps more, in their coarse brown wool and straw sandals, jostling and craning, with the cheerful, restless, avid look of those who have come to see a show. At the far end, where the main avenue entered the square, a low wooden platform had been built, and on it stood a stake.

It was an ordinary stake. It had been planted in a heap of dry brushwood. Beside it a man in a grey robe was reading aloud from a bamboo slip. His voice was too soft to carry. But on the edge of the platform, a little to the side, in a patched cloak, with a long hooked fishing-pole planted in the boards beside him like a staff, stood a very old man.

He was quite small. He had a long white beard and a sunburned, weathered, patient face, with a thousand fine wrinkles round the eyes, the face of someone who has spent a great many years waiting for a very small thing to move. He carried a lantern. It was an ordinary lantern of horn and bronze, but the light inside it burned a strange pale clear gold, quite steady, quite cold, and it did not flicker in the wind.

Jiang Ziya, thought Yue.

She had imagined someone taller.

"There," breathed Fuji. "On the left. By the wine-seller."

They brought the pipa-player out through the crowd between four of the men in grey.

She was walking. She had on the faded blue gown. Her hair was loose and her wrists were bound in front of her with a cord of white hemp, and she carried her instrument cradled against her chest like a sleeping child. She did not look afraid. She looked, if anything, mildly interested, like a performer who has been asked to play an unfamiliar room. As she passed through the crowd, people shrank back, and stared, and spat. Someone threw a lump of dung. It struck her shoulder and she did not flinch.

"Demon!" shouted a voice.

"Burn it!"

"Foxes and lutes! Foxes and lutes!"

Yue had her hand over her mouth. She did not remember putting it there.

"Not yet," said Fuji, very low, in her ear. "Not yet, Yue. The king's men are in position. Forty of them. Look, on the left, the three in brown carrying a sack of millet. They'll move when the torch is lit." Her fingers had closed around Yue's wrist, hard enough to bruise. "Wait."

They put the pipa-player at the stake. They did not tie her. The guard in grey stepped back, and the woman in blue looked out over the sea of faces, and a sort of small smile crossed her long mouth. She lifted her instrument.

She played a chord.

It was a bright sweet careless chord, and it carried across the square like a bird across a pond. A few people in the crowd laughed, startled. The hubbub faltered. And the pipa-player, with every eye in the east quarter fixed upon her, raised her voice and began, in the full rich carrying contralto of a woman who has sung in a wine-shop for sixty years, to sing about a magistrate and a goose.

Nobody moved.

It was the most absurd thing Yue had ever witnessed. It was a bawdy, silly, shameless song, with a chorus that rhymed bottom with goat, and it was being sung from a stake in the middle of a market by a woman who was to be burned alive at midday. She sang it beautifully. She grinned between verses. By the second chorus there was a ripple of laughter running through the front ranks of the crowd, and by the third, to Yue's horror, a few people were singing along.

On the platform, the old man with the lantern lifted his head.

"Enough," said Jiang Ziya.

He did not raise his voice. It cut through the song like a knife through cloth.

"This woman is no woman. This lute is no lute." He stepped forward. His voice was mild and dry and quite unhurried, and it carried to the back of the square. "Good people of Chaoge, you have laughed at her song for sixty years. I should like you to see what you have been laughing at."

He raised the lantern.

The light went out across the platform, a thin pale gold ribbon. It touched the woman at the stake, and she cried out, a short, sharp, wounded sound, and her whole body flickered.

It was like watching a drawing being smudged. The blue gown rippled and ran. The long dark hair went stiff, and thin, and straight. The face went pale and strange and, for an instant, was no longer a woman's at all, but an old, delicate, grained face carved of fine dark wood, with a single curved eyebrow, and eyes of polished horn. The pipa in her arms, for the length of one dreadful heartbeat, was the same as her body.

The crowd screamed.

"Look!" said Jiang Ziya. "See her as she is! A thing of wood and string, four hundred years in the making, hiding in the heart of your city! What did she do with the child? What did she do with the king's good heart?" He swung the lantern. "A demon of the pipa. A spirit with no master. And there, good people, there is where the rot begins."

"Fuji," whispered Yue. "Fuji, he's going to—"

"I know."

"The king's men. Where are they?"

"I don't—" Fuji's voice broke. "There are too many people. They can't get through."

A torch came down.

It was a plain torch of pitch and rag, held by a man in grey, and he put it to the heap of brushwood at the foot of the stake. The brushwood caught at once with a soft greedy sound. The flames rose, yellow and bright, round the pipa-player's feet. The crowd roared. And in the middle of it, the thing at the stake looked straight across ten thousand heads and found Yue's eyes.

She was still smiling.

No, said her lips. Don't.

It was such a small shake of the head. It was the gentle, rueful, entirely unselfish negative of someone who has spent a long time looking after a stubborn child. And Yue, who had been told by a goddess that she could not help herself, found, in that moment, that it was quite true.

She tore away from Fuji's grip. She was through the crowd before she knew she'd moved, shouldering past the stalls, the carts, the people with their hands over their mouths. She was on her knees in the dust at the foot of the platform. The flames leapt up, and the heat struck her face like a hand. The woman at the stake was singing still, one broken note after another, and the strings of the burning pipa were snapping one by one.

Rain, she said. Rain. Please.

The seventh tail went up like a torch.

It did not feel like forgiveness this time. It felt like a wire drawn tight through her body, cut at the root. A cold white light burst across the sky and a black cloud rolled up in a heartbeat from nowhere, from the south, and the first heavy drops struck the dust. Ten thousand faces turned upward in the same moment, open-mouthed. A shriek went through the square. Stalls were overturned. Somewhere a child was crying.

The rain poured down on the stake.

It was the heaviest rain she had ever called. It hissed on the brushwood and drowned the torches and beat the smoke into the ground. And the fire did not go out.

It burned on. It burned in the heart of the rain, steady and clean and pale gold, in a slender column about the woman's feet, the color of the light in the old man's lantern. It did not hiss. It did not give off smoke. It was not fed by the brushwood at all. It ate the wood of the stake, and the hem of the blue gown, and the soft grained substance of the thing within it, and the rain fell through it like water through a lamp-flame and did nothing.

It's his, thought Yue. It's his fire. It's the lantern.

"I'm sorry," she sobbed. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

"Don't be," said the pipa-player.

She said it quite clearly. She was no longer singing. She stood in the golden fire with her head up and her hair streaming, a thing of wood and a thing of flesh and a thing, now, of nothing at all, and looked down at the girl on her knees in the mud. The fire had reached her waist. It had reached her shoulders. It lit her face from beneath. It was a very beautiful face.

"Be nobody, my moon," she said.

She touched a single string.

It was one clear note, low and warm and true. It hung in the air above the market for as long as it took the rain to fall. It echoed, once, in the hollow of the wine-seller's awning, and in the bronze of the lantern, and in the heart of a king who had just broken through the last rank of the crowd, bareheaded, with forty men in brown at his back, and stopped dead in the mud with his hand to his mouth.

Then the string snapped, and the fire went out.

There was nothing at the stake. There was a drift of fine grey ash, and a charred post, and a single curled sliver of dark wood the length of a finger, which the rain had already begun to wash away.


* * *

Yue did not remember getting up.

She was standing in the empty square, in the rain, which had thinned to a drizzle, with the king's arm around her and the king's voice in her ear saying something she could not hear. The crowd had scattered. The platform was deserted. The men in grey had melted away. There was only a woman in a brown coat, and the ash, and the quiet, and, on the edge of the boards, an old man with a lantern.

He had not moved. He stood there with his fishing-pole in one hand and his light in the other, and the strange pale gold of it shone on his white beard and the wet creased skin of his face. He looked, not triumphant, but tired. He looked like a man who has finished a long difficult day's work and does not particularly care for the result.

He was looking at her.

"I'm sorry for your loss, madam," said Jiang Ziya.

The king's arm tightened.

"She was a good musician. I had heard her many times. It's a pity." He lowered his lantern, and the golden light slid away across the ruined boards. "I take no pleasure in it. But the realm has been host to a great many things it ought not to. A lute. A pheasant. A hundred small creatures in the dark." His gaze moved, slowly, from the queen's pale wet face, to the king's, and back. "It's very difficult to count them all."

"Who are you?" said Di Xin.

"A fisherman, Great King." The old man smiled, a thin, courteous, melancholy smile. "I've come a long way to see your city. I find it a great deal more interesting than I'd expected." He paused. His eyes returned to Yue, to the wet brown coat, the seven pale hints of tail that were now, she realized with a lurch of horror, only six, and for the first time that day she saw something shift in his face, something grave and sorry and quite unmistakably kind. "There's one more," he said gently. "Isn't there?"
