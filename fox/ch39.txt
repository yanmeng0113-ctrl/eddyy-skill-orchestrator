Chapter Thirty-Nine: The Verdict

"It's me you want," said Yue. "Not him."

She had to say it twice, because the first time her teeth were chattering. She took her hand out of his sleeve. It was the hardest thing she had done all night, harder than the knife, and when it was done she stood alone in the frost on her own two feet, with the small cold stone still crushed in her fist, and looked up at a god.

It was a very long way up.

"Yes," said Nüwa. "I know."

Above them, in the dark, the lamp turned slowly. Nine wicks in a ring, five of them burning, four of them dead. It was a plain thing, small and bronze and a little battered, like a lamp in a farm kitchen. She had never seen it from outside before. She had spent four hundred years inside it, and it had never once occurred to her that it was so small.

"I am going to tell you three things," said the goddess, "and then I shall go. It is not a trial. I have already judged. I should like you both to hear it said aloud, because I am told that is how it is done."

"Go on," said Di Xin, hoarsely, from somewhere behind her left shoulder.

"The first is for the lamp."

Yue closed her eyes.

She had been afraid of a great many things in her long life, and she had been afraid of this one for four months. She had thought she knew what it would be. She had thought it would be a rush of fire, or a stroke of the knife. But when it came, it came as a kind of hush, as a sleeper feels a hand laid on her hair. There was a long cool touch at the base of her spine, quite gentle, quite impersonal. A pause. A faint, almost apologetic tug.

And then the pain came.

It was not like the broker's snip. It was nothing like the brief clean tick of the little bronze knife. It was as though a rib were being drawn out of her, slowly, with great care, by someone who was sorry. It was white and total and went on for a very long time, and somewhere in it she heard a high thin noise that was, she realized, her own voice. Her knees went. She did not hit the ground. A pair of arms had caught her, and a heart was going frantically under her ear, and a voice was saying Yue, Yue, Yue, over and over, in a tone she had not known he possessed.

"It is done," said Nüwa. "It will stop."

It did. It went away by degrees, like a tide, and left her lying in the king's arms in a wet cold daze. She opened her eyes. Above them in the dark, the lamp turned. There were four wicks burning now, in a ring.

"I am sorry for it," said the goddess, quite simply. "It was the one nearest your heart. I did not choose it for cruelty. A lamp that will not burn for its maker is a thing that must be trimmed."

"Four," said Yue, thickly.

"Four. That is all that I shall take." The flame-eyes did not move. "The second is for the fox."

Oh, no, thought Yue.

"You do not like the face you were given. You do not like the shape. You have worn two others in a season and found that you preferred a third." The long hand lifted and described, in the starlight, a slow, small circle. "I shall give you one more. It is not a gift. I know that it will not feel like one."

Something moved in the air.

She felt it settle, quite lightly, over the whole of her, like a light fall of snow. It had no weight. It left no mark. But she felt it go in, through her skin, down through the bones, into some small cold place at her center that had always been her own, and fold itself up, and lie quiet.

"You will be a woman only by the leave of the moon," said Nüwa. "When she is up, you will have hands. On the nights she is dark she is up all the same, only unseen, and her leave holds. When the sun comes, you will not. You may spend your days in whatever shape you please, so long as it is a fox's." A pause. "It is a leash. I do not pretend otherwise. But I find that I wish to know where you are. And I find that I do not wish you to be lonely."

"That's not a mercy."

"No." The goddess looked at her for a long moment. "But it is not a death. You may count it as one of the things I did not do."

"The third," said Di Xin.

He had not let her go. He was kneeling in the frost with her head against his shoulder, and his voice was quite steady. She felt it through his ribs.

"Yes," said Nüwa. "The third is for the king."


* * *

She turned to him. It took a long time. The great coil of her moved over the steps, slowly, with a sound like water over gravel, and she lowered her head until the flame-eyes were level with his own. It was a very strange thing to see. He did not flinch.

"The Mandate," said the goddess, "is a lease. It has always been one. It was given to the house of Shang on certain terms, and the terms were the altar. A king who feeds the gods may sit upon the throne. A king who does not—" She spread her hands. "I have held the scales a little on your side, for four hundred years, for my own reasons. I take my hand away tonight. It is not for me to say who has it next. But I will tell you what I see, because I think that you would rather know. It will go west."

"To the Zhou."

"To the Zhou. I do not say that they deserve it. They are a good deal worse than you, and a good deal better at it." The faint thin line came back to the corner of her mouth. "It will not be soon. A few years. A few more, perhaps, if you are careful. You will not be able to stop it. I think you know that."

"Yes," said Di Xin.

"And there is the poem."

He lifted his head.

"I did not write it," said Nüwa. "The verses on my wall. The lewd little rhyme, in your own hand, about my body. It was the pheasant's pen, and she is good at hands. I saw it the morning that it was written. I let it stand." She said it quite flatly. "I told myself that it would serve. A king who has insulted a goddess is a simple thing to explain. A king who has merely closed the altar, and been right, is a good deal harder. The histories will carry the poem. No one will remember the other reason. I am sorry for it."

"You'll let them write it?" Di Xin said. His voice was very low.

"I am a god, king of Shang. I am not the history." She straightened, and the starlight went through her. "It is the one thing that I cannot mend. I can only tell you that it was false, and that I know it, and that I think it ought to be remembered by somebody."

"By me," said Di Xin. "I'll see to it."

"I rather thought you would."

She stood, then, in the court, with the dark stars going on again behind her one by one, like lamps lit by a patient hand. She did not look at Yue again. She turned instead, a little stiffly, toward the foot of the steps, where a small figure in a peach-colored gown lay face down in the frost, with the nine bowed necks trembling in her sleeve.

"Come, child," said Nüwa.

Hu Ximei rose. She did not speak. Her face was perfectly white, and under the starlight it was the face of a woman who has just been told that her work was not required. Her gold eyes went once to Yue, and rested there, a long still moment, without any expression whatever. Then she went down the steps, and across the court, and into the dark, at her mother's heel, like a good dog.

She'll be back, thought Yue, quite calmly. She's not done.

The goddess went after her. At the edge of the court she paused, a tall slow shape against the stars, and half turned, and the long coil of her, where it lay across the frozen stones, had already begun to fade into the dark.

"Be kind to each other," said Nüwa. "I find that it is rather what is left."

And then there was only the court, and the tripods, and the cold.


* * *

It was a long time before either of them moved.

The stars went on coming out. The frost went on spreading, in fine white feathers, over the stones. At the far edge of the court, among the dark roofs, a light went up in the window of some servant's room, and a sleepy voice said something, and a door shut. Somewhere in the city, very faintly, a cock crowed, too early, and thought better of it.

They were sitting on the altar steps. He had put his night-robe around her, over her shoulders, and she had let him. She was very tired. It was a deep, clear, hollow tiredness, the sort a person feels at the end of a very long walk, and it was almost pleasant. She could feel the cold stone under her, and the arm around her, and, a little distance behind her, where the two lost ones had been, the broker's and the goddess's, an odd, light, flinching emptiness, like the place where a tooth has been.

"Count them," she said.

"What?"

"The tails. I can't turn round." She laughed, faintly, into his shoulder. "I'd like to hear somebody say it."

He was quiet. She felt him gather himself. She felt, very gently, the touch of a hand at the base of her spine, where something fine and soft and silver lay curled over the hem of the robe, and lightly, one by one, stroked each in turn.

"One," said Di Xin. "Two. Three. Four."

His voice cracked on the last.

"That's the loveliest number I've ever heard," said Yue.

"It's four."

"It's four," she agreed. "It's only a little less than nine. I've always rather liked even numbers." She turned her face up. She could see it very clearly, now, in the starlight, in the first grey beginning at the edge of the sky: a thin tired face, with the dry wit gone out of it, and a great deal left behind. "You were very rude to the goddess."

"I was extremely rude."

"She said you were right."

"I'd rather she'd said it sooner." His mouth moved. "It is an odd feeling. I've a great many unwritten arguments saved up, and I find that I've nothing left to put them in."

"You could put them in a book."

"Hm."

"A very dull book," said Yue. "With tables."

He kissed her. It was not like the night of the rite. There was no fear in it, and no flinching, and no hurry. It was a plain, steady, tired kiss, the kiss of two people who have come through something and found the other still there. She put her hand against his cheek, and the cold stone in it pressed against his jaw, and he did not seem to mind.

"I'll be able to do this at night," she said, against his mouth. "That's what she said. Only at night. In the moon's leave."

"I heard."

"You'll have to be very quick, in the mornings."

"I've always been very quick in the mornings."

"You have not." She was laughing. She was crying a little too. "You're the slowest man I've ever met. It's one of your best features."

She felt it before she saw it.

It was a very small thing. It was like a change in the weather, a faint warming of the air on her face. She opened her eyes. In the east, between the black roofs of the palace, a thin line of pale gold had come up, no wider than a finger. It touched the edge of the nearest roof. It touched the rim of the nearest tripod. It slid, quite slowly, across the frozen court, and the frost went up before it in a faint silver steam.

Oh, thought Yue. Already.

"Di Xin," she said.

"Yes."

"I think the moon's gone down. The one we couldn't see."

She felt him go rigid. His arms tightened. She felt the whole length of him, the hard lean wrists, the thudding heart, the long-fingered hand against her ribs, and she thought, quite clearly, I should like to remember this.

The light touched her hands.

It did not hurt. It was only very bright. She saw her fingers go pale, and then clear, like frost, and then fine as the dust that falls from a moth. She saw the long white hair lift and scatter into a thousand pale motes. She felt his arms close on empty air, and the robe slide, and something small and warm and quick go through her like a breath.

And then there was nothing in the king's arms, in the first grey light of the longest night's end, but a very small white fox with four tails, looking up at him.
