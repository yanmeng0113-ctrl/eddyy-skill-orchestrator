Chapter Eighteen: Three Names, One Ledger

Su Fuji had been standing outside the door of the Counting Room for the better part of a watch with a basin of water going cold in her hands, and she had come to a conclusion.

She was going to be sick.

It had nothing to do with the smoke. Her throat was raw from it, and her hands were blistered, and a long burn ran down her left forearm that Qingpu had dressed twice and told her to leave alone. But those were small, clean, honest pains, and she could carry them. What was making her stomach turn was the sound of two voices on the other side of a plank door, speaking very low, and the slow dreadful realization that she was about to be left out of a conversation about her own life.

He knows, she thought. He's known all along. He knew about Yue, and about the lamp, and about the feast. And in the middle of the night, in the kitchen corridor, with a hundred things to think about, she had suddenly remembered something that had made her go cold from scalp to heel.

Which is the maid's name?

She had not heard him say it. She had only heard Yue's story afterward, in a whisper, through the door. But it was in the way Yue had told it: He almost asked. And Fuji, who had lain awake every night for a month counting the ways in which she was going to be found out, saw very clearly that the last way was also the simplest. A king who counted things would sooner or later count one servant too many.

Behind the door, the voices stopped.

Why didn't you kill me, said the girl. And then, with a long pause, the man: Why haven't you killed me?

It was, Fuji thought, the most idiotic thing she had ever heard in her life. It was also, she thought, with a vague dismay, the most romantic. She shifted the basin to one hip, took a long breath, and knocked.


* * *

The Counting Room was full of the thin early light that makes everything look like a drawing of itself. The king was standing by the table. Yue was sitting on the cot, wrapped in a cloak, with her white hair all over her shoulders and her face the color of bleached bone. They were not touching. They were not looking at each other. They had the air of two people who have just run very hard in opposite directions and arrived at the same wall.

"I've brought a clean under-robe," said Fuji, "and water. The queen should wash."

"Thank you, Ling." The king's voice was perfectly steady. He had not moved. "Please come in. I think we've been waiting for you."

Waiting.

She set the basin down on the table, very carefully, so that it did not spill. She straightened. She looked at him: long, tired, ink-stained, with a three-day shadow along his jaw and a pair of eyes in which every thought was perfectly visible, like fish in clear water. He was not smiling. He was not frowning. He was watching her with the grave, courteous, expectant attention of a man at a negotiation who has already guessed the opening price.

It would have been very easy to lie. She had the whole speech prepared. I am a maid in the queen's service. I know nothing. I beg the king's pardon. It was a good speech. It would have worked for a day, or a week, or a month. Then one day it would have stopped working, in front of someone who mattered, and the whole house would have come down.

She said, "My name is Su Fuji."

The room went quiet.

"I am the daughter of Su Hu, Marquis of Zhongzhou. I left my father's house on the ninth of the autumn month, in a black carriage, with eleven carts and a lacquer box. I was meant to be your hostage." She heard her own voice, thin and level, a stranger's. "I was nearly murdered at the Enzhou post-station. I was saved by a fox. She asked if she might wear me. I said that I'd rather negotiate."

"I did wonder," said the king.

"I know you did. I'd like it on the record that I'd have told you sooner. But I wasn't certain what you were."

"And now?"

"Now I think you're the only man in Shang who reads his own tallies." She had meant to say it coolly. It did not come out coolly. "I think you've been paying a very high rent for something you can't afford. I think there's a fire in the south ward, and a boy named Ming, and forty-one names in a book, and I think you've seen at least some of them. And I think," her voice shook, and steadied, "that you'd have asked, if you had known how."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he crossed the room and stopped in front of her, close enough that she could smell lamp oil and old wood and the faint sharp tang of river mud on his sleeves.

He bowed.

It was not a king's nod. It was not even a courtier's. It was a long, slow, formal bow from the waist, hands joined, the sort of obeisance that a man gives to a person whose house he has walked into with muddy boots. She'd heard about it. She'd heard that he'd given it to a goddess.

"Lady Su," said Di Xin. "I owe you a very great apology, and I don't have a decent way to make it. You were sent here as a hostage by a father who didn't ask your leave. You were nearly burned alive. You were made to be a servant in a house where you ought to have been a queen. And you have, as far as I can tell, in a month, with a lacquer box and a tomcat, done more for the people of my south ward than my whole government has managed in a decade." He straightened. "I shall do what I can. It will be very little. But I should like to start, if you'll let me, with the truth."

"Which is?"

"That I'm very much afraid of you," said the king, "and that I'd be honored to be your ally."

Fuji could not think of anything to say. She stood there with her hands hanging empty at her sides, as a very strange thing happened in her chest, something that began as a pressure and became, quite slowly, a kind of release. It was not relief. It was the sensation of a person who has been carrying a very heavy tray for a very long time and has suddenly been told that she may put it down.

"There is a boy named Ming," said the king, "whom you'll want to meet."


* * *

He had been in the house of the Geng for ten days.

It was the king's own steward who had found him. Di Xin told it in short, dry sentences, pacing the narrow room with his hands behind his back, while Yue wrapped herself tighter in the cloak and Fuji perched on the edge of the table and tried not to hope. He had not believed the steward's account of the fever. A fever that killed a young clerk four days after he'd asked a series of awkward questions about the east holding was a fever with remarkable timing. So he had sent three men, in the dead of night, to the Geng grain-cellars, with a warrant for the inspection of seed-stock that nobody had ever thought to read.

They found a boy in the fourth cellar, chained by one ankle to a post.

"He's alive," said the king. "He's very frightened, and he has not eaten properly for a week, and he is currently in my kitchens being fed an enormous quantity of porridge by a cook who believes he is somebody's cousin. He has, I'm told, been talking without stopping since sunset." The corner of his mouth twitched. "He has a very good memory. He remembered where he'd hidden the slips. We have them. Seven names, with dates, and the place of the pit."

Fuji put her hand over her mouth.

"I told you he had died," said Di Xin, "because I wanted to see your face when I said it."

"That was cruel."

"It was. I'm sorry." He did not look sorry. He looked rather pleased with himself, and abashed about being pleased. "It was the only way I had of finding out who Ling was. A maid might have been sad. A queen's confidante might have been angry. You looked as though somebody had stolen from you." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I've been looking for somebody that looked like that for three years."

"Great King," said Fuji, in a small voice. "May I see him?"

"After breakfast. He won't talk to anyone until he's had breakfast. I've tried."

She laughed. It broke out of her in a wild, wet, startled gust, and she found that she was crying, and that she did not mind. She wiped her eyes on her sleeve. On the cot, in her borrowed cloak, Yue was looking at her with the stunned, shining expression of a child who has just seen a conjuring trick.

"All right," said Fuji. "All right. So. Three of us."

"Three," agreed the king.

"We should write it down."


* * *

It took the rest of the morning.

She wrote it herself, on a fresh bamboo slip, in the quick plain hand she used for accounts. The king dictated one clause, the fox another, and she corrected both. It was not a treaty. They had no word for what it was. It was a list.

One. No one of the three shall hand any other to the Office, the Zhou, or the goddess.

Two. The king shall hold the walls of the South Court, and pay for the roof, and defend its gate in law. The lady shall hold its book, and keep its door. Neither shall tell the other how to do it.

Three. The fox shall continue to wear the queen. She shall be at the king's side in the great hall, and shall give the cousin Ximei such proof of her decadence as will keep the goddess content, and the pheasant a little less suspicious.

"That's a very polite way of saying we're lying," said Yue.

"It's a lie that serves a truth. We'll call it a long pretense." Fuji dipped the brush. "Four. All three shall report to the others any matter touching the lives of the people of the South Court, the safety of the queen, or the movements of the goddess."

"And five," said Di Xin.

"What's five?"

"That we shall tell each other the truth." He was looking at the slip, not at either of them. "Even when it's inconvenient. Especially then. I've spent three years among people who tell me what they think I want to hear, and I'm not at all sure I could survive another."

"That's not a clause," said Fuji. "That's a hope."

"It's the only one I have."

She looked at him. Then, slowly, she wrote it down.

When the thing was done they signed it, each in turn: a wavering thumbprint from Yue, who had never had a name to put to anything, and a neat brushed Fuji in an old square hand, and at the bottom, in a quick, upright, hooked script, the name of the King of Shang. The slip was too small to hold all of it. They had to turn it over. Di Xin rolled it up and sealed it with his thumb, and put it inside the lining of his sleeve, where he kept the names.

"I'd like to say something solemn," he said. "I'm afraid I can't think of anything."

"Say you'll have the roof thatched," said Fuji.

"The roof will be thatched. By tomorrow."

He left them at last, with a sort of awkward bow to each, and went off down the corridor to the morning audience, a tall thin figure with ink-stained hands and a stoop in his shoulders that had not been there when he came in. The sound of his boots on the flagstones was slow and even, and then it was gone.


* * *

Fuji sat for a while on the edge of the table, without speaking, listening to the quiet.

She felt oddly light. It was the lightness of a person who has been walking uphill for a very long time, and has just learned that there is a small flat shelf at the top where one may sit down. She looked at her blistered hands. She looked at the bamboo slips that crowded the room from floor to ceiling, thousands upon thousands of them, ranged on their shelves like the ribs of some great, patient animal. And she looked at Yue.

The fox had not moved. She was sitting on the cot, bundled in the king's cloak, with her eyes on the doorway through which he'd gone. She was not smiling. She looked, in the new light, wide-eyed and absent, like a person who has been struck lightly on the head.

It was a very particular expression, and Fuji recognized it. She had seen it in the mirror, three nights ago, in the polished bronze of a water bucket, after a king had walked into a granary with two sacks of lentils.

"Yue," she said.

"Hm?"

"I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to be annoyed."

"I'm very tired. I'm not capable of being annoyed."

"Don't fall in love with him."

The fox's head turned. Her amber eyes were huge and sleepy and slightly dazed.

"I'm not," she said.

"Good." Fuji looked at the shelves. "Because he is going to lose. I've done the sums. A man who closes the pits and empties the temples and makes enemies of every house in the realm doesn't have long. The Zhou are in the west. Heaven's in the sky. The Office has a goddess. And all he has is a ledger." She heard her voice go quiet. "I don't think it will go well. And I think it would be a very bad investment, to love someone who is going to be written down as a monster."

"I know that."

"Do you?"

"I'm not stupid, Fuji."

"No," said Su Fuji. "You're not. That's what worries me." And she picked up the basin, and went to find a clean under-robe, and did not say that she was also speaking to herself.
