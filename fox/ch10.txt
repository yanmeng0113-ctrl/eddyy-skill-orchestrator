Chapter Ten: The Wine-Shop by the East Gate

The wine-shop had no sign. It had a lantern, a crooked doorway, a smell of spilled millet beer, and from somewhere deep inside it, over the noise of the evening crowd, the sound of a pipa being played by someone who was perfectly aware that she was the best thing in the room.

Yue stood in the lane outside with her hood pulled low and listened.

Fuji had not wanted her to come. It had been the longest argument of the week, conducted in whispers over a lamp, with the persimmon between them on the table like a piece of evidence. A broker offers you a name and you walk straight to it, Fuji had said. That is not a plan. That is bait. And Yue had said, She's my friend, in a voice that had startled them both, and Fuji had been silent for the space of three breaths and then said, Take the east gate, not the south. Go after dark. Do not wear my face.

So she wasn't. She wore her own: white hair bound up under a coarse brown hood, amber eyes, the plain wool coat of a washerwoman who had come for her husband. With eight tails folded under it she looked, she thought, like a person with a slight stoop. Nobody in Chaoge looked twice at a woman with a stoop.

She pushed open the door.

The shop was a long low room with a clay floor and a dozen rough tables, crowded with carters and clerks and off-duty soldiers, all bellowing for more wine. At the far end, on a raised stool beside the cask-rack, sat a woman in a faded blue gown with a pipa across her knee. She was perhaps thirty. She had a long laughing mouth and a mass of dark hair coming down on one side, and her fingers were doing something so quick and dissolute to the strings that half the room had stopped talking to watch.

The song was a bawdy one, about a magistrate and a goose. It ended on a rattling run of notes, and she looked up, grinning, as the applause broke.

She saw Yue.

The grin did not falter. That was how Yue knew she had been seen. A woman who had been surprised would have stumbled. The pipa-player simply let her last chord ring out, set the instrument down with great care, wiped her fingers on her skirt, and said, in the clear carrying voice of a performer to a drunk and affectionate crowd:

"My friends, it appears my sister has come at last, and I owe her a drink and an apology. You'll excuse me for a quarter-hour. Drink up, and heckle the goose."

She came down off the stool. She walked straight through the crowd, which parted for her like water, took Yue by the elbow, and steered her through the kitchen, past a startled cook, out the back door and up an outside stair to a small low room under the eaves, where she shut the door, put her back against it, and looked Yue up and down.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Hello, Pipa."

"You've lost a tail."

"I know."

"You've lost a tail, little moon, and you've been married, and you've come to my door after sixty years with a face like a stolen goat." The pipa-player's eyes, which were black and bright and fond, had filled slightly. She blinked it away. "Come here, you idiot."

She hugged her. It was a long hug, and Yue stood in it, stiff as a post, and felt something very old and very tightly wound come undone behind her ribs. She had not been held in sixty years. She had not noticed.

"I'm sorry about the river," she said into the blue gown.

"I'm not. I won." The pipa-player held her at arm's length. "Sit. I've wine, I've pickled radish, and I've been expecting you since the autumn rite. Something with nine tails was bound to turn up at the biggest executions of the year. The rest of the city thinks you were a comet."

"A comet?"

"There was a small white streak on the temple roof the night the pits were stopped. The priests saw it. They're calling it an omen." She poured, filled two cups with something thin and cheerful, and perched on the windowsill with one knee drawn up. "Now tell me everything. Why are you in the palace, why are you short a tail, and why does the entire sky taste of goddess?"

So Yue told her.

It took a long time. She told her about the rafters and the forged poem and the lamp with nine wicks, about the girl in the East Wing and the pheasant on the gatepost, the cup held under the base. She told her about the king. She told it badly, out of order, and the pipa-player listened without interrupting, her cup untouched on the sill, her expression changing slowly from fond to alert to something else.

"The man who closed the pits," she said, when Yue had finished. "That one."

"That one."

"And she's ordered you to unmake him."

"By wearing his wife."

"And the wife, the real one, the clever one with the sums, is in the palace as a maid." The pipa-player let out a long, low, appreciative breath. "That's the most absurd arrangement I have ever heard. It might even work."

"It's going to get me killed."

"It's going to get you something." She slid from the windowsill and crouched in front of Yue, so that their eyes were level. "Look at me. You're not telling this as a mission. You're telling it as a story. You've got a king who counts the dead, and a girl who gave you wine, and you keep saying 'she' and 'he' as if they had names." Her voice softened. "Yue. When did you last tell me about anyone with a name?"

"They don't have names. They have roles."

"You called them by their names three times. Fuji. Di Xin. Even the uncle." The pipa-player tapped Yue's knee with one finger, gently. "I haven't heard you say a mortal's name since the Duke of Yan. And you were quite cruel to the Duke."

Yue opened her mouth to argue and found there was nothing in it.

"What do I do?" she said.

It was the first time she had ever asked anyone that. It sounded very small in the low room.

The pipa-player was quiet for a long moment. Down in the shop someone was singing, many voices, out of tune, the goose song.

"I think," she said carefully, "you do the thing that frightens you most, which is to decide for yourself. But you do it slowly. And you stay off the streets after dark, and you stay out of sight of anyone from the west with a fishing rod and a grudge. There's a man in the city. An old one. He's been asking at the wine-shops about whiter-than-white foxes, and he's got a lantern with a peculiar light." She grimaced. "He's been three times to this shop. I serve him. I play the goose song. He doesn't laugh."

"Jiang Ziya."

"Is that his name? He wouldn't tell me. He just sat in the corner with a very patient face and watched my hands." She rubbed her arms, as if cold. "I felt like a fish."

"Then you should leave Chaoge."

"I've a contract. And a following." The pipa-player grinned, though it had a thin edge. "And, being honest, I've no particular wish to leave. It is the only city I have ever lived in where the king pays the music-houses on time. Somebody finally counted the arrears." She winked. "Rumor says it was a very conscientious man."

Yue felt her face go warm in the dark, and was furious about it.

They talked until the lamp burned low. The pipa-player played her something soft, a few bars of an old river song, and Yue lay back on the floor with her eyes shut and let it wash over her, as she had done sixty years ago on a bank in the rain. It was the loveliest thing she had heard since the autumn.

When the song ended, the pipa-player said quietly, "There's one more thing. I'm sorry. I ought to say it first."

"What?"

"The pheasant."

Yue sat up.

"Hu Ximei came to my door a few days ago, before you ever got here. She dropped in, quite casually, bringing a jar of plum wine. We talked about you." The pipa-player had gone still, one hand flat on the pipa's belly. "She was extraordinarily kind. She asked after my health. She told me about the new queen who would be arriving, and how the poor lamb would need friends. And I was a fool, Yue. I told her you'd always loved the river songs. I told her you'd come to find me, the moment you could."

"Pipa—"

"She knew you'd come here. She's known all along. She's been waiting for you to walk through that door." The black eyes were bright and steady. "And I am so terribly sorry. Because if I know my pheasant, she's not far behind you."

Below them, in the shop, the goose song broke off in the middle of a verse. The room went quiet. Not the quiet of a crowd that has run out of breath, but the dense, listening quiet of forty drunk people who have all, at once, noticed a woman standing in the doorway.

A woman in grey. With a smile like a fresh-cut peach.

"There you are," said a sweet voice, from the foot of the stair. "I've been looking everywhere. Cousin! Do come down. I've told your little friends all about you."

And through the thin boards of the floor, soft as a feather, came the sound of Hu Ximei humming the goose song.

Yue got slowly to her feet.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"In my own house? Not a chance." The pipa-player picked up her instrument by the neck, like a weapon, and set her shoulders. "Come on. If we're going to be murdered, I'd rather it was in front of customers."

They went down the stair together.

Hu Ximei was standing in the middle of the shop with a cup of beer in her hand, surrounded by half the room. A carter was telling her something earnest about his mother. A young clerk had given up his stool. She had been in the building perhaps a hundred breaths, and already she was the center of it, laughing at all the right places, her grey clothes plain and her smile warm and her gold eyes moving through the faces like a woman counting coins.

"Cousin!" She set down the cup. "There you are. I came to fetch you home, and found your friend's customers keeping me company. Such nice people." The carter beamed. "You shouldn't walk alone after dark, you know. Not in the capital. A queen has no business in an alley."

"I'm not a queen tonight. I'm a washerwoman."

"A very well-spoken one." Hu Ximei's smile did not change. She reached out, took Yue's hand, and pressed something into it. It was cold and small and square. "I've news, dear. The king's uncle, the kind old Prince Bi Gan, thought it only proper that a lonely bride should have family near. He's had a room made ready for me in the queen's wing. The east chamber, just beside yours." Her fingers tightened gently. "The wall is very thin, I'm told. We'll hear each other breathe."

In Yue's palm lay a small bronze seal on a cord. It bore the mark of the Prince Bi Gan's household.

"How thoughtful," said Yue.

"Isn't it?" Hu Ximei turned to the pipa-player and sank into a small bow, a pretty, deferential little dip, the courtesy of a well-brought-up girl to a woman of talent. "And you, madam. I've heard you are the best in the city. The queen is so fond of music. I shall see that you're asked to play at the palace, one day soon. It would be such an honor."

"I'd be delighted," said the pipa-player pleasantly, in the voice of a woman who would rather eat her own strings.

"Wonderful." Hu Ximei squeezed Yue's hand once more and let go. "Shall we?"

They walked back through the dark streets of Chaoge, side by side, under a thin moon, with a pair of Hu Ximei's new friends from the shop trailing a few steps behind to see them safely home. Neither of them spoke. The cousin hummed to herself, under her breath, a tune that Yue knew very well. It was the goose song. She hummed it all the way to the palace gate.


* * *

Fuji was waiting at the lattice screen with a lamp, her face pale and tight. She took one look at Yue's expression and drew her inside by the sleeve.

"Tell me."

Yue told her, in twelve sentences, in the dark, with her back against the door. The pipa-player is alive. The Zhou have a spy in the city. The pheasant has been here all along. The pheasant is going to be sleeping in the next room.

"The east chamber." Fuji said it very slowly. "By the king's uncle's order."

"By Bi Gan's seal."

"Then Bi Gan wants her there. Or he believes he does." She let out a long breath. "He tested you this morning. He doesn't trust you, and he's found the nearest thing he has to a leash. A cousin who is truly family. Who can watch. Who will report."

"She is going to report to a goddess."

"I know. But he doesn't." Fuji pressed her hands to her eyes for a moment. "We've two enemies now, and one of them thinks the other is a friend. It's almost funny."

"It isn't funny."

"No." Fuji lowered her hands. There was a hard line at the corner of her mouth that Yue had not seen before, and under it, unexpectedly, a gleam. "But it is very, very instructive. Everyone in this palace has someone watching them. Everyone is keeping a list. We have been terribly innocent."

Yue sat down on the edge of the platform and put her face in her hands.

On the other side of the wall, quite close, came the sound of a door opening. A bundle was set down. A lamp was lit. There was a creak, a rustle, the contented small sigh of a woman settling in.

And then, very softly, through the thin plaster, a voice began to hum a song about a magistrate and a goose.
