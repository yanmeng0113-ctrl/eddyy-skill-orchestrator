Chapter Thirty-Four: The King's White Fox

Di Xin had never in his life been asked to carry a secret in front of two thousand people, and he discovered that the trick of it was to stop being afraid and start being tedious.

"Yes," he said.

The hall, which had been holding its breath, let it out all at once.

"Yes. There is a fox in my sleeve." He said it in the same dry, patient tone in which he had once read out a grain-return to the Office of Sacrifice. "I should have said so before. I did not, because I judged it would cause a fuss, and I find fusses expensive." He drew his left arm up, slowly, until it rested across his chest. "Preceptor, I should be grateful if you would let me introduce her properly. She's rather shy."

There was a faint, indignant stir at the cuff.

He eased the wool back. For a moment nothing happened. Then a small white head with large amber eyes and a nose like a damp black bead peered out, looked around the hall of Shang with an expression of the deepest suspicion, and sneezed.

It was a very small sneeze. It was also, in the dead silence, the most audible sound that Di Xin had ever heard.

Somebody in the third rank of the nobles snorted. It was a very faint sound, like a man trying to cough and failing. Somebody else, further back, made the same sound. In the space of five breaths a ripple of helpless, horrified laughter had run through the whole hall, half the nobles of Shang, in their furs and their bronze, hunched over their mats with their hands over their mouths, shaking.

The fox glared at them.

"You see," said Di Xin gravely. "She's offended."

He lifted her out of the sleeve. She came without protest, a small warm armful of white fur and bone, with five tails hanging from her haunch like a bunch of wet feathers, and settled in the crook of his elbow with an air of icy dignity. She did not look at the hall. She looked, with great deliberation, at a point just above the Preceptor's left shoulder, as though the whole assembly were beneath her notice.

"She is not a demon," said Di Xin. "She is a creature that followed the Lady Su from the hills of Zhongzhou. She did what the Lady Su has told you, and a good deal more. She has put out a fire, and held off a storm, and sat up with me on the worst nights of my reign. She has a bad temper and no manners and she once ate a whole roast duck from the royal kitchens." He heard his own voice soften. "I should like it to be known that she is under my protection. And that I am extremely fond of her."

The fox, very slightly, leaned against his chest.


* * *

It was Lord Geng who found his voice first.

"This is a mockery." He had risen, shaking, the color of a boiled beet. "A fox. A beast. In the king's own arm, in the great hall, before the Preceptor himself. It is a creature of darkness and it has taken a woman's face." His heavy jowls trembled. "It lay in the king's bed for four months. Great King, I appeal to you, in the name of every house of Shang. Give it to the Office. Let the priests deal with it."

"Yes!" The elder Zhong was on his feet too. "Burn it!"

"Burn it!"

Di Xin did not move. He felt the fox go stiff in his arm, and laid his hand, very lightly, on her back, and felt a faint, fierce, sudden tremor run through the small body. Don't, he thought. Not here. Let me.

"I think," said a quiet voice, "that we might ask the man who saw."

It was Wen Zhong. He had risen from his knees. He stood at the foot of the dais with his helm in his hands and his face drawn and grey, and with his two good eyes red and swollen. He had pulled the black band back across his brow. It did not quite hide the fine line, still faintly pink, that ran between his eyebrows. He looked ten years older than he had that morning. He looked perfectly steady.

"My lords," he said. "I am about to tell you what the Eye found. It is not a long account."

The hall fell still.

"I looked at the Lady Su. She is a woman, and a good one. I looked at the king. He is a man, and a better one than any of you deserve." There was a faint rasp of dry humor in his voice. "And I looked at the animal."

Di Xin felt his heart miss a beat.

"It is a fox," said Wen Zhong. "It is an old one. It is, in a certain sense, a creature of the goddess Nüwa, who made it, and who for her own reasons has set it upon a task it did not wish to perform." His eyes went to the white shape in the king's arm, and for a moment something moved in them that was very much like grief. "I will not tell you more. It is not my secret. I will tell you what you have a right to know: that it has done no harm. That it has no malice. That I have seen the whole of it, from root to tip, and I have not found one cruel thing."

"A fox is a fox!" shouted Lord Geng.

"A tiger is a tiger, my lord. I have met a few that were kinder than some men." The old man's voice was perfectly level. "I am the Grand Preceptor of Shang. It is my office to see what is, and to speak. I have seen. I have spoken. If any lord in this hall wishes to dispute the word of the Heavenly Eye, he may do so to my face, with whatever weapon he cares to bring." He paused. "I will be at the northern gate. I have fifteen hundred horse."

It was very quiet.

The younger Zhong brother sat down, quite suddenly, as though his knees had been cut. After a moment the elder followed. Lord Geng stood a little longer, his mouth working, and then, with a faint wet sound, subsided.

The priest of the Office had risen too, the plump damp-lipped one in ash-white sleeves, who had sat alone on the left all afternoon with the look of a man at the wrong banquet. His voice cracked. "The temple's interdict, Great King. The Master of Sacrifices has laid it upon any creature of the goddess found in the capital without her leave. By the law of the Office, the fox is to be surrendered—"

"The Master of Sacrifices has not left his temple in a month," said Wen Zhong, without turning his head. "If he wishes to lay a claim, he may come and lay it. In person. I should be glad to see him."

The priest opened his mouth, looked at the old man's black brow-band, and sat down.

"Then it is closed," said the king.

"It is closed," said Wen Zhong. "For today."

He turned. He walked up the three steps of the dais, with the slow, careful, over-correct gait of a man who has been awake for a very long time. He stopped in front of Di Xin. He looked at him. Then, quite deliberately, in front of the whole court, he bowed. It was not the short correct bow of that morning. It was the old, deep, full obeisance of a soldier to his king, with his helm held out at arm's length and his grey head bent.

"I have a duty to you," he said, very low, so that only the dais could hear. "I have a duty to Shang. Today, they pointed the same way. I do not promise they always will."

"I know."

"The goddess will not be satisfied with a verdict." The old eyes lifted. They were full of tears. "Look to the solstice, boy. And look to the little fox. She's much braver than you'll want her to be."

He turned, and went down the steps, and out through the great doors, into the white sunlight, with his hands loose at his sides. After a moment, the nobles began, slowly, one by one, to rise.

Di Xin held out his free hand to the girl at the foot of the dais. She came up the three steps, a plain tall woman in a grey gown, and he led her to the small lacquered chair with the carved ducks. She hesitated before it. Then, quite slowly, with her father watching from the door, she sat. It was the first time in her life she had sat there in her own name. She looked, to his eye, exactly as she had looked when she corrected a column of figures: calm, a little tired, and very slightly pleased.


* * *

It was after dark when the last of them had gone.

The great hall was empty, and cold, and smelled of snuffed lamps. Di Xin sat on the lowest step of the dais with his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands. He had not moved in an hour. He did not think he could. Beside him lay a small white fox, curled on the matting with her nose under her tails, asleep, or pretending to be. At the far end of the hall, by the door, the Marquis of Zhongzhou sat on a stool with his daughter's head on his shoulder, very still. They had said nothing for a long time.

"I think it went well," said Di Xin.

The fox opened one eye.

"No, truly. Consider the arithmetic. We lost no one. We did not have to burn anyone. The court laughed. The Preceptor has put his word behind you, which is worth a legion." He rubbed his face. "I am aware that he said 'a demon in your arm' and that for one moment I wished very much to be dead. But on the whole."

The fox made a small, dry, rueful sound. It might have been a laugh.

"You were very brave," he said.

She got up. She stretched, with enormous contempt, first the front legs and then the back, and walked across the matting with her five tails drooping, and climbed into his lap, and turned around three times. She lay down. She put her chin on his knee.

He put his hand on her head.

It was the most extraordinary thing. A creature as old as the hills, a creature that had been named by a pipa-player in the rain, lay in his lap and let him scratch behind her ears. After a time, her body relaxed. After a longer time, something thrummed under his palm, a small deep vibration, like a stew-pot coming to the simmer.

She's purring, he thought. She'd kill me if I said so.

"There's a thing I want to say," he said, very softly, "and I'd rather say it while you can't answer."

The tail twitched.

"I don't know what's going to happen at the solstice. I haven't the least idea. I've been working on it for six days, and the best I can do is a list of things that won't work." His fingers moved in the white fur. "But I want you to know that if the lamp goes out, I'd rather it went out with me standing next to it. I've no right to ask. I'd just like you to have the option."

She did not move. But the purr, under his hand, deepened.


* * *

He walked her home through the dark galleries, at the third watch, with the fox over his shoulder like a scarf and Fuji beside him with a lamp.

It was very quiet. The palace was asleep. They passed the long corridor with its row of bronze lamp-stands, the guard post, the garden gate. At the turn of the stair, a pair of kitchen girls were coming up from the cellars with an armful of linen, giggling together in the whispery, breathless way of girls who ought to be in bed. They stopped when they saw the king. They bobbed. They giggled again, and ran.

"—so she is," one of them said, in a stage whisper, from the safety of the next landing. "My cousin's husband's brother was in the hall. He said the Preceptor saw it, plain as day. With the Eye."

"The queen's a fox?"

"That's what he said! He said the queen of Shang is a fox. That she's been one all along, and the king's in love with her—"

The voices faded down the stair.

Fuji had stopped. She stood with the lamp held out in front of her, quite still, and in the light her plain face was very white. On Di Xin's shoulder, the fox had lifted her head. There was a long silence.

"Well," said Di Xin at last, with a great weariness. "I did say that a fuss was expensive."

"Yes," said Fuji. She began, slowly, to smile. It was the oddest smile he had ever seen on her. It was thin, and bright, and dangerous, the smile of a woman who has just seen a column of figures resolve itself into an answer. "Yes, you did. And do you know, I believe I've just thought of a way to make it pay."
