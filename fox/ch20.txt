Chapter Twenty: The Winter Rite

The offering was a girl of about fourteen, and she was walking to the altar on her own feet, which was somehow the worst part.

Yue had expected a struggle. She had braced for screaming, for ropes, for the dead drag of a body that does not wish to go. But the girl in the white robe came up the long stone path of the Hill of Nüwa between two priests with her head bent and her hands folded, quiet as a child going to be washed. Someone had braided her hair. Someone had pressed a sprig of late chrysanthemum behind her ear. She was very small. She had a long thin face and the large, dull, uncomprehending eyes of a creature that has been told something so many times it has stopped being a fact.

Consecrated to the goddess's acre, Yue thought. Of the Geng east holding.

The whole court was drawn up on the slope below the temple, in their furs and their bronze, under a pale hard morning sky. The air was bitter. In the hall behind the altar the painted face of the goddess looked out over the heads of the crowd, calm and enormous, the long crack down her cheek filled with fresh plaster.

At the foot of the altar stood the Master of Sacrifices, in white, with a bronze knife in his hands.

She asked that the queen, in her own person, should bring the offering. It was a cruelty so elegant, so exactly pitched, that Yue, standing on the king's right in her robe of state with a lacquered ritual rope draped across her palms, could almost admire the craft. Not the pheasant's work. It had the cold unhurried finish of the goddess herself. You will wear her place. You will do her office. And you will lead the lamb.

The rope trembled in her hands. She made herself hold it steady.

"Great King," said Yuan Shi. He had come to the foot of the steps. His voice was low and clear and carried. "Great Queen. We are gathered, at the goddess's word, in the ancient manner. I ask that the rite be accepted."

"I accept the gathering," said Di Xin.

He was standing very straight, in a robe the color of dried blood. His face was quite expressionless. It was a long, pale, tired, closed face, and Yue, who had watched it for almost three months, could see the fine tremor in the muscle at the corner of his jaw.

"And the offering?"

"I'll look at her first."

There was a stir along the ranks. The girl was brought forward. She stopped at the king's feet, and folded herself in a kind of bow, and stayed there, with her eyes on the ground.

"What is your name?" said Di Xin.

"Mei, Great King."

"Where are you from?"

"The Geng. The east holding."

"How long have you lived there?"

"All my life." A very small voice. "Since I was born."

"And did you choose this?"

There was a long silence.

"I was chosen," said the girl, carefully, in the tone of someone repeating a lesson.

Di Xin nodded. He did it slowly, twice, the way a man nods at a figure in a column that has just come out exactly as he had feared. Then he turned, and for the first time that morning he looked at Yue. It was brief. It was only a glance. But she caught in it something she had not seen on any mortal face before, a look of enormous, naked, terrible apology, as of a man who is about to ask someone he loves to hold something very heavy.

Oh, she thought. No.

"Master Yuan," said the king, in a voice that carried to the back of the crowd. "I'm obliged to you for coming. I am sure the goddess will forgive me for being slow. I've been told I'm a very slow man. It's the arithmetic."

"Great King?"

"I should like, before the rite proceeds, to read a list."

He put his hand into his sleeve and brought out a bundle of bamboo slips, bound in black cord. A whisper rose and ran along the ranks like wind through a field. Yuan Shi's face did not change, but his knuckles whitened on the handle of the knife.

"I have here," said Di Xin, "seven names. A woman called Pan, aged thirty-four. A man called Zhu, aged twenty-eight. A boy called Lan, who was nine. Three others, and a girl, who was eleven." He did not read them quickly. He read each one, with its age and its trade and the date, in his clear dry carrying voice, like a clerk reading a grain-return, and the hush that fell over the slope was so complete that Yue could hear the dry rattle of the plum branches in the temple yard. "They were all of the Geng east holding. They were all consecrated to the temple acre. And they all died, according to the book of that holding, of a fever, this year."

"Great King," said Yuan Shi, very quietly. "This is not the place—"

"It is a very good place. It's the temple. It's where they were sent." The king had not raised his voice. "They didn't die of fever. I have it from the clerk who kept the book, who was, until this autumn, chained to a post in the fourth cellar of the Geng granary. I'd be happy to produce him. They were taken at night to an old pit behind the granary, by the steward of that holding, on the order of the lord of that house, under the cover of the rites. Three years after I closed the pits. Three years after I forbade it, by edict, under my own seal."

A sound went through the court, a long sigh, like the sea drawing back from a beach.

Lord Geng had gone the color of whey. He was standing in the front rank, among the great nobles, with his mouth slightly open. His steward was nowhere to be seen.

"I did not know," said Yuan Shi.

It came out of him in a whisper. It was not an excuse. It was a thing a man says to himself, in the dark, when the floor opens under him.

"I know you didn't," said Di Xin. He did not say it unkindly. "That's why I'm saying it here, Master. Not at court. I think you're an honest man. I think you've been lied to by the people who feed on your honesty. I'd like you to hear it from me, in front of the goddess, and judge for yourself what she'd make of the thing that's been done in her name." He turned to the girl on the ground. "Mei. Stand up."

She stood. She was shaking.

"Mei is from the Geng east holding. She was chosen. By whom?" His eyes went to Lord Geng. "I can tell you what the goddess's acre has produced, in three years: a very great deal of millet, a great many sacrifices that the book says were fever, and a girl of fourteen in a white robe who's been told since birth that it is an honor to be killed."

He took the bronze knife from Yuan Shi's hands.

It was done so quickly that no one stopped him. He plucked it from the priest's fingers as a man might pick up a dropped tool, and for one dreadful instant Yue thought that he meant to use it. The ranks gasped. The plump priest cried out.

Di Xin cut the girl's wrist-cords.

"Go home, Mei," he said. "To the South Court. Ask for a woman called Ling. She'll give you something to eat." He laid the knife on the altar. "The rite is not accepted."


* * *

The sky went black.

It happened all at once, with no warning, like a lid being lowered on a pot. One moment the morning was blue and thin and cold; the next, it was as though a great hand had swept over the world and wiped it clean of light. The wind came screaming down from the hills. It tore at the robes of the nobles, and the banners, and the white sprig of plum from the girl's hair. In the temple a dozen bronze bells began to ring by themselves.

"Heaven's wrath," shrieked someone.

"It's the goddess!"

"She's angry, she's—"

The first bolt struck the slope not fifty paces from the king.

It was white and blinding and absolutely silent for the space of a heartbeat, and then the thunder came, like a wall falling on the world. Men screamed. Horses bolted. A great ancient pine on the hill's shoulder split from crown to root and fell in two burning halves. The ranks broke.

Mother, thought Yue.

She did not choose it. She had never chosen any of it. She was simply standing there, in the robe of a queen, with the lightning in her eyes, and there was a place inside her, between her ribs, where seven small cold flames burned steady and white. She turned her attention to them, and to the storm, and she reached.

It was an enormous thing. It was like putting one's hands up to stop a falling cliff. The storm came down on her all at once, a great black weight of rage with an old, pitiless, familiar shape behind it. For a moment, in the roar, she could feel Nüwa's attention on her like the touch of a cold stone.

Not them, she said.

Little lamp, said the storm. You are in the way.

I know. I'm sorry. Not them. Not here.

She did not do anything that a mortal could see. She did not call fire or move the earth. She only leaned. She set her will against the black wall of the sky as a girl sets her shoulder against a door, and held. The wind did not stop. The thunder did not cease. But the next bolt, which should have struck the altar, struck an empty field a quarter-mile off. The cloud, which had been gathering directly above the king's head, drifted away to the east. And when the rain finally came it fell in a long hard slanting sheet on the river, a mile from the Hill of Nüwa, where there was nobody at all.

A storm that goes around, she thought, dizzily, is only weather.

That was the trick of it. A mortal who sees a wonder pays a tail. A mortal who sees a storm that happens to miss them has seen nothing but luck. She held it for as long as she could, while her knees began to shake and the blood rose in her nose. Then the pressure lifted. It went out of the world like a long drawn breath.

The sky was blue again. The wind had dropped. A few torn scraps of cloud drifted over the western hills.

Somewhere on the slope, a child began to laugh with sheer shocked relief.

Seven, she thought. Still seven. It was a thing to cling to. She wiped the blood off her lip with the back of her hand and found that her fingers were shaking too hard to close.


* * *

It was past midnight when he found her on the Deer Terrace.

She had gone there because she did not know where else to go. She had climbed the frozen steps alone, with her queen's face coming off in her hands like wet clay, and lain down on the cold stone bench at the top, looking up at a sky full of stars with no trace of a storm in it.

"I was told you were here."

She did not turn her head. "Who told you?"

"The guard. He seemed to think I ought to be worried." Di Xin came and sat down on the end of the bench, a thin dark shape in the starlight, a careful arm's length away. "I am. It seems to be a permanent condition."

"I'm all right."

"You have blood on your collar."

"It's nothing. It stopped."

"Yue."

She turned over and looked at him. His face was bare in the starlight, tired and grim and exposed. He had taken off his heavy robe of state and sat in his under-robe, his hands hanging between his knees, ink on the right one, a long fresh scrape across the left.

"You held it," he said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The storm. I stood under it. I felt it come down, and then I felt it turn." His voice was very low. "I thought: well, that's that. I've insulted a god in front of the whole court, and it's quite reasonable that she should kill me. And then it simply went around. As if it had been told." He looked at her. "Nobody else saw it. They thought it was luck. I knew."

"It's not a wonder if nobody sees it."

"I saw it."

"You don't count. You knew already, and you never once decided I was a demon's work. You still haven't."

"Is that the rule?" There was something almost like laughter in his voice, thin and unsteady. "I'll remember it. I've always wanted to be exempt from something."

Neither of them spoke for a time. The wind moved over the stones with a soft dry sound.

"You said to ask you at the full moon," said Di Xin.

She sat up.

"It's the full moon," he said. "Strictly it's past midnight, so it's the day after. But I'm told the astrologers are generous with these things." He was looking at the parapet. "I'd like to ask you something. I'd rather you didn't answer, if you don't want to. I'm a man with a very short future and a very long list of enemies, and I am, as of this morning, the object of a goddess's undying hatred."

"Ask."

"The star. The one that governs weddings." His voice was tight and steady, as though he were reading a very difficult figure aloud. "Was it worth it?"

Ask me at the full moon, she had said. And now it was the full moon, and he had asked.

She had a speech. She had practiced it for days. She opened her mouth, and what came out, in a voice she hardly recognized, was a very small, cracked, honest word.

"Yes."

He closed his eyes.

"It was worth it," she said. "It was always going to be worth it. That's the worst of it. I came to ruin you, and I didn't, and I'll very likely die for it, and I'd do the whole thing again." She laughed unsteadily. "I'm a very bad fox. I've never been so happy."

When he kissed her, it was very gentle. It tasted of cold and ink and a little of blood, and she forgot to be afraid.

She did not see the shadow on the stair.

It was a thin shadow, at the very bottom of the steps, where the torchlight did not reach. It was a pheasant's shadow, with a great many necks. And every one of them, in the starlight, was turned toward the roof of the Deer Terrace, watching, with a cool, tender, avid, patient smile.
