Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Prisoner Called Ling

The city prison of Chaoge had been built, as far as Fuji could tell, by someone who disliked light.

It was a long low block of rammed earth, set against the north wall, with a single bronze-bound door and a row of slit windows too narrow for a hand. The cells ran along either side of a central passage. They were six feet by eight, floored with packed clay, and each held a pallet, a bucket, and a smell. Hers was the last on the left. A thin stripe of daylight fell through the slit above the pallet and moved, slowly, across the opposite wall as the hours went by. By the end of the first day she had learned to tell the time by it.

By the end of the third she had counted the stones.

There were two hundred and sixteen in the east wall. Some of them were cracked. One had a scratch on it, three short strokes, as though a previous occupant had been keeping a tally of his own. She did not know what he had been counting. She had decided, in the end, that it was days, and that he had not got very far.

It's good practice, she told herself. It's the thing you're best at.

It did not stop her from being afraid.

She had not been mistreated. That was a surprise. She had expected the lash, or the cold, or the dark. Instead they gave her two bowls of millet gruel a day, a blanket that smelled of other people, and a jailer, a sullen young man with a broken tooth, who left the gruel at the door and said nothing at all. She was questioned once, on the first night, by a priest of the Office with a damp, soft, sorrowful face. He had asked her about the mound. She had said that she knew nothing of it. He had asked about the charm. She had said that she'd never seen it before. He had asked, gently, in the tone of a man humoring a child, whether the queen kept dark company. She had said that the queen kept a cat.

He had written it down.

She had no way of knowing what was happening outside. She could only imagine. She imagined the king, in the Counting Room, going through the warrant slip by slip with his long ink-stained hands, looking for the flaw. She imagined Yue, pacing. She imagined Qingpu, holding the ledger, and the sixty people of the South Court sitting in the long hall with their hands in their laps and their eyes on the door.

It's all right, she had said. She had meant it. She was beginning to think it had been a lie.


* * *

On the fourth evening there was a knock on the wall.

It came from the cell on the right. It was soft, and quite deliberate: three short raps, a pause, two. Fuji lifted her head from her knees. The stripe of light had faded to a thin orange line and the passage beyond her door was in shadow.

Three, two.

She hesitated. Then she lifted her own hand, and rapped the stone, once.

A voice came through the wall. It was a man's voice, hoarse and cracked and surprisingly cheerful. It sounded as though it had not been used for a very long time and was delighted to be put to work.

"You're the new one," it said. "The pretty maid."

"I'm not pretty."

"You're a woman. At this end of the passage, that's the same." A dry chuckle. "They put you in the good cell. The one with the stripe. Last fellow in it went mad by the second week."

"I'm very sorry to hear it."

"Oh, don't be. He was a murderer." The voice paused, considering. "What are you in for?"

"Kidnapping."

"Ha! Who'd you kidnap?"

"Three babies. Allegedly." She leaned her head against the cold stone. "I haven't been able to find them yet."

The man on the other side laughed until he coughed. When he had recovered, he said, in a tone that was almost respectful, "That's the best answer anyone's ever given me. You must be a proper menace." And he told her his name, which was Tan, and said he'd been in for eleven years, for a theft that he had in fact committed, and that he had no complaints.

They talked until it was dark. It turned out that Tan had been a clerk in the granary of the Zhao before he was a thief. He knew, in a shrewd, drowsy, undisappointed way, a great deal about the city. He knew which of the jailers could be bribed with a handful of beans, and which with a joke. He knew which magistrates were honest, which were not, and which were simply tired. He knew the guards on the east gate. And he knew, because the prison was a very small place with a very large appetite for gossip, that the Grand Preceptor Wen Zhong had been lodged in the old Marquis's house on the north avenue, with fifteen hundred of the northern horse.

"They say he doesn't sleep," said Tan. "They say he sits up at night with his hand over one eye, listening."

"Listening for what?"

"Nobody knows." A pause. "They say it's his third eye, in the middle of his forehead. The one that sees what a thing is. They say he opened it once on the road at the passes, and the whole barley field turned out to be foxes."

Fuji said nothing.

"You've gone quiet," said Tan.

"I'm only tired."

"Aye, well. It's a quiet place." The voice softened. "Don't mind my chatter. There's not much to do in here but wait to be tried or die of old age, and I've already done a good deal of the latter."


* * *

They came for her on the sixth morning.

It was not the jailer. It was an officer of the guard, in a plain brown coat, with a long scarred face and a limp. He did not speak. He unlocked her cell and stood aside, and she walked out into the passage with her hands bound, past the other cells, where faces pressed to the bars and watched her go. A hand flapped. A voice called out, hoarse and cheerful: "Mind your step, proper menace!"

They took her up a stair and along a corridor and through a low door into a small stone room with a single window. It held a table, two stools, and a lamp. On one of the stools sat a man.

He was Su Hu.

She had not seen him in more than three months. She had last seen him at the gate of Zhongzhou, in the early light, in his best robe, with his hands hanging empty at his sides and his eyes fixed on a point just above her shoulder, because he could not bear to look at her. He had grown thin. He had grown grey. He was wearing the dark plain mourning-coat of a man who has ridden a very long way without changing, and there was dust on his boots and an unhealed scrape across one cheek. He looked a great deal older than his years.

She stopped in the doorway.

"Sit," said the officer, not unkindly, and went out. The door closed. A bolt slid.

Neither of them moved.

"They told me," said her father at last, "that a maid called Ling had been taken for the matter of the mound. They said she was the queen's own woman. They sent for me to identify her." His voice was low, dry, and quite flat. "I am the queen's father. I came as fast as I could. I rode for three days."

"Father—"

"Don't." He lifted a hand. It shook. "Don't say it. I haven't yet decided whether to look."

He was not looking. His eyes were on the lamp, steadily, as they had been on the morning she left. A muscle jumped in his jaw. She saw his hand close on his knee, the knuckles standing out like a row of small white stones.

"I had a letter," he said. "Two months ago. In my own hand, with my seal. It said that my daughter had arrived safely at Chaoge and been received with honor. That she was in good health and sent her love. I did not write it." He swallowed. "The seal was mine. The hand was a very good copy. I thought: someone has been busy. And I thought, if she's alive, they would not need to forge it."

"I'm alive, Father."

"I thought: she's dead." He said it in the same flat voice. "I thought: they've killed her, and sent me a letter to keep me quiet. I thought it every day for two months. I thought it in my sleep. And then I got a message from the capital, to say that a maid called Ling had been taken for kidnapping, and the queen's father was needed in court, and I sat in my study and I understood that there was only one person in all of Shang who would be called Ling."

"How did you know?"

"It was your mother's name for you." His voice cracked on the last word, and held. "When you were small. She said you were too clever for your own good. It means spirit. It means clever. She called you that when you were four, and found out how to open the grain-chest with a hairpin."

Fuji could not speak.

"Look at me," said the Marquis of Zhongzhou.

She looked.

He had turned. His eyes were red. They were the eyes of a man who has not slept for days, and who has just seen, for the first time in more than a quarter of a year, his daughter alive. He was crying openly, soundlessly, without wiping his face. His hands were on his knees. He was not a strong man. He had never been brave. He had sent a girl of nineteen to a hostile court with four carts of straw because he was too afraid to refuse. He had spent two months believing her dead.

"I knew you," he said. "At once. Before I saw your face. Because you were standing exactly as you do when you've decided to be brave."

She went to him. She did not remember crossing the floor. She was on her knees at his feet, with her bound hands against his knees and his hands in her hair, and he was saying her name, over and over, her real one, the one she had not heard aloud for more than three months. It tasted like salt.

"Don't tell them," she said. "Father. Please. You mustn't say it."

"Say what?"

"My name. Not to the guard, not to the priest, not to anyone." She lifted her head. "If it's known that Ling is Su Fuji, then the woman on the dais is nobody. They'll ask who she is. They'll bring the Preceptor. And she'll die."

"Who?"

So she told him.

She told him in a low rapid whisper, with her face against his sleeve. She told him about the East Wing and the pheasant, the fox, the white fire, the road. She told him about the king. It took the better part of a lamp's burning. When she had finished, he was silent for a long time. He sat with his hand on her head, looking at the lamp.

"Then the woman in the palace," he said slowly. "The one who sits beside the king."

"Is not your daughter."

"Is not a person at all."

"She is very much a person," said Fuji, with sudden fierceness. "She's more of a person than anyone I've met. She'd walk through fire for a stranger. She did. I watched her. She's burned three of her own lives for people she'd never heard of." Her voice cracked. "Father. I'd be dead if it weren't for her. I'd be dead in the East Wing, in the smoke, and nobody would have written down my name."

He was quiet for a very long time.

"I see," said Su Hu.

His face had changed. It had lost something. A kind of weight had gone out of it, a long accumulated weariness, and left something thinner and steadier and rather terrible behind. He lifted her chin with his hand. For the first time in his life, he looked her in the eye.

"There is a thing a father is supposed to say," he said. "I have been preparing it for three months. It is that I'm sorry. That I should never have sent you. That I was a coward and a fool, and that I'll spend the rest of my life making it right." A faint dry smile touched his mouth. "It's all perfectly true. But I find that you've no use for it."

"Father—"

"Listen. I've been summoned to the great hall tomorrow. The Preceptor wishes me to look at the queen and tell the court whether she is my daughter." He drew a long breath. "I have never lied to a king in my life, except in the matter of straw. I do not know whether I can look at a stranger wearing my child's face and call her by your name."

"You must."

"I will do what I can," said her father. "I will do what protects the one who protected you. I can promise no more than that."

"It isn't a plan."

"No. It's what a father has."

She nearly laughed. It came out as something else, and he held her, awkwardly, with his big dry hands. They stayed like that until the lamp began to gutter.

"There's one thing more," said Su Hu, at last, very low. "You'll want to hear it before the guard comes. They can hang a maid called Ling for kidnapping. It's a small crime, and nobody will ask for her. They can't hang the Marquis's daughter. Not without a trial before the king, and a treaty broken, and my seal on the matter. The moment you give your true name in open court, the charge falls to the ground." His voice dropped further. "All you need do to leave this cell alive is to stand up and say who you are."

"And the queen?"

He did not answer.

He did not need to. She sat back on her heels, in the lamplight, with her bound hands in her lap, and saw it with perfect clarity: the plain, simple, terrible arithmetic of it. A maid could be saved only by ceasing to be a maid. Ling could walk out of this prison only by becoming Su Fuji. And there was only one place at the king's side for Su Fuji.

To get out, she thought, I'll have to come back from the dead.

And somebody else would have to go into the ground in her place.
