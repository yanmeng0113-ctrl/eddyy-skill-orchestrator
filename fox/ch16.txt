Chapter Sixteen: Rain

Yue ran toward the fire, because she had spent four hundred years running away from things and had apparently run out.

The long granary was a furnace. The new thatch had gone up in one sheet, and now burning straw fell in long orange ribbons through the roof-beams onto the bedding below. The air was a roar of heat and a stink of smoke and lamp oil. Out of the great door, in a staggering, coughing, shrieking stream, came the people of the South Court: women with babies clutched against their chests, children dragging blankets, an old man leaning on a boy. Qingpu stood in the doorway like a rock in a river, grabbing each one by the collar and hurling them out into the yard.

"How many are inside?" Yue shouted.

"Eleven! Twelve!" The healer's grey braid was singed, and her face was black. "The ones in the back bins, they're sleeping, they were exhausted. And Yaoniang. She can't walk!"

"Where's Fuji?"

"She went in for the children!"

Yue stood very still for the space of one breath.

Then she saw the maid. She came out of the smoke at the end of the aisle, low, bent double, with a child under each arm and a third clinging to her neck. Her headcloth had been torn off. Her hair was loose and smoking. She was running the way people run who have long since stopped thinking, and as she came the ridge-beam above her groaned, and a long crack ran along it from end to end, and the whole west half of the roof leaned out over the aisle like a wave about to break.

"Fuji!"

She heard her own voice go up into a scream. The maid looked up. She did not stop. She flung the children, one after another, through the doorway into Qingpu's arms, and turned back, and went in again.

She's gone back for Yaoniang, thought Yue. She's gone back, and she's going to die in there, and it is going to be very quiet and sensible and mathematical.

She did not decide. There was never a decision. There was only the small cold click at the base of her spine, the same one she'd felt in the East Wing, and then it was done.

She ran around the corner of the granary to the dead persimmon tree, where nobody was looking, and went to her knees in the dirt. Every instinct she'd ever had told her to hide, to wait, to let the thing burn quietly and mourn it afterward. She put her hands flat on the cold earth. Behind her ribs, in the place where the lamp's wicks were, the eight flames woke all at once and bent toward her, patient and pale, like a ring of attentive faces.

I'm sorry, she told them.

She let go of the face.

It fell off her like a veil in the wind, the queen's face, Fuji's face, and she was white fur and amber eyes on four small paws, with eight tails flaring behind her in the firelight like a banner. She threw back her head.

It was not a thing that could be said in words. It was older than words. It came up out of her chest in a long high wavering note, a fox's call, a cry that went up and out and over the roof of the burning granary and into the dark sky above, and the sky, which had been clear and cold and full of stars, listened.

A cloud formed.

It was a small one at first, a smudge of darkness over the south wall, as though somebody had breathed on a bronze mirror. It spread. It thickened. It rolled out across the stars with a long slow muttering sound like a great animal turning in its sleep, and the air changed. It went suddenly cold. It smelled of river and stone and wet earth.

The first drop fell on Yue's nose.

Then it came down.

It was the kind of rain that happens perhaps once in a lifetime, a solid, roaring, silver downpour that fell on the granary as though a river had been tipped out of the sky. It hit the burning thatch with a shriek of steam. It hammered the roof, the yard, the stones, the dead tree. In a few breaths the orange glow was gone. The flames went out in great hissing gouts, one after another, until the roof was only a sodden black ruin, steaming in the dark, and the rain still poured, and kept pouring, until every ember had drowned.

And then Yue's eighth tail caught fire.

It started at the tip. It was not like the first time. The first time she had been braced, and furious, and it had felt like being unpicked. This time it felt like being forgiven. The cold white flame ran down the length of the tail, soft and slow, and where it passed it took with it a long gentle ache, a century of small private griefs she hadn't known she was carrying. It was almost kind. It was the kindest thing that had happened to her in sixty years.

It reached the root.

She felt the lamp, far away on its hill, lose a wick. She felt the goddess look.

Seven, she thought. Seven, and I did it in front of a hundred people.

She had meant to be quiet. She had meant to do it in the dark, behind a tree, in the shelter of the rain. She had not meant to cry out. But the sound had gone across the whole of the south ward, and somewhere in the ruin a child had started to wail, and the voice of the woman who had run back into the fire was saying, over and over, in a high cracked whisper: "It's raining. It's raining. It's raining."


* * *

It was raining. It was also, very quickly, over.

The cloud broke. The rain thinned to a drizzle, and then to nothing, and the stars came back, cold and sharp and entirely uninterested in any of it. A great silence settled over the yard. Steam rose from the sodden thatch. The people of the South Court stood in the mud, soaked to the skin, black with soot, staring up at the sky as though it had said their names.

"It was a miracle," whispered somebody.

"The queen's miracle."

"She put it out with a prayer."

"She wasn't even here. She was in the yard, she was—"

"Where is she?"

Don't, thought Yue. Don't look for me. She lay in the wet dirt beside the dead tree, a small white shape with her cheek in a puddle, and tried to move a leg. It would not. Her body had gone loose and strange, as though somebody had cut every string at once. Behind her, where the tail had been, was a raw, quiet, bottomless place, and her head swam. She could hear footsteps. She could hear someone calling her name, the queen's name, Fuji, Fuji, in a voice that cracked on the second syllable, and it took her a moment to understand that it was not the maid.

"Madam!" It was the guard with the long nose, the boy from the queen's wing. He was shouting at the top of his lungs. "Your Majesty! The king is coming!"

The king.

Of course. The palace was a mile away, but the glow had lit the sky, and the rain had been seen from the Deer Terrace to the Wei gate. There would be chariots. There would be torches. There would be a roll of questions and a hundred witnesses and a queen who had vanished in the middle of a miracle.

She tried again to get up. Her forelegs trembled and gave.

"Don't," said a voice, very close.

She lifted her head.

Fuji was kneeling in the mud beside her. She was black from head to foot, her hair clotted with ash, her hands raw and blistered where she'd hauled Yaoniang from the back bin, and she was looking at the little white fox with an expression Yue had never seen on her, not fear, not even grief, but a kind of stunned, shaking, furious devotion. Her lips were moving. She was counting.

Seven, said Fuji, without sound. You have seven.

"I'm all right," Yue tried to say, and what came out was a thin, creaky, wholly foxlike whine.

"You're not. Be quiet." The maid's voice was very low and very steady. "I'm going to pick you up. Don't struggle. Anyone who looks will see an animal that fainted from the smoke. I'll tell them it's the queen's pet. She was out in the yard with it when the roof went up." She slid her arms under Yue's belly, carefully, as if she were lifting a pot of something hot. "I'll say it's what you were trying to save."

"I—"

"Hush. I'll sort it out."

But the chariots were already in the lane. She could hear the clatter of hooves and the rattle of wheels, and the crowd's voices rising and parting, and then a sudden, immense quiet, like a held breath. Footsteps came across the wet yard, quick and heavy. Fuji's arms tightened. Over the maid's shoulder, in the red glare of the torches, Yue saw him come around the corner of the ruined granary.

He was bareheaded. His hair was loose. He had come in a plain robe, with no guard, no banner, nothing but a lantern, which he had dropped somewhere in the lane. He had run. His boots were black with mud to the knee, and his chest was heaving, and the long scowling face that she'd learned to read like a tally was open and blank and stripped of every careful thing.

He stopped at the edge of the yard. He looked at the burned roof. He looked at the huddled, singed people. He looked at Qingpu, standing like a post in the doorway with her arms still full of blankets. He looked, slowly, at the sky, where a few ragged clouds were drifting off toward the river, and at the puddles in the yard, and at the smoking black ruin, and at the maid, who had somehow turned her body so that her arms and the fox in them were hidden from the crowd.

"Where is the queen?" said Di Xin.

Nobody answered.

"I asked where my wife is." It was not loud. It was dreadful. "I was at the Deer Terrace. I saw the glow, and I saw the rain. I saw a rain that started at the south wall and did not fall anywhere else in the city." His voice shook. "Somebody tell me she isn't in there."

"She is safe, Great King." The maid's voice was thin and clear, and quite expressionless. "She was in the lodge with me when the roof went. She's lying down. She was overcome by smoke."

"I'll see her."

"She asked not to be seen. She said she was very much ashamed."

"Of what?"

"Of not being able to stop it." It was a perfect lie, level and sad and exactly the right size. Yue would have kissed her for it, if she had been able to move. "She prayed. She said it was a thing she had never done. And she said the goddess answered."

Silence.

The king did not look at the crowd. He did not look at the ruined roof. His eyes, over the grey shoulder of a maid with a bundle in her arms, had come to rest on the thing she was holding. It was a very small thing. It was very white. In the glare of the torches, with the rain still dripping from its fur, it had seven long and delicate tails folded along its flank, soft as smoke, and one of them, the last, was still faintly glowing at the tip.

He took three steps. He unclasped his plain brown cloak and let it fall open.

"Give her to me," said Di Xin. He said it very quietly. It was not a command. It was not quite a request. He had gone white to the lips, and his eyes were enormous, and she had never before seen so much in a face. "Please. It's cold. She'll catch a chill."

And Fuji, who had been trained all her life in the art of making sensible decisions, did a thing she would never afterward be able to explain.

She gave him the fox.

He wrapped her in the cloak. He did it carefully, in one swift sure movement, so that the only thing the crowd could see was a bundle of brown wool and a long fall of white that might have been a lady's scarf. His arms closed around her. She was a very small weight against his chest. She could hear his heart, a quick hard hammering, and through the haze of exhaustion she smelled river water and ink and lamp oil and, beneath them, something warm and wholly unexpected.

Don't, she tried to say. You'll see.

"I know," said the king, so softly that nobody else could have heard.

And Yue, who had spent four hundred years not being seen, closed her eyes.
