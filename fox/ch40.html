Chapter Forty: What the Histories Will Say

By the second month of the new year, the South Court had a hundred and eighty-three names on its roll, a roof that did not leak, and a white fox on the wall.

Su Fuji had given up trying to remove the fox. It sat on the top of the garden wall every morning at the fifth hour, in the thin sun, with four tails tucked round its feet and its nose in the air, and every morning the people of the south ward brought it something. A bowl of gruel. A string of cowries. A twist of red thread. The children had worked out that it would deign to be scratched behind the left ear, and only the left, and queued for the privilege. The fox endured it with an expression of such profound and weary contempt that Fuji, passing with an armful of blankets, was obliged to put her sleeve over her mouth.

"You're enjoying it," she said, low.

The fox did not turn its head. One amber eye slid sideways. It said, quite plainly: I am being tolerant. There is a difference.

"I'll put it in the ledger."

The tail-tip twitched.

It was a good morning. It was, Fuji thought, standing in the yard with the sun on her face, very nearly a happy one, and she had learned not to say so aloud. There was porridge on to boil, and a man mending the east roof, and a girl of fourteen called Mei sitting on an upturned basin with a bundle of reed-slips, learning her characters from Ming. Qingpu was in the infirmary shouting at somebody, which meant it was going well. Yaoniang was telling the story of the Fox and the Preceptor to a knot of new arrivals, and had got it as far as the part where the Eye shut.

She herself kept the roll. It was the one thing that she would not delegate. Every morning, at the fifth hour, she sat down at the long table in the hall with a brush and wrote the day's names, and every morning she thought of a string of two hundred and sixteen stones. It was a great comfort, being in charge of something that added up.

"A visitor," said Ming, at her elbow.

It was the Master of Sacrifices.

She did not know him at first. He stood at the gate in a plain brown coat with no ash-white sleeves, no tall cap, and no priest's gold, carrying a sack of millet on one shoulder like a farm hand. He was a tall man with a close grey beard, with the look of someone who has not slept properly in some weeks. He did not speak. He stood in the gateway and looked at the yard: the girl on the basin, the man on the roof, a row of small hungry children eating. He looked at them for a long time.

"I was told," said Yuan Shi, "that you take whatever is brought."

"We do."

"I have been Master of Sacrifices for nineteen years." He swallowed. "I did not know. I should like it understood. I did not know what they were for. I thought that someone must." He held out the sack, awkwardly, as one hands a thing over to a creditor. "I find that I should like to carry something that does not have to be burned."

Fuji looked at the sack. Then she looked at him. Then she took the ladle off its hook by the door, put it into his hand, and pointed at the porridge pot.

"Stir it," she said. "Don't let it catch."

* * *

The boy came on the same afternoon, Yue thought, and I knew at once that I was going to dislike him, which was extremely unfair.

She had seen him from the wall. It was a good wall for seeing. She lay along the top of it in the last of the sun with her nose on her paws, and watched the old prince come down the lane from the Archive with a thin boy at his elbow, and she smelled him before she had quite made out his face.

It was not a bad smell. That was the trouble. It was a clean smell, of something that she could not name: ink, and rain, and a kind of bright cold metal, and under it a faint sweet dry tang that she had never smelled on any living creature, like an archive in a country that did not exist. He was about sixteen. He was thin and brown and wore a coat that was subtly wrong, the cut of the sleeves a shade too narrow, the fastenings in the wrong place. He walked with his head a little bent, in the manner of a person who has been told many times not to stare, and was staring.

"My new apprentice," said Bi Gan. "He came to the Archive three days ago with a letter from nobody and a talent for the old script that I find quite unreasonable. His name is Lin Yan."

"Madam," said the boy, and bowed.

It was an odd bow. It was too low, and it went on too long. When he straightened, there were tears standing in his eyes, which he did not seem to know about. He was looking at Fuji with a stricken, reverent, helpless expression, as one looks at a face one has only ever seen in stone.

"Forgive me," said Lin Yan. "I'm sorry. You remind me of someone I have read about."

"Everyone reminds somebody of somebody," said Fuji, kindly.

"Yes." His voice was not quite steady. "I suppose so."

Yue sat up.

She did not know why. There was nothing to see. The boy had no tail and no glamour. He had no seal on him, and no god's thumbprint, and no hidden shape. He was perfectly, undeniably, flesh and bone. But something in the way he stood in the lane, with the sun going down behind him and the long shadow of the South Court wall across his feet, made the small hairs lift along her spine. It was like finding one slip in a roll and knowing, without the least reason, that it had been written in a different hand from all the rest.

He's not in the ledger, she thought. He's in another one.

The boy's eyes came up. They found the wall. They found the white fox on it, with its ears flat, staring at him, and for an instant he went absolutely still, and something went over his young face that was not fear and was not wonder. It was a look she had seen once, on a tall tired man in a plain coat on a roof, watching a red star climb the sky. It was recognition.

Nothing burned.

She felt for it, quite carefully, the way one touches a bruise, and nothing burned at all.

"Hm," said Bi Gan, mildly. "He does that to everyone. It's very endearing. Come, boy. We've sixty rolls to copy by dark."

They went on up the lane. The boy looked back twice.

* * *

"A boy," said Di Xin.

"A peculiar boy."

"Peculiar how?"

"I don't know." Yue was sitting on the edge of the Counting Room desk, with her feet swinging, in her white night-shift, with the moon coming through the shutters in long thin bars across the floor. It was very late. She had been a woman for four hours, and she was making the most of it. "He looked at you across the yard this afternoon, when you came out of the hall. Did you see?"

"I saw a boy staring at me."

"He looked at you the way people look at something on a tomb." She shivered. "As though he knew how it ended."

Di Xin put down his brush. He was quiet for a moment, a tall thin man in a plain grey robe, in a room smelling of ink and lamp oil, with a stack of red-bound slips at his elbow and his hair coming loose. He looked, she thought, much better than he had a month ago. He looked like a man who had slept three nights out of four.

"Everyone knows how it ended," he said. "It's what I've been trying to tell you. I did the sums last night. The Zhou have three hundred chariots and a great many allies, and a very good strategist, and I have a treasury with a hole in it and a great many friends in the wrong places." He rubbed his eyes. "It will be a few years. I think I can give us five. Perhaps six, if the harvests hold."

"And then?"

"And then I shall be a man who ended the altar and lost his throne, and the Zhou will have a poem on a wall, and a fox, and a very good historian." His mouth curved. "I have been thinking about what we shall do in the meantime."

"Write it down."

"Yes." He drew a stack of slips toward him. "All of it. The roll. The rolls of the roll. The names, and the jars, and the cost of every granary, and the true account of a wall in a temple, with the hand that wrote it. A copy in the Archive. A copy at the South Court. Three in a cave, if I can find one, with a very bad lock." He tapped the stack. "I can't stop them writing it. But I think that if I keep an honest ledger, carefully, for long enough, then sooner or later somebody will open it. It's the only revenge I've ever been able to think of that didn't require a sword."

"It's a very dull revenge."

"Yes," said Di Xin, with deep contentment. "I rather think that's the point."

She laughed. She slid down off the desk and went to the shutters, and set her hands on the sill, and looked out. The moon was high. The plum trees in the garden were just coming into flower, white and tiny and unbelievably fragile in the cold, and she could smell them, even through the wood.

Four, she thought.

She did not say it. She had been counting all day, and all week. It had become a kind of private habit, like touching a wall in the dark. She could feel them, long and silver, curled against the back of her knees. One for a barn. One for a king. One for a girl in grey, who made me a face and took it back. And one, she thought, for me. It was a good sum. It was not nine. But it came out.

On the garden wall, in the moonlight, something small and round sat among the plum-blossom, exactly where a pedlar had sat in the autumn. It was a persimmon. It was perfectly ripe, in the wrong season, and it was tied to a bamboo slip with a bit of red string.

She went out and took it. The slip said, in a quick, freckled hand: Not yet. But I wanted you to know that I keep good books.

"Who's that from?" said Di Xin, from the door.

"A friend," said Yue, and bit into the fruit. "I'll tell you about it. It's rather a long story."

"I have time."

"Do you?"

He came out into the moonlight, and stood beside her, and took her hand. It was warm. It was only, she thought, a little after midnight, and the moon would not set until the fifth hour.

"Yes," said Di Xin. "I think I do. A few years of it. I mean to spend them very carefully."

* * *

Far to the west, in the high dry country at the foot of the mountains, where the river runs shallow over its stones and the wind smells of snow, an old man sat alone in a tent.

He had been a fisherman, once, and a great many other things, and he was tired in a way that sleep had never mended. A small bronze lamp stood at his elbow, and its flame was not the color of any lamp that a man might light: pale, still, and cold, the gold of a lamp seen from the bottom of a well. On his knee lay a roll of fresh slips, cut that morning, white and clean, with not a single mark upon them. Beside it stood a pot of black ink.

He looked at it for a long time.

It was not a decision. He had made that, many years ago, on a riverbank, with a straight hook in the water and no bait on it. It was only that every history has a first line, and he had not quite believed, until this moment, that he was going to be the one to write it.

He ended the altar, Jiang Ziya thought. He opened the granaries. He kept a jar of earth for every name. A good king. A bad one. A king who was right. The lamp-flame moved in the draft. And not one of the lords of the west will march for a man who was right.

They would march for a fox.

He dipped the brush. He thought, briefly, of a small white animal on a wall in the sun, with four tails and an expression of profound contempt. He thought of an old prince with a square white beard, who had said: I am afraid of being right in the wrong book. He thought, with the very faint, particular sorrow of a man who does what has to be done and does not enjoy it, that it was a pity that the little creature was so likeable.

Then he laid the first stroke on the clean white slip, and the second, and the third, and wrote, in a quick, upright, careful hand, the first line of the history of the age:

Di Xin, King of Shang, was bewitched by a fox.
