Chapter Twenty-Two: The Mirror of Nüwa

The goddess's mirror was a shallow bronze basin, three paces across, set into the floor of the hall at the Hill of Nüwa, and it had not been filled with water for two hundred years.

Yue knew this because she had been told it, on the day she was made, in a voice like the cold edge of a river. Do not look into the basin, little lamp. It does not show what is. It shows what must be. She had looked into it once, as a small white kit, and seen nothing but her own reflection, wide-eyed and trembling. She had never gone near it since.

She stood on its rim now, in the middle of the night, in the dark empty hall, with her hands clasped before her and her heart going like a rabbit's.

She had not meant to come. She had meant to stay in the Counting Room with the lamp lit and the door barred, and a king asleep in the chair beside her. But the dream had not stopped. It had gone on, night after night, two weeks of it: the lamp in the goddess's hand, the counting of wicks, the three small words in the voice of cold water. On the fifteenth night she had woken at the third watch in her own bed in the queen's wing, to find that she was standing on the doorstep of the palace in her bare feet, with the road to the Hill of Nüwa unrolling before her under a fingernail moon. She had not walked there. She had simply been brought.

Come, said the voice. Look.

She looked.

The basin was full.

It was not water. It was a dark, still, glossy stuff, like black lacquer, or very old oil, and it held no reflection. It held the sky. She saw the stars in it, and a pale scatter of cloud, and the thin horned moon. Then the stars wheeled, and shifted, and began to arrange themselves, one at a time, into something else.

It was a city. A great one, walled in earth and bronze, with a tall gate and a broad avenue. She knew it at once. She had lived in it for three months. It was Chaoge, from above, as a bird might see it, with the smoke rising from a thousand hearths. And then, slowly, from the north side of the palace, a red light began to spread.

Mother, said Yue. What is this?

What must be.

The fire crept south, street by street, and the city went up under it like dry straw. She saw the walls fall. She saw the roofs cave in. She saw a very small, very distant figure on a tower, wreathed in smoke, standing quite still with his arms at his sides, and she did not need to be told whose it was.

No, she said.

You were asked to wear his queen, said Nüwa. You were asked to take his court from within. That is what you were sent to do, and it is what must be done. You have instead taken his hand.

The voice was not angry. It had never been angry. It was only very old, and very tired, and absolutely certain.

I see the hall of my altars, little lamp. I see how many of them are cold. I have counted them. There were nine hundred and forty-one temples in Shang when you were born. Last year there were four hundred and six. The surface of the basin rippled. The burning city dissolved, and in its place came something else, a long grey row of figures, indistinct and thin, bent over a stone bench. They were so insubstantial that Yue could see the bench through them. Do you know who these are? These are the gods of the rivers. These are the ones who held up the rain. This one was called Lu of the Ford, he kept a ferry and had a good voice, and he died last winter. He was the first.

He died of the cold, whispered Yue. Because there were no more offerings.

He died because a man closed a pit. A man with a good heart. The black lacquer shivered. I don't hate him, my lamp. I want you to know that. I think he's the best king that Shang has had in three hundred years. And I think that he is going to kill us all.

Yue stood very still.

It was a thing she had never thought of. In all her four hundred years, in all her dealings with the powers, she had never once considered that the goddess might be afraid. She was too old. She was too large. She was a mountain, a river, the mother of every living soul in the realm. But as she looked at the black basin, and the grey figures bent over their bench, she heard, for the first time, what was under the cold, patient, unbending voice. It was the sound of a very old woman at a window, in the dark, counting.

They're hungry, she said slowly. The gods.

They're dying. We do not die as you do. We simply thin. We lose our names, and then our faces, and at the end we become a draft in an empty room. A pause. I had two hundred years to make my peace with it. The younger ones have not. The river gods have children, little lamp. Small ones. They have not yet learned to be afraid.

Then why not tell him? The words came out of her before she could stop them. He'd listen. He listens to everyone. He reads the tallies himself. If you told him what the pits are for, if you showed him this—

Do you imagine I haven't?

The basin darkened.

I told his father. In a dream, forty years ago. I said: the gods depend upon the offerings of men, and if the offerings stop, we will fade. And his father said: then let the gods fade. There was a silence. They are all like that, in the end. The kings. They think a god is a thing that happens to them. They think we are a sort of weather. They do not understand that we are made of them. That we are made of what they give.

He understands, said Yue.

He understands it better than any king in history. That is the horror of it. A long breath, like wind in an empty reed. He has looked at the cost of what we are, and he has decided it is too high. He may be right. I would be a poor mother if I could not see that he might be right. The black lacquer stilled. But I am a god, little lamp. I do not have the option of being right. I only have the option of being alive.

For a long moment neither of them spoke.

It was the strangest conversation Yue had ever had. She was standing at the edge of a basin of dark lacquer, in a cold hall, barefoot, with the voice of a goddess in her head, and what she was thinking was: she's lonely. It was not a thought she'd have believed she could have. It made the next words hard.

What do you want me to do?

Finish it. The voice was gentle. You were given nine tails and a season. You've spent two of them on barn-fires and rain. You've seven left, and you're slow. I'll give you until the solstice: a few weeks more. When the night is longest, I will come to the king's altar, and he will be there. And you will be there, with a knife, as you ought to have been from the start.

And if I won't?

Then I will take your lamp, and snuff it myself, and send the pheasant to do it properly. There was no threat in the words. They came out almost sadly. I would rather not. I have grown fond of you. But I do not have the option of being fond.

I've seven, said Yue, dizzily. I've still got seven.

Five. The voice was patient. You'll give me two more, on the way. You always do. You can't help yourself. The basin began to cloud. The stars went out, one by one. Go home, little lamp. Sleep. You have until the longest night.

Mother—

But the black lacquer had gone dull. It was only bronze, cold and empty, with a thin skin of dust, and in it, small and white, she saw her own reflection: a wild-eyed young woman in a sleeping-robe, with seven faint tails curling behind her like smoke.

She was alone in the hall. The painted face of the goddess looked down at her from the plinth, serene and blank and utterly silent.


* * *

She was halfway down the hill, in the grey dawn, with her feet bleeding on the frozen path, when she heard it.

It came from the edge of the wood, from a stand of leafless birches on the slope below the temple. It was thin and clear and unmistakable, a tune picked out on four strings by a woman with a lazy touch and a long mouth. It was the goose song.

She had not heard it in the wine-shop for weeks. She had not heard it anywhere that was not a pheasant's mouth. But this was not Hu Ximei. It was too loose, too warm, too nicely unhurried. It had an extra flourish at the end of each verse, a sly little falling run, that nobody but one person in the world had ever played.

"Pipa?"

She turned. Through the trees, in a patch of grey light, sat a woman in a faded blue gown on a fallen log, with an instrument across her lap. She looked up from the strings and grinned.

"I wondered how long you'd be."

"What are you doing here?"

"Waiting for you. I've been following you for a week." The pipa-player set down the instrument and held out her arms. "You're barefoot, you fool. Come here."

Yue went. She did not remember crossing the distance. She was simply in the blue gown, with her face in the old familiar smell of lamp smoke and beeswax and strings, and a hand on the back of her head, stroking.

"I heard," said Pipa quietly. "I was in the wood the night you were brought up here. I saw the road go dark. I couldn't come in. I never could, it's not my hall." Her voice was rough. "I came out the next day and sat by the door and played every song I knew, in case you could hear."

"I could," said Yue. "A bit."

"Good." A pause. "What did she tell you?"

So she told her. She told her all of it: the basin, the burning city, the grey figures with the bench showing through them. She told her about the thinning gods, and the solstice, and the knife. It took a long time. The dawn came up grey and cold through the birches, and somewhere a bird began to sing, tentative and thin, as if it were not sure that the sun had really meant it.

When she had finished, the pipa-player did not speak for a while.

"She's afraid," she said at last.

"Yes."

"I thought she would be. She's older than the rivers, Yue, and she's still never learned to lose." The black eyes were wet. "I'll tell you something, and you'll hate it. She's right. About the gods. About all of it. I've watched them go, one after another, for forty years. The little ones first. A spring in a village. A grove by a ford. They don't scream. They don't ask for anything. They just stop."

"You think he's wrong."

"I think he's brave. I don't know whether that's the same." The pipa-player took her hands. "But I know you've a choice, my moon, and it isn't between a king and a goddess. It's between being what she made you and being what you've become. And I won't pretend that's an easy one."

"What would you do?"

"Me?" She laughed, a broken, tender, entirely unsurprised sound. "I'd go to the wine-shop and play the goose song until the world ended. I'm not made for choices. I'm a pipa. But I'll tell you what I'd want, if I were you."

"What?"

"A day." The long mouth twisted. "Just one. Before the solstice. A day when nobody wanted anything, and you weren't anyone's lamp. I'd want to know what it felt like."

Yue closed her eyes.

Five, the goddess had said. You always do. You can't help yourself.

"There's a market tomorrow," said the pipa-player gently. "In the east quarter. It'll be the last warm day before the deep cold. The whole city will be there. I'll be playing by the well." She squeezed her hands. "Come. Bring your king. Bring your friend with the ledger. Eat a hot millet cake. Be nobody."

"It's dangerous."

"Everything's dangerous. That's rather the point."

She went down the hill with her feet wrapped in the pipa-player's shawl, and for the length of the walk she let herself think about a hot millet cake, and a crowd, and a whole morning of being nobody. It was the last uncomplicated thing she thought for a long time.


* * *

Fuji was waiting in the queen's wing with the door barred, and she did not say good morning.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'm all right. I only went for a walk—"

"You went to the Hill of Nüwa in your bare feet at the third watch. The guard saw you go. He told the king, who has been walking the corridor since the fifth watch, and who has now gone to put on his boots." She had a face like a shut door. "Sit down, Yue. I have something to tell you, and I'd rather you were sitting."

Yue sat.

"The wine-shop by the east gate was raided an hour ago," said Fuji. "Eight men in plain grey, with a warrant bearing the seal of the district magistrate. They didn't ask for anyone. They went straight upstairs. There was a tall old man with them, a stranger, very patient, carrying a lantern." Her voice did not shake, and Yue understood that it was taking all her strength. "They took the pipa-player. The customers say she didn't struggle. She picked up her instrument, and asked politely if she might bring it."

The room went very quiet.

"There's a notice on the market gate," said Fuji. "It's already on every wall in the east quarter. The cook brought it. I've copied it out."

She held up the slip. The characters on it were small, neat, and precise.

By the word of the people of the east quarter, and with the sanction of the District, the demon that has long lived among us in the shape of a lute-player shall be burned in the market square at noon tomorrow, that the quarter may be cleansed. Let all who love the realm attend.

"It's a first-rate notice," said Fuji, very low. "Whoever wrote it has a gift. 'Let all who love the realm attend.' They want a crowd." She laid down the slip. "They aren't hunting her, Yue. She's the bait. They know you'll come."
