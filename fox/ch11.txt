Chapter Eleven: Cousin Ximei Wants Results

"A very slow breakfast," said Hu Ximei, "for a queen who is supposed to be ruining a kingdom."

She was sitting on the low bench across the table with her chin on her hand and a millet cake in her fingers, which she was dismantling one crumb at a time. It was the fourth morning since she had moved into the east chamber. On the first she had brought flowers. On the second, a jar of pickles. On the third she had merely appeared, as though through the wall, and now she sat in the queen's own breakfast room with the air of a woman who had come to collect a debt.

Yue kept her hands under the base of her cup. "I've been busy."

"With what? The man's been asleep on you for a week."

"He doesn't sleep on me."

"He sleeps beside you with a pile of bamboo slips on his chest, and in the mornings he walks out with ink on his sleeves. I've been listening through the wall." A crumb rolled across the lacquer. "I have never in my life heard so little happen in a bedroom."

From the corner, where she was dusting a bronze lamp-stand with great concentration, Ling made a small noise that might have been a cough.

"I'm being patient," said Yue.

"You're being slow." The gold eyes came up, and the smile stayed exactly where it was. "I wrote to Mother on the new moon. I told her the queen was settling in. She was gracious about it. But the solstice is a long way off, sister, and the harvest failed in Zhongzhou for the third year and the gods are thin, and Mother likes to be shown that the first tail was well spent." She set the cake down. "Where is the ruin? Where's the excess? Show me one rotten thing a king has done since you arrived that he wouldn't have done before."

She wants evidence, thought Yue, with a cold flicker of understanding. Not for the goddess. For herself. Whoever came to Nüwa with the final proof, whoever stood beside the goddess when the king fell, would be the one she thanked.

And Hu Ximei did not intend it to be the fox.

"There's a feast," Yue heard herself say.

"A feast."

"At the full moon. For the great houses of Shang. The queen's welcome." She put down her cup, because her fingers had begun to shake. "I'll make it very large. Drinking, meat, music. Everything the Office despises. It'll be talked about from here to the Wei."

"Hm." The pheasant considered her for a moment, head tilted. "That's the first sensible thing you've said all week. I'll tell Mother you were showing initiative. I expect her to be pleased." She stood, with a swish of grey skirts, and dropped a kiss on the top of Yue's head, which was the most alarming thing she had done yet. "Do try to make it vulgar. The temples always remember the vulgar ones."

She left. The door closed.

Ling put down the lamp-stand and the duster, and turned around very slowly.

"A feast," she said.

"I panicked."

"You did not panic. You improvised." Fuji's voice shook slightly. She crossed the room, sat down heavily on the bench where the pheasant had been, and picked up the half-eaten cake. She looked at it with an odd, absent expression. "A great feast for the great houses. The Geng. The Zhong. The Lü and the Wei, the ones who hold the estates and the bondservants and the granaries, all in one hall, with the king at the head and a great deal of wine."

"Is that good?"

"It might be very good." She put the cake down. "Or it might be the end of us. I need to think."


* * *

She thought for two days. Yue watched her do it: sitting on the floor of the maids' room in the evenings, with a lamp, a stylus, and a clay tablet, writing columns and scraping them out. She spoke to no one. She ate when Yue pushed bread into her hand. On the third night she walked in to where Yue sat on the platform, set the tablet on her knees, and said, "I need you to ask him for it."

"For the feast?"

"For permission to spend the king's money. A great deal of it." Her face was pale and fierce with concentration. "You'll ask in front of the Master of the Granary and as many of the uncle's clerks as you can gather. A queen who begs for gold for a banquet is a queen who is ruining a kingdom. The pheasant will have what she asked for. And the king..." She stopped.

"What about the king?"

"I don't know yet. That's what worries me." Fuji rubbed her eyes. "I want to see what he does when he's offered the chance to be exactly what they say he is."


* * *

The Counting Room was the king's study, a long narrow chamber on the north side of the palace, with a high window and one wall made entirely of shelves. The shelves held bamboo slips, hundreds of rolls, bound in red cord and black cord and plain hemp. A strong smell of lamp oil and old wood hung in the air. In the middle of the floor stood a long table covered with tallies, and at the table sat the king, in a plain under-robe, with his hair coming loose and a smear of ink along his jaw.

He looked up as she came in. It took him a moment. Then his face did the small, unguarded thing that she had begun, without intending to, to watch for: a loosening around the eyes, a faint, rueful lift at one corner of the mouth, as if she were a figure that had come out correctly after he'd given up expecting it to.

"My queen. I'd rise, but I've the whole of last year's tithe under my elbow."

"Please don't." Yue stopped at the end of the table. Four clerks sat along it with brushes in their hands. At the far end, a stout elderly man in the brown robe of the Master of the Granary was eating a boiled egg with an expression of settled gloom. "I've come with a request."

"The Master is here as well. I'm told it concerns money." He put down his brush. "That's usually the case."

Say it, said the maid's voice in her head, in a clear, dry, patient murmur. Don't apologize. Don't explain. Ask like a queen who has never been told no.

"I wish to hold a feast," said Yue. "At the full moon, in the great hall. For the houses of Shang, in my honor. I want it to be remembered." She lifted her chin. "I want a hundred cooks and a thousand guests. I want meat on every table, and the best wine in the royal stores, and musicians from the three finest houses in the city. I do not want to hear that the granaries are low. I want it to be large."

The room was silent.

One of the clerks had dropped his brush. The Master of the Granary had stopped halfway through his egg. A thin, hollow look spread across the old man's face, the look of a person who has heard a number and is calculating the debt that follows it.

Yue's heart was beating so hard she thought it might show through her gown. She stared steadily at a point just above the king's left shoulder, as she'd been taught, and waited for him to refuse.

He did not refuse.

For a long moment he did not say anything at all. He was looking at her with that same curious, slow attention, as though she had set down a riddle and he were turning it to catch the light. Then he picked up his brush, dipped it in the ink, and drew a line across the slip in front of him.

"A thousand guests," he said.

"Yes."

"I think it should be bigger."

What?

"A thousand is conservative. The great houses each have retainers, cousins, hangers-on. They'll resent being left out, and a resentful guest is a drinker with a grievance. Say two thousand." The brush moved. "The hall can't hold that. Open the courtyard. Open the forecourt too. I'll want long tables, and torches, and a place for the musicians to stand where people can see them." He glanced up. "And the wine. You said the best wine in the royal stores?"

"Yes?" It came out as a squeak.

"It isn't enough. Master of the Granary, how many jars of new wine are in the cellars at the south gate?"

The Master put down his egg with trembling fingers. "Four hundred, Great King. Perhaps four hundred and twenty."

"Make it six hundred. Make it a pond." The king's eyes were bright, now, and a little wild; there was a high color in his cheeks that Yue had never seen. "Dig a pit in the forecourt. Line it with clay. I want the wine to run into it from jars, so that anyone who walks in can see how very much there is. Have the meat hung from the trees on the north side, on hooks, on cords, as many cuts as the royal kitchens can bear to part with. They'll say it is the most disgraceful thing the House of Shang has ever done."

"Great King," said the old man faintly. "The cost—"

"I'll sign for it. Today." He was already writing. "My queen is quite right. It must be remembered. Master, you'll please send the order to all the houses tonight. By my seal, with my name. Every great family in the realm will be at my table at the full moon, and they will eat and drink as they have never eaten or drunk before." He looked up, and smiled, a swift, narrow, dangerous smile that did not look like any smile Yue had seen on him. "I expect it to be talked about for a hundred years."

He handed the order to a clerk, who scuttled out as though pursued.

The Master of the Granary was staring at his hands. The other clerks had bent over their tablets, their faces carefully blank, in the manner of men who have seen a king fall off a cliff and do not wish to be the first to mention it.

Yue stood very still.

"My queen." Di Xin's voice was mild again. He was smiling at her, the ordinary lopsided smile of a man who has just been handed a very welcome present. "Thank you. I shall be looking forward to it a great deal."

"I," said Yue. "Yes. Good."

She made a bow that was almost correct and backed out of the room.

In the corridor, she stopped and leaned against the wall with her eyes shut, her heart still racing, and discovered that she was grinning. She did not know why. She had just asked a king to waste the realm's grain on a night of drunken indecency, and he had said yes, and made it larger, and she ought to have felt triumphant.

Instead, she thought, I feel as though I've just helped him string a bow.


* * *

Fuji was waiting at the end of the corridor with a pile of laundry in her arms. She did not say anything, only fell into step beside Yue as they walked, and when they were out of earshot of the guards she asked, very quietly, "What did he say?"

"He said bigger."

Fuji stopped dead.

"He ordered two thousand guests. And six hundred jars of wine, in a pit. And the meat hung from the trees." Yue's voice was strange in her own ears. "He did it before I'd finished asking. As if he'd been planning it. As if I'd handed him exactly what he needed."

For a moment Fuji's expression was perfectly blank.

Then, slowly, as the sums began to resolve themselves behind her eyes, something happened to her face. The corners of her mouth tilted. Her eyes widened. A small, incredulous, delighted breath escaped her, and she put one hand flat against the laundry, as though holding down a great weight.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, he's clever. He is a clever, clever man."

"What?"

"Two thousand guests," said Su Fuji. "Every great house in Shang. In one courtyard, with torches, and a pond of wine." She had begun to laugh, silently, with her shoulders. "Do you know what you've done? You've called every landholder in Shang to one table, all at once, and told them to get drunk. And he's the only one who'll be sober."
