Chapter Twenty-Six: The Pheasant's Move

Three women came to the gate of the South Court at dawn, weeping, and Su Fuji knew before they opened their mouths that she was going to be ruined.

It was nothing she could have named. It was a feeling in the back of the neck. She had been standing at the door of the long granary with her ledger on her knee and a bowl of cooling porridge in her other hand, watching the grey light come up over the south wall, listening to the great bronze horns from the northern gate that had been sounding since before sunrise. She had been thinking about Wen Zhong, and the army camped outside the walls, and how many days it would be before a man with a Heavenly Eye walked into the great hall and looked at the woman on the king's right. She had been thinking that they ought to start moving. And then the gate had creaked, and a small ragged procession had come through, three mothers with their hair down and their hands over their mouths, and one of them had fallen to her knees in the mud and begun to wail.

"My baby," she sobbed. "My baby. They took my baby."

"Peace," said Fuji, setting down the bowl. "Peace. Who took him?"

"Your people! The queen's people!" The woman's face was blotched and swollen. She had the look of a person who has rehearsed a line so many times it has stopped meaning anything. "I left him here at the full moon, with a woman at the door, because I couldn't feed him, I had no milk. She said he'd be safe. She said the queen would find him a nurse. And I came back this morning, and the woman said there was no such child!"

The other two began to cry, in a strange, loud, simultaneous way.

Fuji looked at them.

She had a very good memory. She had it for faces, for hands, for the particular rhythm of a person's breath, and she was quite sure she had never seen any of them before. There was a name in her book for every woman who had given up a child at that door, and no one had given up a child at the full moon. A baby had come in two weeks ago, with a broken arm, whose mother had died. She had not left. She was asleep in the hall with a skin of goat's milk at her side.

"What is your name?" said Fuji.

"Hua."

"Where do you live?"

"The east lane. By the dyers'."

"Who is your husband?"

There was a pause. Not a long one. It lasted precisely as long as it takes someone to realize that the answer has not been rehearsed.

"I haven't got one," said the woman.

"No," said Fuji, quietly. "I don't suppose you have."


* * *

"You've done well."

The voice came from the gate, light, warm, amused. The three women went suddenly silent. They did not look round. They turned their faces to the ground, as one, like sheep in a shadow, and began, very slowly, to back away.

Hu Ximei stood in the archway of the South Court's gate in a plain grey dress, with her hands folded in front of her. She looked like a lady who had walked in to see about the charity. The first light fell across her face and made her look pale and sweet and rather tired. She was smiling.

"Don't go, ladies," she said. "I've just come to say thank you."

She laid a small string of cowries in the palm of the nearest woman, and the woman fled. The others followed. Their feet pattered on the hard clay and were gone. Fuji did not move.

"I wondered when you'd come," she said.

"Did you? That's flattering." Hu Ximei walked in, looking about her with open pleasure. She glanced at the roof, the well, the dead persimmon, the long row of sleeping bundles beyond the door. "What a charming place. I'd heard so much. It's a pity it's all so temporary."

"Lady Ximei."

"Oh, you needn't be so formal, Fuji."

It dropped between them like a stone into a pool.

Fuji's face did not change. It had been trained not to. She'd spent nineteen years in a house where a flicker of expression could cost a person her dinner. She took a small breath through her nose, and counted four, and let it out.

"I'm afraid I don't know whom you mean."

"Of course not." The pheasant's smile widened by a hair. "Su Fuji, nineteen, daughter of the Marquis of Zhongzhou. Tall. Plain-faced. Counts everything. Reads the old script. Left her father's house in a black carriage, was nearly murdered at a post-station, and has spent the months since in a headcloth, keeping a ledger for a barn." She tilted her head. "I do so admire a woman who takes the long view. They told me tall. I'd pictured taller."

"I'm nineteen. I'll do my best."

Hu Ximei laughed. It was a delighted, silvery sound, and a little frightening, because it was real. "Oh, I like you. I really do. It's such a shame." She took a step closer. "I've known for months. Since the fire. I was on the wall, you know, that night. I saw you go back for the woman in the bin. A girl who runs into a burning roof isn't a maid." A pause. "I've never said a word. Not to Mother. Not to the king. Do you know why?"

"I expect you'll tell me."

"Rope," said Hu Ximei. "I told your friend. I'm very fond of it. It's so useful to a person who plans to climb." She smiled, quite tenderly. "I've been keeping you in reserve. Like a cowrie in a sleeve. And now, you see, I've a use for you."

She reached into her sleeve and brought out a small, flat, bronze disc, the size of a child's palm. It was worn smooth. One face bore a faint raised pattern, a circle with three lines, the personal mark of the queen of Shang. It hung on a bit of blue silk.

"A little charm," she said. "From a baby's cradle. The kind a mother gives to a child she's about to lose. It's engraved with the queen's seal. I had it cut." She turned it over in her fingers, lazily. "In about an hour, it'll be found behind your stable. With a small cloth. And a small mound."

Fuji went cold.

"No," she said.

"Yes. It's a very nice mound. I had it dug at midnight." Hu Ximei's voice was gentle, patient, almost sorry. "Three children, missing from the south ward. Three mothers, in tears, who saw them last at the gate of your charity. And a bundle of rags beneath a persimmon tree, with the queen's mark on it." She lifted her eyes. "Do you know what the people of Chaoge say about the king? That he eats the livers of the young. That he keeps a pit. That his new queen is a thing that has come from the dark to help him." The smile deepened. "I'm only giving them what they've asked for."

"There are no missing children."

"There don't need to be. There only need to be three mothers, and a charm, and a priest to look at it." She tucked the disc back into her sleeve. "Of course, if there were someone in charge of this house who could swear to the court that the book was kept and every name accounted for, who'd sit up in front of the Grand Preceptor and say no baby has ever been taken, well." A little shrug. "That's the keeper of the book. That's you."

"And I'm a maid."

"You're a maid called Ling. Nobody will believe a maid." She came a step nearer, and her voice dropped. "But if the maid were the Marquis's daughter, the true Lady Su, the real one, and swore it before the king's court, in her own name..."

"The queen would be exposed."

"The queen would be replaced. You'd go back to the dais. The fox would be gone." The gold eyes were very bright. "Wen Zhong would look at you, and see a perfectly ordinary girl. And the king would be a very lucky man. He'd have his charity, and his wife, and no demon at all." She lifted her shoulders. "I'm offering you a gift, Fuji. You'd be safe. Your barn would be safe. Only the fox would go."

For a moment Fuji could not speak.

It was a very good offer. It was, in a cold way, the best she could ever have been given. She was a Marquis's daughter, and she'd been a hostage, and a servant, and a thief of her own life, and here was a pheasant in a grey dress offering to hand it back. She could walk out of the South Court in a silk gown. She could sit at the king's right hand. She could be a person again, in her own face and her own name.

And Yue would be dead in a week.

"You say the fox would be gone," said Fuji, slowly.

"Mother would see to it."

"The goddess would kill her."

"It's rather what she's for."

"No." It was the quietest word she had ever said. It came out of her like a bone out of a socket. "No. I'll not do it."

Hu Ximei sighed.

"I did hope you wouldn't be tiresome." She turned to go. At the archway she stopped, with her hand on the post, and looked back, and for a moment the sweetness slipped from her face. What lay under it was neither cruel nor cold. It was very old, and very tired, and almost wistful. "You're like her, you know. The fox. You think there's a choice that doesn't cost anything. I've been alive for two thousand years, child. I have never once seen one."

She went out into the lane.


* * *

The watch came at midmorning.

There were twenty of them, in the leather and bronze of the city guard, led by a captain with a heavy jaw and a bored expression. With them walked a priest of the Office in ash-white sleeves, carrying a lacquer box. Behind them came four men in the green coats of the Zhong, with spears, to "assist." They filed in through the gate in a long, tidy, unhurried line, and the people of the South Court drew back against the walls with their children in their arms and their eyes round.

The captain stopped in the middle of the yard.

"By the word of the district," he said, in the flat voice of a man reading a warrant, "a complaint has been laid. Three infants of the south ward are missing. Their last known place is this house. A search will be made."

"By whose seal?" said Qingpu.

She had come out of the long hall wiping her hands. Her grey braid was coming undone, and her face was set like a stone. The captain looked at her without interest.

"The seal of the Master of Sacrifices, countersigned by the Grand Preceptor's marshal."

"The Master of Sacrifices has been locked in his temple for nearly three weeks."

"That," said the captain, "is not my concern."

They found it in under an hour.

It lay behind the collapsed end of the stable, where the ground had been freshly turned. A small mound, as long as a man's forearm. A scrap of stained cloth. A bronze charm on a blue silk thread, bearing the mark of the queen. The priest knelt, and touched it, and made a small sign, and whispered something to the captain, who nodded.

"Who keeps the book of this house?"

A silence.

"I keep it," said Fuji.

She stepped forward. She did it without hurry. She set the ledger down on the stool by the door, carefully, squaring the corners, and straightened her headcloth. She was not afraid. That was the odd thing. She had expected to be. But there was a cold clear stillness in her, like the stillness at the top of a staircase, and she walked through it as though through a dream.

"I am called Ling," she said. "I am the queen's maid. I keep the names of those who come here. There is no record of any infant left at this gate on the night of the full moon." She looked the captain in the eye. "I'll swear it before the king."

"You'll swear it before the Preceptor," said the captain. "Take her."

They bound her hands in front of her with hemp. It was not tight. The soldiers did it with a sort of embarrassed gentleness, like men uncomfortable with the thing they are told to do. Behind her, a child began to cry, and a woman called out her name, her false one, in a high thin voice that broke.

"Qingpu," said Fuji, without turning her head.

"I'm here."

"The book's on the stool. Keep it. Everything's in order. Send word to the queen. Tell her..." She hesitated, for the first time. "Tell her I did the sums. Tell her it's all right."

"Child—"

"Tell her it's all right."

They led her out through the gate, between the spearmen, past the dead persimmon and the sleeping bundles and a tomcat that sat on the wall and watched her go with a bright cold unblinking eye. The lane was full of faces. She didn't look at them. She looked straight ahead, at the long grey wall of the city prison, which was just visible over the roofs.

The real Lady Su, she thought, with an odd, clean, fierce calm, is in custody for kidnapping, under the name of a maid.

It was very nearly funny. She did not laugh.
