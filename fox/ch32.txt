Chapter Thirty-Two: The Fox in the Sleeve

A fox in a king's sleeve, Di Xin discovered, weighed about as much as a sack of lentils and had the heartbeat of a sparrow.

He felt it through the wool, quick and light and fierce, a tiny hammering against his forearm. She had not spoken. She could not. When she had slid out of the red robe in the garden and become a small white animal with five pale tails, she had looked up at him with enormous amber eyes, and made a short, dry, apologetic sound, like a cough. Then she had walked, with great dignity, up his arm and into his left sleeve, and turned around three times, and lain down.

"Is she all right?" Fuji had whispered.

"She's sulking," he'd said. "I believe it's a compliment."

Now the sleeve was warm and heavy at his side, and the great hall of Shang was filling up, and he stood at the head of the dais in his robe of state, with his hands clasped before him, and tried to remember how to breathe.

It was almost midday. The doors stood wide to the white sky. The nobles had come in their hundreds, in their finest bronze and furs, and sat in their ranks along the clay floor with their faces turned toward the dais: Lord Geng, grey and sullen, with the Zhong brothers on either hand; the young Wei, wide-eyed and sulking; a hundred lesser lords, and their stewards, and their wives. The Office had sent a single priest, in ash-white sleeves, to sit alone on the left. At the foot of the dais, on a stool, a very old man with a square white beard had folded his hands in his lap and begun, with perfect concentration, to count his breaths.

Beside the throne, the queen's chair was empty.

It was a small, high-backed, lacquered thing, with arms carved like ducks. Four months it had held a creature that was not what it seemed. It stood there now in the pale light, empty and enormous, and every head in the hall had turned to look at it.

She's not here, he heard them think. The queen's afraid. She's run. She's confessed.

Yes, he thought. Think it. It'll be easier.

He felt the fox shift in his sleeve.

In the front rank, three mats from the dais steps, sat the cousin in her modest grey. She had been smiling when she came in. He had watched the smile die by degrees as her eyes went from the empty chair to the king's face, and from the king's face, slowly, to the left sleeve of his robe. Something went across her features then, quick and bright and dreadful, like the glint of a knife in a drawer. She did not look afraid. She looked, for one moment, like a gambler who has just seen a card turned that she had not known was in the pack.

Then she lowered her eyes, and folded her hands, and was smiling again.

She knows, he thought. She can count the tails, even through wool. And he laid his palm flat over the sleeve, as though to shield a flame.


* * *

The Grand Preceptor came in at the midday gong, when the sun stood highest.

He wore his grey campaign coat, and a plain bronze helm, and the black band across his brow. He carried no weapon. He walked up the long aisle between the mats at his usual slow even pace, with his hands loose at his sides, and the whole hall fell silent as he passed, as a field goes quiet at the approach of a hawk. At the foot of the dais he stopped. His eyes went to the empty chair, and rested there a moment, and rose.

"Great King," said Wen Zhong. "I had thought to find the queen."

"The queen is not present," said Di Xin.

A murmur ran through the hall.

"I should explain. It's a long story. I shall try to be brief." He stepped down from the dais. It took an effort not to put a hand to his sleeve. "Yesterday, in this hall, the Marquis of Zhongzhou told the court that the woman on my right was not his daughter. I have thought about it for a day and a night. I find that I cannot dispute it. I've therefore asked a person to come before you, to give her own account, in her own words, and I ask you to hear her out before you judge any of us."

"Who?"

"A maid." Di Xin turned. "Bring in Ling."

The doors at the far end of the hall swung open.

She came in alone.

It was a very long walk, and she did it very slowly. She wore a plain grey gown, the one the Marquis had found her, with her hair bound up in a simple cap of undyed linen, and she carried nothing in her hands. She walked down the middle of the aisle through the double rank of nobles, with her eyes on the dais, and the whispers rose and spread around her like water around a stone. The maid. The kidnapper. The queen's woman. There was a hiss from somewhere in the Zhong ranks. A small cold laugh.

She did not look to either side. She looked at Di Xin.

Be calm, he thought, with a rush of pure tenderness, a feeling he had no use for and could not suppress. Be calm, Fuji. You've the straightest back in the kingdom.

She stopped at the foot of the dais. She bowed to the king, a plain, correct, workmanlike bow, a servant's. Then, very slowly, she turned to face the Grand Preceptor, and the court, and the empty chair.

"My name," she said, "is Su Fuji."

The hall did not breathe.

She said it quietly. It was not a theatrical voice. It was dry, and level, and rather hoarse, the voice of a person who has spent six days in a cell and has not been given a great deal of practice at speaking in public. It did not carry far. It did not need to. It dropped into the silence of the hall like a pebble into a well, and every head in the room leaned toward it.

"I am the daughter of Su Hu, Marquis of Zhongzhou. I left my father's house in the autumn month, in a black carriage, with eleven carts and a lacquer box. I was sent to this court as a hostage for the peace." She did not raise her voice. She did not look away from the old man in the grey coat. "I reached the post-station at Enzhou on the evening of the third day. I was housed in the East Wing, which was said to be haunted. It was set on fire in the night. I'd have died there, Preceptor, if it had not been for a creature."

"What creature?" said Wen Zhong.

"A fox."

Somebody gasped. It was a thin high sound, quickly stifled. In the front rank, a pale woman in grey, with a face like a fresh peach, had put a hand to her throat.

"A white one," said Fuji. "A fox with a great many tails, who had followed me from the hills above my father's lands. I gave my bread to a hungry child once, on the road, and I think she saw it." She drew a small breath. "She did not come to harm me. She came to see that I was not harmed. When the fire was put out, and there was a pheasant on the roof that I'm told was no pheasant, she asked me if I would let her take my face."

"Take it," said Wen Zhong.

"Wear it. To walk into the palace in my stead, while I hid. So that the one who wished me dead might believe she had succeeded." She lifted her chin. "It was my own idea, Preceptor. I'd like that understood. I was afraid. I did not know whom to trust. I put on a maid's coat and kept my head down. The fox put on my face, and she went to the king, and she did what I would have done, if I had had the courage." She paused. "She did better. I think I should be proud of her."

There was a dreadful silence.

It was Lord Geng who broke it, shoving himself to his feet, scarlet to the roots of his hair. "It's a lie!" he bellowed. "It's a demon's tale, it's—a sorceress's trick! She's no more Su Fuji than I am! She's a tool of the king's, a creature, they've put her up to it, I demand—"

"Lord Geng," said Wen Zhong.

He did not raise his voice. He did not even turn his head. The great lord stopped. His mouth opened and closed.

"Sit down," said the Grand Preceptor, mildly.

Lord Geng sat.

"Great Preceptor." The old voice came from the foot of the dais, thin and dry and perfectly steady. Bi Gan had risen from his stool. He stood with his hands folded in his sleeves, small and neat and very pale, the only man in the hall who had not looked at the maid. "If the court will permit. I should like to add a word."

"Prince Bi Gan."

"For four months a small white fox has been seen in the king's private garden, by the queen's wing. I have seen it myself, once. I took it for a stray. I now believe it to have been the creature of whom the lady speaks." His voice did not shake. "I do not say I understand it. I say that I have kept this house's accounts for twenty years, and that I have never known him to be taken in by a demon. If a fox has been in his house, it was a fox that did him no harm."

It was a very careful sentence. Di Xin, who had known him since he was nine years old, heard in it every one of the things it did not say, and felt his throat close.

Not yet, he thought. Not yet. Thank you, Uncle.


* * *

Di Xin did not remember crossing the floor.

He found himself standing, a pace from the Marquis of Zhongzhou, who had risen from the foot of the dais and was staring at the girl in grey with a look of such naked, shaking, unconcealed pride that it was painful to see. He found that his hand, without any instruction from him, had come up and closed over the sleeve at his left side. It was warm under his palm. It was trembling.

"Su Hu," said Wen Zhong. "Marquis. This is a very different tale from the one you told me yesterday."

"It is, Preceptor." The old Marquis's voice was thick. "I did not tell it. I was afraid to. I told the court that the woman on the dais was not my daughter, and I told it truly, and I knelt to her anyway, because I did not know what else I could do." He swallowed. "I did not know my daughter was alive."

"And now?"

"And now I know."

"Do you swear it?"

"By the bones of my fathers." He turned. He took a step forward. And Su Hu, a man who had spent forty years studying how not to be looked at, put both hands on the shoulders of the girl in the grey gown and looked at her, in front of the entire court of Shang, with a face as naked as a child's. "This is Su Fuji. This is my daughter."

"I will examine her," said Wen Zhong.

It was said very quietly. It dropped into the hall like a stone into still water, and every ripple of it went out and out. Fuji did not move. But Di Xin, who had learned to read her in the last four months, saw the pulse jump in her throat.

"Preceptor," he said. "She has been in prison. She is not well. I ask for a day's grace."

"I am sorry, Great King." Wen Zhong's grey eyes were sorrowful and absolutely fixed. "I have given too many days. I have waited a week. I will look at this woman now, in front of the court, and I will say what I see. If I'm wrong, I'll beg her pardon. If I'm right..." He did not finish.

"If you're right?"

"I should much rather not say."

The old man lifted his hands.

They moved slowly, as though they were very heavy, to the black bronze band that circled his brow. His fingers found the clasp at the back. He closed his two good eyes, and for a moment his lips moved, as if in prayer or apology. Di Xin heard, absurdly, in the dead silence, the sound of a very small bronze catch coming undone.

The band slid down.

In his sleeve the fox gave one short, quick, dreadful shiver.

"Look at me, girl," said Wen Zhong.

Fuji looked.

The whole hall saw it. It began as a crease in the skin between the brows, a thin vertical line, hairline-fine, as though the Preceptor had been struck there by a very small blade. It deepened. It trembled. It began, very slowly, to part, and a light came out of it, cold, white, and silver-gold, like a bronze mirror held to a flame, and the air in the hall seemed to bend toward it, as a field of grass leans before a wind.

And the Heavenly Eye opened.
