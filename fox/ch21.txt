Chapter Twenty-One: What the Terrace Is For

By the time the sun came up, Di Xin had discovered that being in love was an extremely inefficient use of a morning.

He had a great deal to do. A lord of the first rank stood accused, before two thousand witnesses, of ordering the deaths of seven people. The Master of Sacrifices had shut himself in the Hill of Nüwa with the doors barred. The Zhong brothers had sent word that they were concerned. Somewhere in the palace the steward of the Geng east holding was either fleeing the realm or being hidden by a man with a very large estate, and the first of these was the better outcome. There were four petitions on his table about a goose-farmer in the Wei district who was claiming the king had cursed his flock.

Instead he was sitting on a cold stone bench on the roof of the Deer Terrace, with a woman asleep against his shoulder, and he did not want to move.

She had fallen asleep around the fourth watch. It had not been intended. They had talked, haltingly, in short bursts, like two people crossing a river on stones, and then there had been a long silence, and then he had felt her head tip against his arm. He had sat very still. He had pulled his cloak over both of them. He'd watched the stars wheel, and the red one rise, and the whole slow bowl of the sky go from black to indigo to grey, and in all that time he had not thought of a single tally.

It was possibly the most useful night of his life.

She stirred. She was wearing her own face, white-haired and sharp-boned, with a smear of dried blood at the corner of her mouth, and she had burrowed into the wool until only her eyes showed. They were very large. They were looking at him.

"You're still here," she said.

"I'd be surprised if I weren't. I haven't the least idea how to leave."

"You could just stand up."

"I could. But I'd have to put you down." He considered it. "I'm told that's very rude."

She laughed, a small blurry sound against his shoulder, and did not move. The wind had dropped. Far below them, the city of Chaoge was beginning to wake: smoke rising from a thousand hearths, the thin cry of a vendor, the creak of an early cart. It lay under its blanket of old snow like a great sleeping animal, and he found that he was looking at it, for once, without counting anything.

"It isn't what they say," said Yue.

"What isn't?"

"The Terrace. Fuji says the cook swears it's a pleasure tower. That you've got a hundred concubines in the cellars and a pit of snakes." She wriggled free of the cloak, sat up, and looked around the stone platform with her head on one side, like a bird, at the round table, the bronze bowl, the bare bench. "It's very plain."

"It's very plain on purpose."

"What's it for?"

He thought about it. It was, he realized, a question nobody had ever asked him. The whole of Chaoge knew the Deer Terrace as a place of scandal. The Office called it an abomination. The nobles called it a folly. His uncle called it, in a tone of long-suffering patience, your hobby. None of them had ever thought it might be for something.

"Come down," he said. "I'll show you."


* * *

He took her down the inside stair, through a low door in the parapet, into the hollow of the mound.

It was dry down there and cool and very quiet. A long vaulted corridor ran away into darkness, lit by small clay lamps set into the walls, and on either side, from floor to ceiling, ran shelves. They were old shelves, of dark oak, dovetailed without nails, and they held neat, endless, undramatic rows of bamboo slips. Some were bound in red and some in black and some in plain hemp. Each had a small bronze tag, with a date, hanging from its cord. The air smelled of lamp oil and dry wood and, very faintly, of smoke.

"This is the Archive," said Di Xin. "My father's, and his father's, and all of theirs. The records of the realm, in rough order. Tithes. Levies. Harvests. Floods. Births, deaths, marriages, as far back as the clerks bothered to write them down." He ran a finger lightly along the edge of a shelf. "When I came to the throne, it was in a state you wouldn't credit. Half of it had rotted. The rest was being used as kindling. I had it all moved here and bound and shelved. It took six years."

"Why?"

"Because someone had to know." He did not quite know how to say it. "If a thing has been done, someone ought to know. It's very odd. I can't explain it. Most of the world seems perfectly content for things to happen and then simply... stop having happened. A famine in a village. A flood. A man who dies in a pit. It's all so quickly forgotten." He stopped at the end of a row, where a small door of wood and bronze stood slightly ajar. "I'm not a particularly good king. I haven't the voice, or the manner, or any gift at all for ceremony. But I can count. And I think that the least I can do for the dead is to have counted them."

She was looking at him. It was not a look he knew how to read.

"This is where the names are," she said slowly. "The Spared."

"Yes. And others." He pushed open the door.

The inner room was small, and lit by a single lamp. It held one long table and one chair. On the table lay a stack of red-bound slips, fifteen or twenty feet of them, laid end to end. On the shelf above, in a single orderly row, stood a hundred or so clay jars, sealed with wax.

"The jars are for the ones who had no names," said Di Xin. "When a pit is dug up. When a body is found. I keep a handful of earth from each. It's superstition, I suppose. But I thought somebody ought to be given a jar." He cleared his throat. "There are four thousand names in this room that I know of. A little over a thousand of them I was in time for. I'm still looking for the rest."

She said nothing. She walked slowly down the length of the table, and touched the first slip with one finger, as a person touches a very old sleeping animal. Then she touched the next.

"You never told anybody."

"There wasn't anyone to tell."

"We read them. Fuji and I. On the wedding night, while you slept. You'd dropped the bundle."

"Ah." He gave a short, humorless laugh. "I woke with the slips tied in the wrong order and decided I'd done it in my sleep. I see I owe my past self an apology."

"We were very quiet."

"You were very kind. I woke with the wine cup safely on the table." There was a smile in his voice, but when he looked at her, she was not smiling. She was standing by the table with her hand flat on the red slips, and her eyes were shining, and the tears were running down her face without a sound.

"Yue—"

"I thought," she said. "I thought you were the monster. I thought you'd never counted. I thought that was the whole point of monsters." She wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist, roughly. "And you've been down here the whole time, with a jar of dirt for every one of them."

He did not know what to say. He did not, he found, need to say anything. He crossed the room and put his arms round her, and she turned her face into his collar, and for a time the Archive was silent except for the small dry crackle of the lamp.


* * *

They were interrupted at the sixth hour by a cough.

It was a very discreet cough. It was the cough of a man who has been waiting at the top of a flight of stairs for some time, and who does not intend to be accused of eavesdropping. By the time Di Xin had disentangled himself and opened the door, Yue's queen's face was back, flawless and pale, and a red mark on her mouth had been blotted out with the cuff of her sleeve.

Bi Gan stood in the corridor with his hands in his sleeves.

"Nephew," he said.

"Uncle."

"I am told the queen did not return to her wing last night."

"The queen was assisting me with an inventory."

"All night."

"It was a very large inventory."

The old man's eyes went to Yue, who bowed, with the calm of a woman who had never in her life been anywhere but exactly where she ought to be. Something moved in his face, a long, slow, complicated movement, as of a man doing a difficult piece of arithmetic and arriving at a figure he had expected but hoped to be wrong about.

"I won't ask," said Bi Gan. "I came to say that the court is in a state of some agitation. Lord Geng has withdrawn to his estate. He has said that he will answer the king's charges in the king's court, at the king's pleasure, and not before. I should tell you that this is a threat." He paused. "The Zhong brothers have sent four hundred armed retainers to escort him home. The Office has declared a period of interdict on the temple. And the Master of Sacrifices has asked me to tell you, in private, that he is not angry."

"He isn't?"

"He says that he is afraid." Bi Gan's mouth tightened. "I have known Yuan Shi for thirty years. I have never heard him use that word."

Di Xin was silent. He was thinking of a tall grave man at the foot of an altar, a bronze knife in his hands, whispering I did not know.

"I'll go to him," he said.

"I'd advise against it."

"I know."

"He is a good man, nephew. That is the trouble." The old man's voice, for a moment, was not dry at all. "A good man who has just learned his whole life has been used is a very dangerous thing. He will have to decide what to do about it. I'd very much like it not to be decided in front of the goddess."

He bowed, not to the king but to the queen, and went away down the corridor with his small neat steps. His shadow went before him on the wall, long and thin and a little stooped.

Not yet, thought Di Xin. He did not know why the words came into his head.


* * *

She slept again that afternoon.

He had meant to take her back to her wing, but she had gone grey on the way up the stairs, and he had caught her by the elbow and carried the rest of the distance, over her objections, to the cot in the Counting Room. She fell asleep before he had finished tucking the cloak around her. He sat in the long carved chair for an hour, pretending to read a tally, and listening to her breathe.

She was not at peace. He had learned to tell. Her breath came faster than it should, and every so often her fingers closed on the cloak, and the seven pale tails along her side stirred and twitched, like the tails of a dreaming animal. Her lips moved. Once, quite clearly, she said, "No." Once she said, "I know, Mother."

He put down the tally.

"Yue."

Nothing.

"Yue. Wake up."

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I'm sorry. I didn't—"

He crossed the room in two strides and took her shoulders. She jerked awake all at once with a gasp, eyes wide, pupils huge, and for a moment she looked at him without the least idea who he was. Then she did. She clutched his sleeve in both hands, and he could feel her trembling right through the wool.

"She came," said Yue. Her voice cracked. "In the dream. She was on the hill, in the hall, and the lamp was in her hand, and it had seven wicks. She counted them. She counted them out loud." She swallowed. "She didn't shout. She didn't even look angry. She just said it, very quietly, in a voice like water under ice."

"What did she say?"

"She said, You are late."
