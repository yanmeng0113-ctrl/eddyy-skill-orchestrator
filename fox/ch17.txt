Chapter Seventeen: What the King Already Knew

Di Xin had sat up with a great many things in his life: sick horses, failing harvests, a dying father who had wanted a tally of the royal treasury read aloud in the third watch of the night. He had never sat up with a fox.

She lay on the narrow cot in the corner of the Counting Room, where the night clerks slept when the tithes were due, wrapped in his cloak. She was no longer a fox. Somewhere in the long hours since the fire she had slid back, without waking, into a young woman's shape, thin and white-haired and curled on her side like a child, with one hand tucked under her cheek. It was not the face he had married. The stubborn plain mouth was gone. This was a narrower, sharper, odder face, all cheekbone and shadow, with a bruise of exhaustion under each closed eye.

He had known it would be different. He had not known it would be so young.

He did not touch her. He sat in the long carved chair by the table, with the lamp turned low and a pile of unread tallies in his lap, and counted her breaths. There were nineteen to a hundred heartbeats. He had counted it three times. He told himself it was good practice.

Behind her, on the cloak, lay seven pale tails.

They were not exactly there. They were there in the way a thing is there that you see at the edge of a lamp's light, a shimmer, a soft double edge to the air, and when he looked straight at them they thinned and wavered, like breath on a cold mirror. But he had learned how to look. He had been looking, now, for a month. And he knew precisely how many.

Eight, he thought, the night I was wed. Seven tonight.

He had written it down, in the margin of a tally for the Wei granary, in a hand so shaky he'd had to copy it out again. It was the most ridiculous entry he had ever made in a ledger. Queen: eight tails. After the fire: seven. He had looked at it a long while, and then, with great care, he had burned the slip in the brazier, and stirred the ash.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the gong sounded the fifth watch.

On the cot, the fox opened her eyes.


* * *

She did not move. She did not make a sound. She lay absolutely still for the length of three heartbeats, looking up at the beams of his study with amber eyes that took in the room one detail at a time, shelves, table, lamp, the long dark shape of the man by the table. Then she tried to sit up, and her arms would not hold her, and she fell back with a small, furious gasp.

"Don't," said Di Xin.

"Where..."

"The Counting Room. It's the safest place I know. I told the guard you were resting in my chambers and not to be disturbed. Nobody ever comes in here." He put down the tallies. "Your maid is in the next room with a basin and a clean under-robe. She's been there all night. I think she wanted to come in, and I think she couldn't decide whether to hit me first."

The amber eyes widened.

"You know," said the girl. Her voice was hoarse. It was a different voice from the one she'd used in the great hall, lower, rougher, with a faint accent he couldn't place. "You know. You've seen..."

"Yes."

"How long?"

He had planned this. He'd planned it all night, in the lamp-lit silence, rehearsing it in his head as he had once rehearsed speeches to the Office. It was a good, short, neutral speech. It began with the phrase I want to assure you. It did not survive the first look at her face.

"Since the first night," he said.

She stared at him.

"In the bridal chamber. After I lifted the veil. I turned to set it on the table, and the lamp threw your shadow on the wall, and there were eight tails in it." He heard his own voice, flat and careful, as though he were reading a figure aloud. "I counted them twice. I didn't believe the first count. And then I picked up the wine jar, and poured you a cup, and asked you about the Wei granary, because I couldn't think of a single other thing to do."

"You moved the lamp."

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"I thought it would be easier for you." He rubbed a hand across his face. It came away damp. "I had no idea what you were. A demon. A ghost. Something that had killed the real Su Fuji and was wearing her to my bed. But you looked so..." He faltered. "You looked as if you were bracing for a blow, and had no idea who was going to land it. I couldn't bear to add to it."

For a long moment the girl on the cot said nothing at all. Her hands, in the folds of his cloak, had curled into fists. When she spoke it was barely above a whisper.

"A month."

"Yes."

"You knew for a month. And you let me..." She stopped. "The feast. The pond. You let me ask for the thing you needed, and you said bigger. You knew, the whole time. You knew what I was and you used me."

"I did," said Di Xin.

It cost him something to say it. It was always going to. But he had decided, around the third watch, that if there was one thing he owed this creature on the cot it was an answer that he had not shaped to please her.

"I'm not going to apologize for the feast. It was necessary, and it worked, and I would do it again. But I should have told you first, and I didn't, and I'm sorry for that." He met her eyes. "I didn't know who sent you. I thought it might be the Zhou. Or the Office. Or, forgive me, something older. I thought the safest thing was to let you play your part and see what the play was."

"And what was the play?"

"I'd hoped you could tell me."

She laughed. It was a ragged, startled sound. It broke off, and she turned her face against the cloak and was silent for a while. When she looked up again her eyes were wet, and furious, and very bright.

"A goddess," she said. "That's the play. Nüwa. She sent me for you. You've been bowing to the wrong person, Great King. She's been angry since the day you closed the pits, and she's very old, and she's very hungry. She told me to wear the queen and take your court apart from the inside, and when it was done she'd snuff the lamp and let me go."

"The lamp."

"My tails. They're her wicks. Nine of them. Every time I do something a mortal can see, one burns." She lifted a hand and let it fall. "That was the second. Tonight."

"For the rain."

"For the rain."

He was silent. He was thinking of the sky over the south wall, which had been clear when he left the Deer Terrace, and had gone black, within a few breaths, over a single burning roof. He was thinking of a little white fox on four small paws at the base of a dead tree, throwing back her head and calling.

"You did that for a barn full of strangers," he said slowly.

"I did it for Fuji."

"Which—" He checked. "Which is the maid's name?"

It was out before he'd thought. He saw her go rigid. He saw her face shut, close and swift and cold as a trap, and understood that he had gone one step too far.

"I mean," he said, "that the real Lady Su has been missing from every account I've been given." He kept his voice light. "I had begun to wonder if she was anywhere at all."

"She's safe," said Yue, very quietly.

"Safe."

"That's all I'll say." The amber eyes were hard. "She isn't in the palace. She isn't in danger from you. And if you ask me again, I'll go through that window and you'll never find me."

He looked at her for some time.

It was, he thought, the first completely honest thing she had said to him. It was a refusal. He recognized it as such. It had the shape of loyalty, the plain, stubborn, defensive shape of a person who has decided where she stands and means to stand there. He was suddenly, absurdly, very glad of it.

"Then I won't ask," he said.

She blinked.

"I'll not ask. I'd rather you told me yourself, one day, if you choose. In the meantime I shall assume that the real Lady Su is exactly where she wishes to be." He found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "I have a great deal of respect for people who know where to put a secret."

"You're mad."

"It's been suggested."

"You've got a monster in your study, and you're offering her breakfast." She struggled up on one elbow. Her hair fell over her face in a long white sheet and she pushed it back with a hand that trembled. "I came here to destroy you. Do you understand that? I'm not a friend. I'm not a guest. I was sent to take your court from the inside. I was in the rafters the day you freed those thirty men. I was watching you. Eater of children, I thought. That's what I thought."

"And now?"

She opened her mouth. Shut it. The color came up in her thin cheeks, a slow red tide, and for the first time since she had woken she looked away.

"I don't know," she said.

"That," said Di Xin, "is a very reasonable answer."

"One more thing," she said, after a while, to the cloak. "The poem. On the wall of Nüwa's temple."

"I didn't write it."

"I know you didn't. I watched it being written." She looked up. "A woman called Hu Ximei. She's a pheasant. A very old one. She stood behind a pillar and watched you bow to the goddess, and then she took your brush off the ledge, while the ink was still wet, and wrote eight lines in your hand. It took her no longer than it takes to drink a cup of wine. She said it was a gift."

Di Xin sat quite still.

"She's the cousin," he said. "The one who moved in next to you. The one who hums."

"The goose song. Yes."

"She wrote it enjoying herself." He said it slowly, the way a man sets down the last figure in a sum. "I told the Office that. I told them the writer wasn't in a temper. I said he was enjoying himself. I thought I was being clever." He shut his eyes. "I'd just not imagined that it was true."

"She's in the next room. She listens through the wall."

"Then we must be very careful what is said in this one." He opened his eyes. There was a light in them that she had not seen before, thin and bright and hard as a blade. "Thank you. That's worth more than anything you could have given me. I've had a name for the hand and nothing to put it to."

"You aren't angry?"

"At you?" He seemed genuinely surprised. "You were told to ruin me, and you handed me the first honest piece of evidence I've had in three years. I should be asking what I owe you."

Dawn was coming. It showed in the high window as a grey line, and then a paler one, and then a thin cold gold that spilled down across the shelves of slips and the long scarred table and the girl with the white hair, huddled in his cloak, looking at her own hands. He found that he could not stop looking at her. He was ashamed of it, and he did it anyway.

"I've a question," she said at last.

"Ask."

"It's... a stupid one." She hugged the cloak tighter. "You knew what I was. For a month you had a thing in your house with eight tails and a goddess's order, and every reason in the world to be afraid. You could have called the Office. You could have called your uncle. You could have had me hanged in the courtyard." Her eyes came up, fierce and uncertain and golden in the morning light. "Why didn't you kill me?"

He had known she would ask. He had spent the whole night composing the answer, and now it was gone. There was only a woman on a cot in the grey of morning, with seven tails, asking him something no one had asked him in his life.

"Why haven't you killed me?" said Di Xin.
